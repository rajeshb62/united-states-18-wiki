The Life of George Washington — Volume I

Author: Washington Irving
Publisher: Desmond Publishing Co., Boston, 1899
Source: Internet Archive (University of Florida library copy)
OCLC: 7523322

Overview

Volume I of Washington Irving’s five-volume biography covers George Washington’s life from his family’s English origins through the end of 1775, when he was commanding the Continental Army in the Siege of Boston and directing operations in Canada. The volume traces Washington’s transformation from a Virginia planter’s son into a frontier soldier and ultimately the commander-in-chief of the American revolutionary forces.

Full Chapter-by-Chapter Breakdown

Chapter 1 — Genealogy of the Washington Family

The volume opens with the deep ancestral roots of the Washington family, tracing their lineage to Norman times following the Conquest. Irving describes the family’s origins in the county of Durham, where they held estates under the princely prelates of the palatinate as knights bound to follow the banner of St. Cuthbert in war and hunt. We meet William de Hertburn, the first recorded progenitor, who exchanged his village of Hertburn for the manor of Wessyngton in 1183, thereby giving the family its name. The narrative follows the De Wessyngtons through the wars of the barons, the Scottish invasions, and the tournament lists of Edward III. Sir Stephen de Wessyngton tilts at Dunstable in 1334; a later William is the last male of the line to hold the manor. The name passes through the cloisters with Prior John de Wessyngton, a formidable champion of monastic privilege, then through the English Reformation to Laurence Washington of Gray’s Inn, who received the manor of Sulgrave from Henry VIII. The Sulgrave line distinguishes itself in the Civil Wars: Sir Henry Washington, the gallant defender of Worcester, returns a defiant reply to Fairfax and holds out for three months before capitulating on honorable terms. Finally, the narrative reaches John and Andrew Washington, great-grandsons of the Sulgrave grantee, who emigrate to Virginia in 1657, settling in Westmoreland County on the northern neck between the Potomac and Rappahannock rivers. John Washington marries Anne Pope, becomes a magistrate, member of the House of Burgesses, and colonel leading forces against Seneca raiders — establishing the Virginia branch from which George Washington will descend.

Chapter 2 — The Home of Washington’s Boyhood — His Early Education — Lawrence Washington and his Campaign in the West Indies — Death of Washington’s Father — The Widowed Mother and her Children — School Exercises

Shortly after George’s birth, his father Augustine removes the family to an estate in Stafford County opposite Fredericksburg, on a rise overlooking a meadow bordering the Rappahannock. This is the home of George’s boyhood — long since vanished, its site traced only by fragments of brick and china. Irving describes the “old field school-house” kept by a tenant named Hobby (also the parish sexton), where George receives the rudiments of reading, writing, and ciphering. More importantly, he receives moral and mental cultivation at home from his father, who impresses upon his ductile mind high maxims of religion, virtue, justice, generosity, and above all a scrupulous love of truth through practical, domestic instruction. When George is seven or eight, his beloved elder half-brother Lawrence returns from England, well-educated and accomplished, fourteen years George’s senior. A deep attachment forms between them: Lawrence looks down with protecting eye upon the boy, while George looks up to his manly, cultivated brother as a model. Lawrence Washington had served as a captain in the British expeditions against the Spanish West Indies, participating in Admiral Vernon’s disastrous attack on Carthagena in 1741 — a campaign of fever, tempest, and Spanish resistance that decimated the British forces. He returned with his health impaired but his military reputation enhanced. When George is about eleven, his father Augustine dies suddenly, leaving the widowed mother, Mary Ball Washington, to raise five children. She proves a woman of strong sense and energetic character, managing the estate and instilling in her children habits of order, industry, and self-reliance. George is described as a grave, thoughtful boy, already showing signs of the methodical precision and moral seriousness that will mark his adult life.

Chapter 3 — Paternal Conduct of an Elder Brother — The Fairfax Family — Washington’s Code of Morals and Manners — Soldiers’ Tales — Their Influence — Washington Prepares for the Navy — A Mother’s Objections — Return to School — Studies and Exercises — A School-Boy Passion — The Lowland Beauty — Love Ditties at Mount Vernon — Visit to Belvoir — Lord Fairfax, His Character — Fox-Hunting a Remedy for Love — Proposition for a Surveying Expedition

On their father’s death, Lawrence Washington takes a truly paternal interest in young George, having him as frequently as possible as a guest at Mount Vernon. Lawrence, now adjutant-general of the district and a leading figure in the colony, brings George into familiar intercourse with the family of his father-in-law, the Honorable William Fairfax, who resides at Belvoir, a beautiful seat a few miles below Mount Vernon. William Fairfax is a man of liberal education, varied and adventurous experience — he served in the East and West Indies, governed New Providence after helping rescue it from pirates, and now manages the immense landed estates of his cousin Lord Fairfax. In this refined and cultivated circle, the somewhat homebred school-boy George compiles his famous “Rules of Behavior in Company and Conversation” — a meticulous code of 110 maxims covering everything from personal deportment to table manners. Lawrence’s military comrades — Adjutant Muse, a veteran of Carthagena, and Jacob Van Braam, a Dutch soldier of fortune — fill George’s ears with soldiers’ tales that kindle martial ambitions. So strong is this impulse that Lawrence arranges for George to receive a midshipman’s warrant in the British navy. The trunks are packed, the vessel is ready — but George’s mother objects. Her opposition is decisive, and the naval career is abandoned. George returns to his studies with renewed diligence, mastering geometry and surveying under Mr. Williams. Meanwhile, a youthful romantic attachment develops: a “school-boy passion” for a “lowland beauty” — believed to be a Miss Frances Alexander or a Miss Grimes — leads him to write love ditties. The affaire de cœur proves fleeting. A visit to Belvoir introduces him to Lord Fairfax himself — Thomas, sixth Baron Fairfax of Cameron, a curious and eccentric figure. Educated at Oxford, a contributor to the Spectator, he has retired to his vast Virginia estates, preferring the wilderness to the drawing-rooms of England. He lives as a fox-hunting country gentleman, and when he hears of young Washington’s romantic troubles, he prescribes hunting and the surveying of his western lands as the sovereign remedy. A proposition is made: George shall accompany George William Fairfax on a surveying expedition beyond the Blue Ridge.

Chapter 4 — Expedition beyond the Blue Ridge — The Valley of the Shenandoah — Lord Fairfax — Lodge in the Wilderness — Surveying — Life in the Backwoods — Indians — War-Dance — German Settlers — Return Home — Washington as Public Surveyor — Sojourn at Greenway Court — Horses, Hounds, and Books — Rugged Experience among the Mountains

In March 1748, just after turning sixteen, Washington sets out on horseback with George William Fairfax, crossing the Blue Ridge at Ashley’s Gap into the great Valley of Virginia. Irving paints a vivid picture of the journey: winter still lingering on the mountaintops, melting snows swelling the rivers, but spring softening the lower landscape. They pass through the lovely, temperate Shenandoah Valley, twenty-five miles wide, bounded by the Blue Ridge on one side and the North Mountain (a ridge of the Alleghanies) on the other, with the bright, abounding Shenandoah River — “the daughter of the stars” to the Indians — flowing through it. Their first station is a wilderness lodge, “his lordship’s quarter,” where Lord Fairfax’s steward resides with farming negroes, twelve miles from the site of present-day Winchester. Washington keeps a minutely detailed diary — practical, observant, recording soil qualities, tree species, and land values. The gleam of romance from his lowland beauty is gone; the real business of life has begun. He meets German settlers living in rude cabins, encounters Indians performing war-dances, and experiences the hard life of the backwoods — sleeping on straw, hunting for food, and surveying vast tracts. After returning home, he is appointed a public surveyor, a position of responsibility and profit for a youth of seventeen. He sojourns at Lord Fairfax’s Greenway Court, enjoying the library of books, the horses and hounds, and the company of the eccentric nobleman. His surveying work takes him deep into the mountains, where he gains the rugged experience and knowledge of the wilderness that will serve him so well in his military career.

Chapter 5 — English and French Claims to the Ohio Valley — Wild State of the Country — Projects of Settlements — The Ohio Company — Enlightened Views of Lawrence Washington — French Rivalry — Celeron de Bienville — His Signs of Occupation — Hugh Crawford — George Croghan, a Veteran Trader, and Montour, his Interpreter — Their Mission from Pennsylvania to the Ohio Tribes — Christopher Gist, the Pioneer of the Yadkin — Agent of the Ohio Company — His Expedition to the Frontier — Reprobate Traders at Logstown — Negotiations with the Indians — Scenes in the Ohio Country — Diplomacy at Piqua — Kegs of Brandy and Rolls of Tobacco — Gist’s Return across Kentucky — A Deserted Home — French Schemes — Captain Joncaire, a Diplomat of the Wilderness — His Speech at Logstown — The Indians’ Land “Where?”

During Washington’s surveying campaigns, a grand colonizing scheme sets the stage for imperial conflict. The Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle (1748) had left the boundaries between British and French possessions in America undefined. Both nations now race to occupy the vast, desirable region west of the Alleghany Mountains, from the Great Lakes to the Ohio River and its tributaries — a territory of salubrious climate, fertile soil, and immense commercial potential through its connecting waterways. The French claim by right of discovery through Marquette and Joliet’s 1673 descent of the Mississippi; the English claim through the charter of Virginia granting “sea to sea.” Lawrence Washington and other leading Virginians form the Ohio Company in 1749, securing a royal grant of 500,000 acres west of the Alleghanies, on condition of settling one hundred families within seven years and building a fort. The French respond by sending Captain Celeron de Bienville with a force of two hundred and fifty men to claim the Ohio Valley formally, burying leaden plates at various points as signs of possession. Irving details the diplomatic maneuvering among the Indian tribes — the Six Nations, Delawares, Shawnees, and Mingoes — as both European powers court them with gifts and speeches. George Croghan, a veteran Pennsylvania trader, and his interpreter Montour are sent to secure the allegiance of the Ohio tribes. Christopher Gist, a hardy pioneer from the Yadkin River in North Carolina, becomes the agent of the Ohio Company, making an extraordinary winter expedition into the Ohio country, negotiating with Indians at Logstown (where he finds reprobate traders corrupting the tribes with rum), and at Piqua distributing kegs of brandy and rolls of tobacco. Gist’s return across Kentucky reveals a landscape of astonishing richness. Meanwhile, the French advance their schemes through the wily Captain Joncaire, a diplomat of the wilderness, who makes a powerful speech at Logstown, claiming the land for France. When the Indians ask “Where is your land?,” Joncaire replies with characteristic French diplomacy. The chapter closes with the stage set for confrontation.

Chapter 6 — Preparations for Hostilities — Washington Appointed District Adjutant-General — Mount Vernon a School of Arms — Adjutant Muse a Veteran Campaigner — Jacob Van Braam the Master of Fence — Ill Health of Washington’s Brother Lawrence — Voyage with him to the West Indies — Scenes at Barbadoes — Tropical Fruits — Beefsteak and Tripe Club — Return Home of Washington — Death of Lawrence

The French now prepare for war, launching an armed vessel on Lake Ontario, strengthening Niagara, and advancing outposts on the upper Ohio. The British colonies stir with military preparation. Virginia divides into military districts, each with an adjutant-general (rank of major, £150 per year) responsible for organizing the militia. Lawrence Washington secures this appointment for his nineteen-year-old brother George — a mark of the young man’s maturity and the confidence he inspires. Mount Vernon becomes a school of arms. Adjutant Muse, a Westmoreland volunteer who served with Lawrence at Carthagena, instructs George in tactics and the manual exercise. Jacob Van Braam, a Dutch soldier of fortune, gives lessons in swordplay. Amid these military preparations, Lawrence’s health — undermined by his West Indian campaign — deteriorates. George accompanies him on a voyage to Barbadoes in the autumn of 1751, hoping the tropical climate will restore him. Irving describes the scenes at Barbadoes: tropical fruits, the social life of the island, the “Beefsteak and Tripe Club” to which Lawrence is elected. George contracts smallpox, which leaves his face slightly pitted but renders him immune for life — a significant detail, as smallpox would ravage the Continental Army years later. Finding no improvement in Lawrence’s health, George returns to Virginia in February 1752. Lawrence sails for Bermuda, but the change does him no good. He returns home and dies in July 1752 at Mount Vernon, leaving George as one of his executors and, eventually, the reversion of the Mount Vernon estate. The death of his beloved brother is a profound blow. Washington now steps into Lawrence’s place in the community, taking on responsibilities far beyond his years.

Chapter 7 — Council of the Ohio Tribes at Logstown — Treaty with the English — Gist’s Settlement — Speeches of the Half-King and the French Commandant — French Aggressions — The Ruins of Piqua — Washington Sent on a Mission to the French Commander — Jacob Van Braam, his Interpreter — Christopher Gist, his Guide — Halt at the Confluence of the Monongahela and Allegany — Projected Fort — Shingiss, a Delaware Sachem — Logstown — The Half-King — Indian Councils — Indian Diplomacy — Rumors concerning Joncaire — Indian Escorts — The Half-King, Jeskakake, and White Thunder

A treaty council of the Ohio tribes (Delawares, Shawnees, and Mingoes) meets at Logstown. The chiefs of the Six Nations disdain to attend, insisting that if the Governor of Virginia wishes to treat with them, he must come to Albany. Colonel Fry and two other Virginia commissioners secure a treaty with the western tribes: they will not molest English settlers south of the Ohio. Tanacharisson, the “Half-King” — an influential Seneca chief appointed by the Six Nations to manage their western affairs — urges the Virginians to build a strong house at the fork of the Monongahela. Christopher Gist begins laying out a town and building a fort at Chartier’s Creek, establishing a settlement beyond Laurel Hill where eleven families join him. The Ohio Company establishes a trading post at Wills’ Creek. The Half-King personally remonstrates with the French at their post on Lake Erie: “Fathers, you are the disturbers of this land by building towns and taking the country from us by fraud and force.” The French ignore him. French aggressions continue; the ruined village of Piqua bears witness to their depredations. Governor Dinwiddie of Virginia, alarmed by intelligence of French movements, decides to send an emissary to the French commander on the Ohio to demand his withdrawal. He chooses the young Adjutant-General Washington — now barely twenty-one — for this dangerous winter mission. Washington selects Jacob Van Braam as his interpreter (Van Braam’s French being, as it will prove, imperfect) and engages Christopher Gist as his guide. They halt at the confluence of the Monongahela and Allegany rivers, where Washington notes the strategic importance of the site for a fort. They visit the Delaware sachem Shingiss, confer with the Half-King and his fellow sachems Jeskakake and White Thunder, and receive Indian escorts and ceremonial diplomacy as they push deeper into the wilderness toward the French posts.

Chapter 8 — Arrival at Venango — Captain Joncaire — Frontier Revelry — Discussions over the Bottle — The Old Diplomatist and the Young — The Half-King, Jeskakake, and White Thunder Staggered — The Speech-Belt — Departure — La Force, the Wily Commissary — Fort at French Creek — The Chevalier Legardeur de St. Pierre, Knight of St. Louis — Captain Reparti — Transactions at the Fort — Attempts to Seduce the Sachems — Mischief Brewing on the Frontier — Difficulties and Delays in Parting — Descent of French Creek — Arrival at Venango

On December 4, 1753, after a difficult seventy-mile journey through inclement weather, Washington and his party arrive at Venango, where the French flag flies over the house from which the English trader John Frazier had been driven. There they encounter the redoubtable Captain Joncaire, the veteran intriguer of the frontier, who claims to “have the command of the Ohio.” Joncaire is a jovial host: that evening he invites Washington and his party to a frontier revelry at headquarters. Irving describes the scene with novelistic flair — the old diplomatist Joncaire and the young envoy Washington discussing empire over the bottle, the wine loosening tongues, Joncaire and his brother officers declaring that “by G— they would do it” and take possession of the Ohio, boasting that English motions were too slow and dilatory to prevent any French undertaking. The Half-King, Jeskakake, and White Thunder are plied with drink until “staggered,” and Joncaire attempts to seduce the sachems from their English allegiance with the speech-belt. After several days of delay, Washington extracts himself from Venango and proceeds to the fort on French Creek. There he meets the Chevalier Legardeur de St. Pierre, Knight of St. Louis, a courteous but firm commander who receives Dinwiddie’s letter with diplomatic gravity. Washington also meets Captain Reparti. The French attempt to separate the Indian escorts from Washington. Mischief is clearly brewing on the frontier. After further delays and difficulties, Washington departs, descending French Creek by canoe back toward Venango, his mission largely unsuccessful but his observations invaluable.

Chapter 9 — Return from Venango — A Tramp on Foot — Murdering Town — The Indian Guide — Treachery — An Anxious Night — Perils on the Allegany River — Queen Aliquippa — The Old Watch-Coat — Return across the Blue Ridge

On Christmas Day 1753, Washington and his party set out from Venango on foot for the long winter journey home through a treacherous wilderness. The pack-horses are jaded; Washington dismounts, gives up his saddle-horse for baggage, and proceeds on foot in Indian hunting-dress with Van Braam, Gist, and the interpreter John Davidson. Determined to hasten ahead and make his report, Washington leaves the main party under Van Braam’s command, buckling himself into a watch-coat, strapping his pack on his shoulders, and striking ahead through the woods with only Gist as companion. At a place with the sinister name of Murdering Town, they encounter a party of Indians who seem to have been waiting for them. One Indian, whom the wary Gist recognizes from Joncaire’s camp, offers to guide them to the Allegany River by a shorter route — a shortcut that proves to be a betrayal. When the Indian guide suddenly stops and fires his gun at Washington from close range, missing, Gist moves to kill him, but Washington restrains him. They elude the treacherous guide by kindling a fire to deceive him and then slipping away into the darkness, walking all night through the frozen forest. Reaching the Allegany River, they find it choked with ice. With only one hatchet, they build a raft and attempt to cross — but Washington is thrown into the freezing water and only saves himself by grasping a raft log. They spend an anxious night on an island, nearly freezing to death. They eventually cross on the ice and visit Queen Aliquippa, a friendly Indian queen whom Washington mollifies with the gift of his old watch-coat and a bottle of rum after learning she had taken offense at being neglected. Washington finally recrosses the Blue Ridge and reaches Williamsburg, his report and journal providing the first detailed intelligence of French intentions in the Ohio Valley.

Chapter 10 — Reply of the Chevalier de St. Pierre — Trent’s Mission to the Frontier — Washington Recruits Troops — Dinwiddie and the House of Burgesses — Independent Conduct of the Virginians — Expedients to Gain Recruits — Jacob Van Braam in Service — Toilful March to Wills’ Creek — Contrecœur at the Fork of the Ohio — Trent’s Refractory Troops

The Chevalier de St. Pierre’s reply to Governor Dinwiddie is courteous but firm: he will transmit the letter to his general, the Marquis du Quesne; as to the summons to retire, he does not think himself obliged to obey. Washington’s journal, printed and widely circulated throughout the colonies and England, awakens the nation to the French threat. Governor Dinwiddie, perceiving the urgency, dispatches Captain Trent to raise a company and complete the fort at the Fork of the Ohio. Washington himself is commissioned to raise troops in the frontier counties. But the independent spirit of the Virginians proves a challenge: they are reluctant to enlist, and Dinwiddie faces resistance from the House of Burgesses. Various expedients are employed to gain recruits. Jacob Van Braam is brought into the service. Washington begins a toilful march from Alexandria toward Wills’ Creek, gathering men as he advances. Meanwhile, Contrecœur, the French commander, arrives at the Fork of the Ohio with a force of five hundred men in sixty bateaux and three hundred canoes. Captain Trent is away, and his lieutenant, Ensign Ward, is left with only forty-one men in the unfinished fort. When the French land and demand surrender, Ward has no choice but to capitulate. The French demolish the English works and begin building Fort Duquesne on the site — the strategic position Washington had noted and recommended for fortification. The loss of the Ohio Fork is a devastating blow to British hopes.

Chapter 11 — March to the Little Meadows — Rumors from the Ohio — Correspondence from the Banks of the Youghiogeny — Attempt to Descend that River — Alarming Reports — Scouting Parties — Perilous Situation of the Camp — Gist and La Force — Message from the Half-King — French Tracks — The Jumonville Skirmish — Treatment of La Force — Position at the Great Meadows — Belligerent Feelings of a Young Soldier

On April 29, 1754, Washington sets out from Wills’ Creek at the head of 160 men, joining those sent ahead to clear the road through Savage Mountain and the dreary forest region called “The Shades of Death.” Progress is agonizingly slow — barely four miles a day. Gloomy reports arrive daily from the Ohio: the French are strongly reinforced, diligently constructing their fort. La Force, the wily French emissary who had shadowed Washington on his mission, is prowling about the country with soldiers, suspected of spying. Washington receives word that the Half-King is coming to meet him with fifty warriors. After crossing the Youghiogeny River, Washington receives alarming intelligence: the French are advancing. Scouting parties are sent out. The camp’s situation becomes perilous. Gist brings news of La Force’s movements. A message arrives from the Half-King: the French are approaching; he needs help. Washington marches through the night in a pelting rain, guided by the Half-King’s messengers. At dawn on May 28, guided by the Half-King and his warriors, Washington surprises a party of French soldiers in a rocky glen. A skirmish erupts — the Jumonville affair. The French commander, Ensign Jumonville, is killed. Accounts differ on whether he was attempting to deliver a diplomatic summons or conducting a reconnaissance. The skirmish becomes a European cause célèbre: the French denounce Washington as an assassin; the English defend him. Washington treats the wounded prisoners, including the dangerous La Force, with care. He withdraws to the Great Meadows, a pleasant, level plain, and begins fortifying — the position he calls Fort Necessity. The young soldier’s belligerent feelings are running high; he has tasted action and is eager to press the advantage.

Chapter 12 — Scarcity in the Camp — Death of Colonel Fry — Promotions — Mackay and his Independent Company — Major Muse — Indian Ceremonials — Public Prayers in Camp — Alarms — Independence of an Independent Company — Affairs at the Great Meadows — Desertion of the Indian Allies — Capitulation of Fort Necessity — Van Braam as an Interpreter — Indian Plunderers — Return to Williamsburg — Vote of Thanks of the House of Burgesses — Subsequent Fortunes of the Half-King — Comments on the Affair of Jumonville and the Conduct of Van Braam

Scarcity becomes severe in Washington’s camp at the Great Meadows. For six days the troops are without flour. The Half-King arrives with thirty or forty warriors and their families — more mouths to feed. News arrives of Colonel Fry’s death at Wills’ Creek, devolving command of the regiment on Washington, who promptly promotes Captain Adam Stephen and recommends Jacob Van Braam for a captaincy. The Fort Necessity palisade is completed — named for the “pinching famine” that prevailed during its construction. Captain Mackay arrives with an independent company of regulars from South Carolina, and questions of rank immediately arise: Mackay holds a royal commission and considers himself superior to Washington despite his lower rank, while Washington holds only a provincial commission. This friction over precedence between regular and provincial officers will become a recurring theme. The Indian allies grow restless and the Half-King becomes increasingly disillusioned with Washington’s defensive posture. On July 3, 1754, a superior French force under Captain de Villiers (Jumonville’s brother) attacks Fort Necessity in a driving rain. The trenches fill with water, the men’s muskets become useless, and the position is untenable. Washington is forced to capitulate. The terms of surrender, negotiated through Van Braam’s interpretation, contain an admission that Washington had “assassinated” Jumonville — a word Van Braam allegedly translated as “killed” or “death of.” This admission will haunt Washington. The Half-King, disgusted, abandons the English and goes over to the French. Washington marches his men back to Williamsburg, where the House of Burgesses votes him its thanks for his gallant conduct while studiously avoiding comment on the capitulation. Irving offers extended commentary on the Jumonville controversy and Van Braam’s dubious translation.

Chapter 13 — Founding of Fort Cumberland — Secret Letter of Stobo — The Indian Messenger — Project of Dinwiddie — His Perplexities — A Taint of Republicanism in the Colonial Assemblies — Dinwiddie’s Military Measures — Washington Quits the Service — Overtures of Governor Sharpe, of Maryland — Washington’s Dignified Reply — Questions of Rank between Royal and Provincial Troops — Treatment of the French Prisoners — Fate of La Force — Anecdotes of Stobo and Van Braam

In the aftermath of Fort Necessity, the British begin fortifying Wills’ Creek, naming the post Fort Cumberland. Captain Stobo, who had been held as a hostage by the French after the capitulation, manages to smuggle out a secret letter revealing French strength and weakness — intelligence that will play a role in future planning. Governor Dinwiddie, struggling with the independence of the colonial assemblies — what Irving calls a “taint of republicanism” — finds his military measures obstructed at every turn. Washington, frustrated by the inequity of rank between royal and provincial officers and by the slights he receives as a provincial commander, resigns his commission. He refuses Governor Sharpe of Maryland’s overtures with characteristic dignity, stating he will not serve on terms that subordinate him to officers with less actual command experience. The French prisoners from the Jumonville affair are sent to England; La Force, the dangerous frontier operative, is kept in close confinement. Irving recounts anecdotes of Stobo’s resourcefulness and Van Braam’s ambiguous behavior during and after the capitulation. Washington returns to private life, turning his attention to his Mount Vernon estate.

Chapter 14 — Return to Quiet Life — French and English Prepare for Hostilities — Plan of a Campaign — General Braddock — His Character — Sir John St. Clair, Quartermaster-General — His Tour of Inspection — Projected Roads — Arrival of Braddock — Military Consultations and Plans — Commodore Keppel and his Seamen — Ships and Troops at Alexandria — Excitement of Washington — Invited to Join the Staff of Braddock — A Mother’s Objections — Washington at Alexandria — Grand Council of Governors — Military Arrangements — Colonel William Johnson — Sir John St. Clair at Fort Cumberland — His Explosions of Wrath — Their Effects — Indians to be Enlisted — Captain Jack and his Band of Bush-Beaters

Washington returns to the quiet life of a Virginia planter, but the calm is short-lived. Both France and England prepare for open war. The British government, roused by the reverses in the Ohio Valley, resolves on a campaign of three prongs: against Fort Duquesne, against Niagara, and against Crown Point. Major-General Edward Braddock is sent to Virginia with two regiments of regulars. Irving offers a portrait of Braddock: a brave, experienced officer of the Coldstream Guards, honorable, punctilious, and a strict disciplinarian — but arrogant, obstinate, and contemptuous of colonial ways and “savages.” Sir John St. Clair, the quartermaster-general, makes an incendiary tour of inspection, projecting roads and exploding in wrath at the colonial governments’ slowness. Ships and troops assemble at Alexandria under Commodore Keppel. Washington’s martial excitement revives. Governor Dinwiddie recommends him to Braddock, who invites the young colonel to join his staff as a volunteer aide-de-camp. Washington’s mother objects strenuously to her son’s return to danger. But Washington sets out for Alexandria, where a grand council of governors confers with Braddock on military arrangements — including the plan to enlist Indians as auxiliaries. Colonel William Johnson, the Indian agent, is consulted. Sir John St. Clair’s explosions of wrath at Fort Cumberland have their effect in spurring reluctant contractors. Meanwhile, Captain Jack and his band of “bush-beaters” — frontier riflemen — offer their services on their own unconventional terms.

Chapter 15 — Washington Proclaimed Aide-de-Camp — Disappointments at Fredericktown — Benjamin Franklin and Braddock — Contracts — Departure for Wills’ Creek — Rough Roads — The General in his Chariot — Camp at Fort Cumberland — Hugh Mercer — Dr. Craik — Military Tactics — Camp Rules — Secretary Peters — Indians in Camp — Indian Beauties — The Princess Bright Lightning — Errand to Williamsburg — Braddock’s Opinion of Contractors and Indians — Arrival of Conveyances

Washington is formally proclaimed aide-de-camp to General Braddock. The expedition encounters its first disappointments at Fredericktown, Maryland, where the promised supplies and wagons fail to materialize. At this critical juncture, Benjamin Franklin — then postmaster-general for the colonies — visits Braddock’s camp. Irving recounts Franklin’s famous interview with the general: Braddock expresses confidence in his ability to sweep the French from the Ohio with regulars alone; Franklin presciently warns him of the dangers of Indian ambush in the wilderness, which Braddock waves aside with contempt. Franklin nonetheless procures wagons from the Pennsylvania farmers through his personal influence. The army departs for Wills’ Creek over rough roads, with Braddock riding in his chariot rather than on horseback — a detail Irving notes as emblematic of the general’s inability to adapt to wilderness conditions. At Fort Cumberland, the army assembles: Braddock’s regulars, colonial troops under Washington, and a small company of Indians. Washington is reunited with Dr. Craik, a Scottish physician who will become his lifelong friend and personal doctor, and meets Hugh Mercer, a fellow Scot who served at Culloden. Camp rules and military tactics are drilled. Secretary Peters assists with administration. Irving describes the Indians in camp, including “Indian beauties” and the Princess Bright Lightning, a young Indian woman who attracts attention. Washington is sent on an errand to Williamsburg to expedite supplies. Braddock’s opinions of contractors — universally contemptuous — and his view of Indians as useless auxiliaries are recorded. After many delays, the conveyances finally arrive and the march can begin.

Chapter 16 — March from Fort Cumberland — The Great Savage Mountain — Camp at the Little Meadows — Division of the Forces — Captain Jack and his Band — Scarooyadi in Danger — Illness of Washington — His Halt at the Youghiogeny — March of Braddock — The Great Meadows — Lurking Enemies — Their Tracks — Precautions — Thickety Run — Scouts — Indian Murders — Funeral of an Indian Warrior — Camp on the Monongahela — Washington’s Arrival There — March for Fort Duquesne — The Fording of the Monongahela — The Battle — The Retreat — Death of Braddock

The army marches from Fort Cumberland in June 1755, a formidable array of regulars, provincials, wagons, and artillery, struggling over the Great Savage Mountain. At the Little Meadows, Braddock, impressed by Washington’s advice on the need for speed, divides the force: twelve hundred chosen men under his personal command will press ahead, while Colonel Dunbar follows slowly with the heavy baggage. Captain Jack and his bush-beaters join, but Braddock’s rigid discipline soon alienates them. Scarooyadi, an Indian chief, narrowly escapes harm from soldiers who mistake him for an enemy. Washington falls desperately ill with a fever and is forced to halt at the Youghiogeny River, joining the main force later. Braddock pushes on, crossing the Great Meadows where Fort Necessity had stood — now a silent memorial to Washington’s defeat. The army is haunted by lurking enemies; their tracks are everywhere. Scouts report signs of Indians. Precautions are taken, but Braddock dismisses the danger. At Thickety Run, an Indian attack kills several soldiers. Washington, still weak from illness, rejoins Braddock’s camp on the Monongahela on July 8. The next morning, July 9, the army fords the Monongahela in full battle array — bright uniforms, drums beating, colors flying — a magnificent spectacle. Two fords are crossed successfully. Then, emerging from the second ford into the forest, the advance guard is struck by a devastating volley. The French and their Indian allies, hidden behind trees and ravines, pour a murderous fire into the compact British ranks. The regulars, unable to see their enemies, fire blindly into the woods. Braddock charges forward, has horses shot from under him, and falls mortally wounded. Washington, albeit weak from illness, rides through the battle, his coat pierced by four bullets, experiencing what he will later describe as a providential deliverance. The British force disintegrates in a panic-stricken retreat. Braddock is carried from the field and dies four days later near the Great Meadows, buried in the road at the center of the retreating column to prevent his body from being discovered and mutilated.

Chapter 17 — Aftermath of the Defeat — Arrival at Fort Cumberland — Letters of Washington to his Family — Panic of Dunbar — Triumph of the French

The shattered remnants of Braddock’s army straggle into Fort Cumberland. Washington writes letters to his family describing the catastrophe with remarkable composure, acknowledging his own providential escapes. The French and their Indian allies triumph on the field, acquiring a vast quantity of military stores, artillery, and the confidence that the British regulars can be beaten in the wilderness. The frontier lies exposed.

Chapter 18 — Costs of Campaigning — Measures for Public Safety — Washington in Command — Head-Quarters at Winchester — Lord Fairfax and his Troop of Horse — Indian Ravages — Panic at Winchester — Cause of the Alarm — Operations Elsewhere — Shirley against Niagara — Johnson against Crown Point — Affair at Lake George — Death of Dieskau

Washington tallies the costs of the campaign — financial, material, and human. Virginia takes measures for public safety, appointing Washington to command all provincial forces with the rank of colonel and a salary, though he is given only limited authority. He establishes his headquarters at Winchester in the Shenandoah Valley. Lord Fairfax raises a troop of horse and joins the defense. The frontier is ravaged by Indian war parties emboldened by Braddock’s defeat — settlements burned, families massacred, panic spreading from the mountains to the Tidewater. At one point, Winchester itself is thrown into panic by a false alarm. Irving also surveys military operations elsewhere: Governor Shirley of Massachusetts attempting against Niagara, and Colonel William Johnson marching against Crown Point. Johnson’s campaign culminates in the Battle of Lake George, where the French commander Baron Dieskau is defeated and mortally wounded — Dieskau himself, ironically, had previously given Washington a friendly warning during his mission to the French frontier. The mixed fortunes of 1755 show that while the French hold the Ohio, the British are still capable of victory in the north.

Chapter 19 — Reform in the Militia Laws — Discipline of the Troops — Dagworthy and the Question of Precedence — Washington’s Journey to Boston — Style of Travelling — Conference with Shirley — The Earl of Loudoun — Military Rule for the Colonies — Washington at New York — Miss Mary Philipse

Washington works tirelessly to reform Virginia’s militia laws, which he finds wholly inadequate for frontier defense. He drills discipline into his raw provincial troops. A new dispute over precedence arises with Captain Dagworthy of the Maryland forces, who holds a royal commission and claims superiority over Washington despite commanding fewer men. Washington, determined to settle the question of rank once and for all, undertakes a long winter journey to Boston to confer with Governor Shirley, the commander-in-chief of British forces in America. Irving describes the style of travelling in colonial America — the roads, the inns, the hospitality encountered along the way. Washington’s conference with Shirley results in a favorable resolution: Dagworthy is put in his place, and Washington’s rank is recognized. News arrives of the Earl of Loudoun’s appointment as the new commander-in-chief of British forces, bringing a more systematized military rule for the colonies. During his journey, Washington stops in New York and is introduced to Miss Mary Philipse, a celebrated belle and heiress. Though Washington is said to have been charmed, no serious attachment forms — Miss Philipse later marries another.

Chapter 20 — Troubles in the Shenandoah Valley — Greenway Court and Lord Fairfax in Danger — Alarms at Winchester — Washington Appealed to for Protection — Attacked by the Virginia Press — Honored by the Public — Projects for Defence — Suggestions of Washington — The Gentlemen Associators — Retreat of the Savages — Expedition against Kittanning — Captain Hugh Mercer — Second Struggle through the Wilderness

The Shenandoah Valley suffers continuous Indian raids. Greenway Court, Lord Fairfax’s wilderness seat, is threatened; the elderly nobleman refuses to abandon his home. Winchester is repeatedly alarmed, and Washington is appealed to for protection by frantic settlers. The Virginia press, frustrated by the continuing frontier crisis, attacks Washington’s conduct. The public, however, honors him. Washington proposes various defensive projects: a chain of frontier forts, better-organized ranging parties, and more effective militia recruitment. The “Gentlemen Associators” — volunteer cavalry formed by the gentry — provide mobile defense. The savages eventually retreat for the winter. An expedition is mounted against the Indian town of Kittanning, where Captain Hugh Mercer — who had been wounded at the battle of the Monongahela and barely escaped with his life — distinguishes himself. But the frontier remains a crucible of suffering, and Washington’s frustration with the inadequate response from the colonial government and the British command continues to grow.

Chapter 21 — Founding of Fort Loudoun — Washington’s Tour of Inspection — Inefficiency of the Militia System — Gentlemen Soldiers — Cross-Purposes with Dinwiddie — Military Affairs in the North — Delays of Lord Loudoun — Activity of Montcalm — Loudoun in Winter Quarters

Washington oversees the founding of Fort Loudoun at Winchester, named for the Earl of Loudoun, the new British commander. He makes a thorough tour of inspection along the frontier, finding the militia system hopelessly inefficient — the men are poorly armed, undisciplined, prone to desertion, and the officers are frequently “gentlemen soldiers” with more social standing than military competence. He finds himself at cross-purposes with Governor Dinwiddie over funding, troop deployment, and the organization of the regiment. Meanwhile, military affairs in the north take a grave turn. Lord Loudoun, a cautious and methodical commander, delays incessantly at Albany, allowing the French under the new Marquis de Montcalm — an energetic and brilliant commander — to seize the initiative. Montcalm captures Fort Oswego on Lake Ontario, a stunning blow to British prestige. Loudoun settles into winter quarters in New York, his campaign effectively over. Washington watches these developments with growing dismay.

Chapter 22 — Washington Vindicates his Conduct to Lord Loudoun — His Reception by his Lordship — Military Plans — Lord Loudoun at Halifax — Montcalm on Lake George — His Triumphs — Lord Loudoun’s Failures — Washington at Winchester — Continued Misunderstandings with Dinwiddie — Return to Mount Vernon

Washington travels north to meet Lord Loudoun in Philadelphia, vindicating his conduct and the needs of the Virginia forces. His reception by his lordship is polite but unproductive: Loudoun is absorbed in grand strategic schemes. The centerpiece of Loudoun’s campaign is an amphibious expedition against Louisbourg — he sails with most of his forces to Halifax, only to find the French fleet too strong and the season too far advanced, and returns without firing a shot. Meanwhile, Montcalm capitalizes on Loudoun’s absence, marching against Fort William Henry on Lake George and capturing it after a bloody siege. Irving details Montcalm’s triumphs and Loudoun’s failures with sharp contrast. Washington returns to Winchester to find his misunderstandings with Dinwiddie as intractable as ever — quarrels over recruiting, supply, and the authority of the governor versus the colonel. Wearied and frustrated, Washington obtains leave and returns to Mount Vernon for the winter.

Chapter 23 — Washington Recovers his Health — Again in Command at Fort Loudoun — Administration of Pitt — Loudoun Succeeded by General Abercrombie — Military Arrangements — Washington Commander-in-Chief of the Virginia Forces — Amherst against Louisburg — General Wolfe — Montgomery — Capture of Louisburg — Abercrombie on Lake George — Death of Lord Howe — Repulse of Abercrombie — Success of Bradstreet at Oswego

Washington’s health, undermined by the rigors of frontier service, improves during his winter rest at Mount Vernon. He returns to command at Fort Loudoun to find the strategic picture transformed. In London, William Pitt has taken control of the government, infusing new energy into the war effort. Lord Loudoun is recalled and succeeded by General Abercrombie. Under Pitt’s grand design, three major operations are mounted: Amherst against Louisbourg, Abercrombie against Ticonderoga and Crown Point, and Forbes against Fort Duquesne. Washington, despite his frustrations with the provincial system, is appointed commander-in-chief of all Virginia forces, finally receiving the authority he has long sought. Louisbourg falls in July 1758, a brilliant victory executed by General Amherst and a young brigadier named James Wolfe, who emerges as a rising star. But Abercrombie’s campaign on Lake George ends in disaster: the promising Lord Howe is killed in a skirmish, and Abercrombie throws his troops in futile frontal assaults against Montcalm’s entrenched position at Ticonderoga, suffering appalling losses. The one bright spot is Colonel Bradstreet’s capture of Fort Frontenac (Oswego), cutting French communications. Irving’s narrative weaves these disparate threads together, showing how Washington’s own campaign fits into the larger imperial struggle.

Chapter 24 — Slow Operations — Washington Orders Out the Militia — Mission to Williamsburg — Halt at Mr. Chamberlayne’s — Mrs. Martha Custis — A Brief Courtship — An Engagement — Return to Winchester — The Rifle Dress — Indian Scouts — Washington Elected to the House of Burgesses — Tidings of Amherst’s Success — The New Road to Fort Duquesne — March for the Fort — Indiscreet Conduct of Major Grant — Disastrous Consequences — Washington Advances against Fort Duquesne — End of the Expedition — Washington Returns Home — His Marriage

The 1758 campaign against Fort Duquesne proceeds with agonizing slowness. General Forbes, commanding the expedition, is seriously ill and proceeds cautiously, hewing a new road through Pennsylvania rather than using Braddock’s old route. Washington, eager for action, orders out the militia and prepares his regiment. In the spring, while en route to Williamsburg on military business, he stops at the estate of Mr. Chamberlayne, a planter, where he meets Martha Dandridge Custis — a wealthy young widow with two small children. The courtship is famously brief: within a few weeks, Washington proposes and is accepted. His thoughts now divided between war and marriage, he returns to Winchester. The troops under his command adopt a distinctive “rifle dress” — a practical frontier uniform. Indian scouts are employed. Washington is elected to the House of Burgesses — his first step into political life. Tidings arrive of Amherst’s capture of Louisbourg, boosting morale. Forbes’ army advances painfully toward Fort Duquesne, building their new road through the wilderness. Major Grant, dispatched on a reconnaissance with eight hundred men, behaves indiscreetly, parading within sight of the fort as if on a peaceful maneuver; the French sortie and maul his force severely, taking Grant prisoner. Washington, now second-in-command, proposes various aggressive moves, but Forbes proceeds with extreme caution. When Forbes finally advances on the fort, the French, their position weakened by Bradstreet’s capture of Frontenac and their Indian allies melting away, blow up Fort Duquesne and retreat. The British occupy the smoking ruins on November 25, 1758, renaming the site Fort Pitt in honor of the great minister. Washington resigns his commission and returns to Virginia. He marries Martha Custis on January 6, 1759, at her estate, the White House, on the Pamunkey River. The wedding is celebrated with the hospitality typical of old Virginia.

Chapter 25 — Plan of Operations for 1759 — Investment of Fort Niagara — Death of Prideaux — Success of Sir William Johnson — Amherst at Ticonderoga — Wolfe at Quebec — His Triumph and Death — Fate of Montcalm — Capitulation of Quebec — Attempt of De Levi to Retake it — Arrival of a British Fleet — Last Stand of the French at Montreal — Surrender of Canada

This chapter turns from Washington’s personal narrative to the wider canvas of the war’s climactic campaigns of 1759-60 — “the year of miracles” in British annals. The British plan of operations for 1759 involves a three-pronged offensive: Prideaux against Niagara, Amherst against Ticonderoga and Crown Point, and Wolfe against Quebec. Prideaux invests Fort Niagara but is killed in the trenches; Sir William Johnson assumes command and completes the victory, capturing the fort after a desperate battle, effectively severing French communications with the interior. Amherst advances methodically, capturing Ticonderoga and Crown Point without a fight — the French abandon them rather than be trapped. But the climactic event is Wolfe’s campaign against Quebec. Irving describes the siege with epic detail: the British scaling the cliffs at night, the battle on the Plains of Abraham, the death of both Wolfe and Montcalm, the capitulation of Quebec. The following spring, the Chevalier de Levi attempts to retake Quebec, defeating the British under Murray outside the walls — but the arrival of a British fleet forces him to retreat. The last stand of the French at Montreal, where Amherst’s converging columns trap the remnant of the French army, ends with the surrender of Canada. The war in North America is over.

Chapter 26 — Washington’s Installation in the House of Burgesses — His Rural Life — Mount Vernon and its Vicinity — Aristocratical Days of Virginia — Washington’s Management of his Estate — Domestic Habits — Fox-Hunting — Lord Fairfax — Fishing and Duck-Shooting — The Poacher — Lynch Law — Aquatic State — Life at Annapolis — Washington in the Dismal Swamp

Washington takes his seat in the House of Burgesses, where Speaker Robinson delivers a formal tribute to his military services. Washington rises to respond — but finds himself too flustered to speak. He is saved when the Speaker says, “Sit down, Mr. Washington; your modesty is equal to your valor, and that surpasses any power of language I possess.” The scene epitomizes the contrast between the confident soldier and the diffident public man. The bulk of the chapter, however, is a richly detailed portrait of Washington’s life as a Virginia planter during the years 1759-1769 — the longest period of domestic peace he would ever know. Mount Vernon, his beloved estate on the Potomac, is described in loving detail: the mansion house, the grounds, the views of the river. Irving evokes the “aristocratical days of Virginia” — the grand hospitality, the race meetings, the elections, the balls. Washington is shown as a systematic, scientific farmer: he rotates crops, experiments with fertilizers, keeps meticulous accounts, and expands his landholdings. His domestic habits are regular and industrious: he rises early, dines at two, takes tea in the afternoon, and retires early. Fox-hunting with Lord Fairfax and the local gentry is a favorite pursuit. Fishing and duck-shooting on the Potomac provide recreation. A poacher is dealt with by “lynch law” — a term Irving explains as deriving from a Virginia justice of the peace. The “aquatic state” of Virginia plantation life — travel by boat on the great rivers and bays — is described. Washington visits bustling Annapolis and explores the Great Dismal Swamp, a vast, gloomy region of cypress and tangled vegetation, where he becomes involved in a land-speculation scheme.

Chapter 27 — Treaty of Peace — Pontiac’s War — Course of Public Events — Board of Trade against Paper Currency — Restrictive Policy of England — Navigation Laws — Discontents in New England — Of the Other Colonies — Projects to Raise Revenue by Taxation — Blow at the Independence of the Judiciary — Naval Commanders Employed as Custom-house Officers — Retaliation of the Colonists — Taxation Resisted in Boston — Passing of the Stamp Act — Burst of Opposition in Virginia — Speech of Patrick Henry

The Treaty of Paris (1763) ends the war, stripping France of her North American empire. But almost immediately, Pontiac’s War erupts — a desperate Indian uprising against British rule in the newly conquered western territories. The British government, staggering under a massive war debt, adopts a series of measures that will drive the colonies toward revolution. Irving traces the course of public events: the Board of Trade’s opposition to colonial paper currency, the tightening of the Navigation Acts (which restricted colonial trade to British ships and markets), and the project to raise revenue by direct taxation of the colonies. A blow at the independence of the colonial judiciary and the employment of naval commanders as customs officers further inflame tensions. Retaliation begins: smuggling increases, and the colonists resist taxation in Boston and elsewhere. The Stamp Act of 1765 — requiring stamps on all legal documents, newspapers, and pamphlets — sparks a firestorm of opposition. In Virginia, the House of Burgesses erupts in debate. Patrick Henry delivers his incendiary speech, defying the king himself, crying “Caesar had his Brutus, Charles I his Cromwell, and George III —” before being interrupted by cries of “Treason!” Henry’s concluding line — “may profit by their example” — electrifies the assembly. Washington, sitting in the Virginia House, hears Henry’s thunder but maintains his own more measured course, watching the imperial crisis unfold.

Chapter 28 — Washington’s Ideas concerning the Stamp Act — Opposition to it in the Colonies — Portentous Ceremonies at Boston and New York — Non-Importation Agreement among the Merchants — Washington and George Mason — Dismissal of Grenville from the British Cabinet — Franklin before the House of Commons — Repeal of the Stamp Act — Joy of Washington — Fresh Causes of Colonial Dissensions — Circular of the General Court of Massachusetts — Embarkation of Troops for Boston — Measures of the Bostonians

Washington’s views on the Stamp Act emerge through his correspondence. While he deplores the act as unconstitutional — arguing that the colonists can only be taxed by their own representatives — he opposes the violent demonstrations that erupt in Boston and New York, where effigies are burned, customs officials are attacked, and the stamp distributor is forced to resign. Merchants in the major ports agree on a non-importation agreement to pressure British merchants. Washington corresponds with George Mason, a neighbor and political ally, exploring the constitutional arguments and practical responses. In London, the Grenville ministry falls and is replaced by the Rockingham Whigs. Benjamin Franklin appears before the House of Commons, delivering a masterful examination that exposes the practical impossibility of enforcing the Stamp Act. Parliament repeals the act in 1766. Washington writes of his joy at this news, expressing hope that harmony between Britain and the colonies can be restored. But Parliament immediately passes the Declaratory Act, asserting its right to legislate for the colonies “in all cases whatsoever” — a harbinger of future conflict. Fresh causes of dissension quickly arise: the Townshend duties (taxes on imported glass, lead, paper, and tea), the New York Assembly’s suspension for resisting the Quartering Act, and the dispatch of troops to Boston. The circular letter of the Massachusetts General Court, calling for united colonial resistance, circulates through the colonies. The Bostonians take measures of defiance, including renewed non-importation agreements.

Chapter 29 — Cheerful Life at Mount Vernon — Washington and George Mason — Correspondence concerning the Non-Importation Agreement — Feeling toward England — Opening of the Legislative Session — Semi-regal State of Lord Botetourt — High-toned Proceedings of the House — Sympathy with New England — Dissolved by Lord Botetourt — Washington and the Articles of Association

Despite the gathering storm, Washington continues to enjoy the cheerful life of a Virginia planter at Mount Vernon. He corresponds regularly with George Mason, exchanging views on the political crisis and the details of the non-importation agreement. The feeling toward England among the Virginia gentry is still conflicted — proud of their English heritage, they resent parliamentary encroachments but hope for reconciliation. The opening of the legislative session of the House of Burgesses is described in vivid detail: the entrance of Lord Botetourt, the new royal governor, in semi-regal state, with a coach-and-six, liveried outriders, and a guard of grenadiers. The Burgesses, however, proceed with high-toned proceedings, asserting their rights and sympathizing openly with Massachusetts. Lord Botetourt dissolves the assembly in response. The Burgesses reconvene at the Raleigh Tavern, where they adopt the Articles of Association — a comprehensive non-importation agreement binding the signatories to boycott British goods. Washington signs the articles and works to enforce them among his neighbors, strengthening his reputation as a leader in the colonial resistance.

Chapter 30 — Hood at Boston — The General Court Refuses to do Business under Military Sway — Resists the Billeting Act — Effect of the Non-Importation Association — Lord North Premier — Duties Revoked Except on Tea — The Boston Massacre — Disuse of Tea — Conciliatory Conduct of Lord Botetourt — His Death

Commodore Hood’s ships patrol Boston harbor; the town is occupied by British troops. The Massachusetts General Court refuses to conduct business under military sway, resisting the Billeting Act that requires the colony to quarter the soldiers. The non-importation association proves effective: British trade drops sharply, pressuring London. Lord North becomes prime minister, adopting a policy of partial concession: most of the Townshend duties are revoked, but the tax on tea is retained — a symbolic assertion of Parliament’s right to tax. Tensions in Boston escalate. On March 5, 1770, the Boston Massacre occurs: a confrontation between a crowd and British soldiers results in the deaths of five colonists. The incident electrifies the colonies. Tea becomes a symbol of British oppression; colonists begin a widespread disuse of the beverage. In Virginia, the conciliatory conduct of Lord Botetourt stands in sharp contrast to the military coercion in Massachusetts — he governs with tact and moderation, seeking to heal the breach. Virginia remains comparatively calm. Botetourt’s sudden death in October 1770 is mourned by the colonists; he had won their affection through his even-handedness. His successor will prove far less conciliatory.

Chapter 31 — Expedition of Washington to the Ohio, in Behalf of Soldiers’ Claims — Uneasy State of the Frontier — Visit to Fort Pitt — George Croghan — His Mishaps during Pontiac’s War — Washington Descends the Ohio — Scenes and Adventures along the River — Indian Hunting Camp — Interview with an Old Sachem at the Mouth of the Kanawha — Return — Claims of Stobo and Van Braam — Letter to Colonel George Muse

In the autumn of 1770, Washington undertakes a journey to the Ohio country to inspect his western lands and advocate for the bounty lands promised to the soldiers of the French and Indian War. The frontier remains uneasy despite Pontiac’s defeat. He visits Fort Pitt (formerly Fort Duquesne), where he meets the veteran trader George Croghan, who recounts his harrowing experiences during Pontiac’s War — captured, tortured, and held for ransom by Indians. Washington and his party descend the Ohio River for several hundred miles in a canoe, Irving narrating the scenes and adventures along the way: the pristine forests, the abundant game, the majestic river. They encounter an Indian hunting camp, where Washington observes the daily life of the native inhabitants. His most significant encounter is with an old Seneca sachem at the mouth of the Kanawha River, who delivers a speech about the land — a poignant meditation on the dispossession of his people. Washington’s diary records his careful assessment of the lands for their quality and potential value. On his return, he deals with the long-standing claims of Stobo and Van Braam for their services, and writes a memorable letter to Colonel George Muse, who had apparently slighted him — a vigorous exercise in Washington’s penchant for defending his honor.

Chapter 32 — Lord Dunmore Governor of Virginia — Piques the Pride of the Virginians — Opposition of the Assembly — Corresponding Committees — Death of Miss Custis — Washington’s Guardianship of John Parke Custis — His Opinions as to Premature Travel and Premature Marriage

Lord Dunmore arrives as the new royal governor of Virginia, and almost immediately piques the pride of the Virginians by his haughty manner and his assertion of prerogative. The Assembly opposes him on a series of issues — land grants, fees, and the dissolution of the elected body. Corresponding committees are formed throughout the colonies, maintaining communication and coordinating resistance. In Washington’s personal life, tragedy strikes with the death of Martha’s daughter, Miss Custis, at the age of seventeen. Washington becomes the guardian of the surviving son, John Parke Custis (Jacky), a youth of intelligence but unsettled habits. Irving records Washington’s letters on the subject of education and child-rearing — his firm opinions on the dangers of premature travel abroad for young men (the Grand Tour before the mind is formed) and his objections to premature marriage. Jacky Custis will be a source of both affection and anxiety for Washington throughout his life.

Chapter 33 — Lord North’s Bill Favoring the Exportation of Teas — Ships Freighted with Tea to the Colonies — Sent Back from Some of the Ports — Tea Destroyed at Boston — Passage of the Boston Port Bill — Session of the House of Burgesses — Splendid Opening — Burst of Indignation at the Port Bill — House Dissolved — Resolutions at the Raleigh Tavern — Project of a General Congress — Washington and Lord Dunmore — The Port Bill Goes into Effect — General Gage at Boston — League and Covenant

Lord North’s Tea Act of 1773, designed to bail out the struggling East India Company by allowing it to ship tea directly to America, unintentionally precipitates the final crisis. Ships freighted with tea arrive at American ports; at some (Philadelphia, New York, Charleston) the tea is either sent back or stored; at Boston, on December 16, 1773, disguised “Mohawks” dump 342 chests of tea into the harbor. Parliament retaliates with the Boston Port Bill, closing the port until the tea is paid for. The news reaches Virginia in May 1774, during the session of the House of Burgesses. The session opens with splendid ceremonial — Lord Dunmore in his coach, the Burgesses in full dress — but the mood turns to fury when the Port Bill is announced. The House passes a resolution declaring June 1, 1774 (the date the Port Bill takes effect) a day of fasting, humiliation, and prayer. Lord Dunmore dissolves the assembly in anger. The Burgesses reconvene at the Raleigh Tavern, passing resolutions of sympathy with Boston and proposing a general congress of the colonies. Washington and Lord Dunmore face off as the political atmosphere becomes charged. The Port Bill goes into effect; General Gage arrives in Boston as the new military governor. Throughout the colonies, a “League and Covenant” is formed, binding subscribers to cease all trade with Britain until the grievances are redressed.

Chapter 34 — Washington Chairman of a Political Meeting — Correspondence with Bryan Fairfax — Patriotic Resolutions — Washington’s Opinions on Public Affairs — Non-Importation Scheme — Convention at Williamsburg — Washington Appointed a Delegate to the General Congress — Letter from Bryan Fairfax — Perplexities of General Gage at Boston

Washington chairs a political meeting at the courthouse in Fairfax County, where the citizens adopt a set of patriotic resolutions — the Fairfax Resolves, drafted largely by George Mason, affirming colonial rights, condemning the Boston Port Bill, and calling for a non-importation agreement and a continental congress. Washington’s correspondence with his neighbor Bryan Fairfax, a loyalist who argues for more moderate responses, reveals Washington’s increasingly firm views on public affairs: he is now convinced that the British government is engaged in a deliberate conspiracy against American liberty. A convention of Virginia delegates meets in Williamsburg in August 1774; it endorses the call for a General Congress and appoints seven delegates, including Washington, to represent Virginia at the First Continental Congress in Philadelphia. Bryan Fairfax writes Washington a letter expressing his reluctance to go as far as the patriots and his hope for reconciliation — Washington responds with patient arguments but makes clear he will not retreat from the position the colonies have taken. Meanwhile, General Gage, at Boston, finds himself in deepening perplexity: he commands the strongest military force in America, yet the entire countryside is in arms against him.

Chapter 35 — Meeting of the First Congress — Opening Ceremonies — Eloquence of Patrick Henry and Henry Lee — Declaratory Resolution — Bill of Rights — State Papers — Chatham’s Opinions of Congress — Washington’s Correspondence with Captain Mackenzie — Views with Respect to Independence — Departure of Fairfax for England

The First Continental Congress meets in Philadelphia in September 1774, gathering delegates from twelve colonies (Georgia alone is absent). Irving describes the opening ceremonies with their solemnity and gravity — the men who have assembled include the most distinguished figures in America. Patrick Henry and Henry (“Light-Horse Harry”) Lee deliver speeches that are long remembered for their eloquence and fire. Congress adopts a Declaratory Resolution asserting the rights of the colonies and a Bill of Rights enumerating their grievances. State papers — addresses to the king, to the people of British America, and to the inhabitants of the Province of Quebec — are drafted and issued. In England, Lord Chatham (William Pitt) speaks in favor of the Congress and urges conciliation, but the government is unmoved. Washington corresponds with his former military acquaintance Captain Mackenzie, a British officer, in a remarkable exchange of letters that reveals Washington’s mind at this critical juncture: he is not yet for independence, but he is convinced that the colonies must resist arbitrary power or submit to slavery. Bryan Fairfax, unable to join the patriots, departs for England, foreshadowing the tragic sundering of friendships that the Revolution will bring.

Chapter 36 — Gage’s Military Measures — Removal of Gunpowder from the Arsenal — Public Agitation — Alarms in the Country — Civil Government Obstructed — Belligerent Symptoms — Israel Putnam and General Charles Lee, their Characters and Stories — General Election — Self-constituted Congress — Hancock President — Adjourns to Concord — Remonstrance to Gage — His Perplexities — Generals Artemas Ward and Seth Pomeroy — Committee of Safety — Committee of Supplies — Restlessness throughout the Land — Independent Companies in Virginia — Military Tone at Mount Vernon — Washington’s Military Guests — Major Horatio Gates — Anecdotes concerning him — General Charles Lee — His Peculiarities and Dogs — Washington at the Richmond Convention — War Speech of Patrick Henry — Washington’s Military Intentions

General Gage’s military measures in Massachusetts push the colony toward open conflict. In September 1774, a party of British soldiers removes gunpowder from the arsenal at Cambridge, triggering a wave of public agitation. Throughout the countryside, alarms spread; tens of thousands of militia muster and march. Civil government in Massachusetts is effectively obstructed as the royal courts cannot function. Belligerent symptoms multiply. Irving introduces two colorful characters: Israel Putnam, a blunt, courageous Connecticut farmer and veteran of the French and Indian War, famous for his exploits and stories; and General Charles Lee, a eccentric, brilliant former British officer who has cast his lot with the American cause. A general election in Massachusetts produces a new Provincial Congress; John Hancock is chosen as its president. The Congress adjourns from Salem to Concord for safety. It sends a remonstrance to Gage, who finds himself in impossible perplexities — forced to maintain British authority with insufficient troops in a hostile country. Generals Artemas Ward and Seth Pomeroy are appointed to lead the Massachusetts forces. Committees of Safety and of Supplies are formed to coordinate military preparations. Restlessness spreads throughout the land. In Virginia, “independent companies” of volunteer militia spring up, drilling and arming themselves. Mount Vernon takes on a military tone: Washington drills the Fairfax Independent Company, and his guests are increasingly military men — Major Horatio Gates (a former British officer of German birth who settled in Virginia), and the eccentric General Charles Lee, who travels with his pack of dogs and whose peculiarities provide endless anecdotes. Washington attends the Virginia Convention at Richmond in March 1775. Patrick Henry delivers his “Give me liberty or give me death” speech, which Irving recounts with dramatic power. Washington, rising to speak, states his military intentions: he is ready to take up arms in defense of American liberty.

Chapter 37 — Infatuation in British Councils — Colonel Grant, the Braggart — Coercive Measures — Expedition against the Military Magazine at Concord — Battle of Lexington — The Cry of Blood through the Land — Old Soldiers of the French War — John Stark — Israel Putnam — Rising of the Yeomanry — Measures of Lord Dunmore in Virginia — Indignation of the Virginians — Hugh Mercer and the Friends of Liberty — Arrival of the News of Lexington at Mount Vernon — Effect on Bryan Fairfax, Gates, and Washington

Irving describes the “infatuation” in British councils — the conviction that the Americans would not fight. Colonel Grant, one of Braddock’s veterans and a notorious braggart, voices this contempt publicly, boasting that he could march from one end of America to the other with five thousand regulars. The coercive measures continue. In April 1775, Gage dispatches an expedition to seize the military stores collected by the Massachusetts Provincial Congress at Concord. The result is the Battle of Lexington and Concord (April 19, 1775). Irving narrates the events: the night march, the confrontation on Lexington Green, the “shot heard round the world,” the bloody retreat of the British under incessant fire from the aroused Massachusetts countryside. The cry of blood spreads through the land. Old soldiers of the French War — John Stark of New Hampshire, Israel Putnam of Connecticut — seize their muskets and rally the yeomanry. In Virginia, Lord Dunmore, emulating Gage, removes gunpowder from the Williamsburg magazine, triggering an explosion of indignation. Hugh Mercer and the “Friends of Liberty” arm themselves. The news of Lexington reaches Mount Vernon late in April. It has a profound effect on those gathered there: Bryan Fairfax, still hoping for reconciliation, is crushed; Gates, the former British officer, recognizes that civil war is inevitable; and Washington, in his quiet, determined way, resolves his course. He will lead the Virginia forces to the defense of Boston. The die is cast.

Chapter 38 — Enlisting of Troops in the East — Camp at Boston — General Artemas Ward — Scheme to Surprise Ticonderoga — New Hampshire Grants — Ethan Allen and the Green Mountain Boys — Benedict Arnold — Affair of Ticonderoga and Crown Point — A Dash at St. Johns

While the southern colonies arm themselves, events race forward in the north. The Massachusetts Provincial Congress hurries to enlist troops, establishing a camp at Cambridge under General Artemas Ward. A bold scheme is hatched in the New Hampshire Grants (present-day Vermont) to surprise the strategically vital British forts at Ticonderoga and Crown Point, which guard the water route to Canada. Ethan Allen and his Green Mountain Boys — rough, independent frontier fighters who have already defied New York’s claims to the Grants — organize the expedition. They are joined by Benedict Arnold, a Connecticut militia captain of restless ambition and military genius, who arrives with a commission from the Massachusetts Committee of Safety. The two forceful personalities clash, but Allen leads the assault, bursting into the commandant’s bedchamber at Ticonderoga early on May 10, 1775, demanding surrender “in the name of the Great Jehovah and the Continental Congress!” The fort falls without a fight. Crown Point is captured the next day. A cavalry dash at St. Johns captures its garrison. The green cannon of Ticonderoga — more than a hundred pieces of artillery — will soon prove crucial to the American cause.

Chapter 39 — Second Session of Congress — John Hancock — Petition to the King — Federal Union — Military Measures — Debates about the Army — Question as to Commander-in-Chief — Appointment of Washington — Other Appointments — Letters of Washington to his Wife and Brother — Preparations for Departure

The Second Continental Congress convenes in Philadelphia in May 1775. John Hancock presides. Congress adopts a petition to the king (the Olive Branch Petition) affirming American loyalty and seeking redress — a last attempt at reconciliation that will be rejected. It also begins to assume the powers of a federal union, authorizing the raising of armies, the issuance of currency, and the conduct of war. The critical question is who shall command the Continental Army. The debates are intense: John Hancock covets the position; others argue for General Charles Lee or Artemas Ward. But John Adams rises and, in a carefully prepared speech, nominates Washington — a Virginian whose appointment will bind the South to a struggle centering on New England. The choice is unanimous. Washington is appointed commander-in-chief on June 15, 1775. He accepts the command but refuses any salary, asking only that his expenses be paid. Other general officers are appointed: Ward, Lee, Schuyler, Putnam, and others. Washington writes letters to his wife Martha and to his brother John Augustine — the letter to Martha expresses his deep anxiety and his sense of duty, his hope that he will return safely, and his trust in Providence. Preparations for departure are made. He purchases books on military tactics, arranges his affairs, and prepares to ride north.

Chapter 40 — More Troops Arrive at Boston — Generals Howe, Burgoyne, and Clinton — Proclamation of Gage — Nature of the American Army — Scornful Conduct of the British Officers — Project of the Americans to Seize upon Breed’s Hill — Putnam’s Opinion of It — Sanctioned by Prescott — Nocturnal March of the Detachment — Fortifying of Bunker’s Hill — Break of Day, and Astonishment of the Enemy

While Congress debates, the military situation around Boston escalates. More British troops arrive under Generals Howe, Burgoyne, and Clinton — the latter two famously declaring that they would “soon show the Yankees what they were about.” Gage issues a proclamation threatening to treat captured rebels as traitors. The American army, camped around Boston, is a motley collection of militia regiments, poorly armed, badly supplied, and lacking any common organization. The British officers treat them with open scorn. The Americans resolve to seize the heights overlooking Boston — the Charlestown peninsula. The plan is to fortify Bunker’s Hill, but Colonel Prescott, on the night of June 16-17, leads his detachment to Breed’s Hill instead, closer to the British positions. Putnam, consulted, expresses doubts about the exposed position but defers. The men work through the night, digging redoubts and entrenchments under the silent stars. At daybreak, the British look out from Boston to see a formidable fortification raised overnight where there had been only an empty hill. General Howe exclaims in astonishment: the Americans have done in one night what the best engineers in Europe would have thought possible only in a fortnight.

Chapter 41 — Battle of Bunker’s Hill

The Battle of Bunker Hill (June 17, 1775) is described in full detail. The British, under General Howe, land at Charlestown and form for battle on the beach. The American position is held by Prescott, Putnam, Stark, and their men — farmers and mechanics who have never faced regulars in pitched battle. The British advance in perfect order, their bright uniforms making them targets against the green hillside. The Americans are ordered to hold their fire until they can see the whites of the enemy’s eyes. When they fire, the front ranks of the British are shattered. The troops waver, fall back, and re-form for a second advance. Again the deadly volleys tear through them. It is not until the third assault, when the American powder runs out, that the British carry the redoubt by bayonet. The Americans retreat, fighting as they go. The British “victory” is a Pyrrhic one: over a thousand casualties, including a large proportion of officers. The battle proves to the colonies and the world that American militia can stand against British regulars. It also proves to Washington that he will need a disciplined army — raw courage alone will not win a war.

Chapter 42 — Departure from Philadelphia — Anecdotes of General Schuyler — Of Lee — Tidings of Bunker’s Hill — Military Councils — Population of New York — The Johnson Family — Governor Tryon — Arrival at New York — Military Instructions to Schuyler — Arrival at the Camp

Washington departs Philadelphia on June 21, 1775, traveling north to take command of the army. He is accompanied by Generals Schuyler and Lee, and his aides. Irving relates anecdotes of the characters: Schuyler, a New York patrician of Dutch descent, wealthy, cultivated, and a capable military organizer; Lee, the eccentric English soldier of fortune whose caustic wit and peculiar habits provide a constant counterpoint. News of Bunker Hill reaches Washington in New York, where he receives the first official tidings. He holds military councils, assessing the strategic situation. New York’s population is deeply divided between patriots and loyalists; the powerful Johnson family (Sir William Johnson’s heirs) commands the allegiance of the Mohawk Valley. Governor Tryon, though nominally still in office, has lost the confidence of the patriots and is living aboard a British ship in the harbor. Washington issues military instructions to Schuyler for the defense of New York and the projected invasion of Canada. Finally, he arrives in Cambridge, Massachusetts, on July 2, 1775. The following day, under the great elm tree at Cambridge Common, he formally takes command of the Continental Army.

Chapter 43 — Washington Takes Command of the Armies — Sketch of General Lee — Characters of the British Commanders, Howe, Clinton, and Burgoyne — Survey of the Camps from Prospect Hill — The Camps Contrasted — Description of the Revolutionary Army — Rhode Island Troops — Character of General Greene — Washington Represents the Deficiencies of the Army — His Apology for the Massachusetts Troops — Governor Trumbull — Craigie House, Washington’s Head-Quarters

Washington takes command of the heterogeneous army assembled around Boston. Irving offers a sketch of General Lee, whose intellectual gifts and military experience are balanced by his erratic personality and overweening ambition. The British commanders are characterized: Howe, brave and competent but indolent; Clinton, able but cold and touchy; Burgoyne, brilliant, theatrical, and inclined to underestimate his foes. Washington surveys the American camps from Prospect Hill, taking stock of his army. The contrast between the two sides is stark: the British, well-fed, well-clothed, well-disciplined, secure in fortified Boston; the Americans, ragged, half-starved, poorly armed, but burning with the spirit of liberty. Irving describes the Revolutionary Army in detail — regiments from Massachusetts, Connecticut, New Hampshire, and Rhode Island, each with its own character. Among them, the Rhode Island troops under the command of General Nathanael Greene stand out for their discipline. Greene, a young Quaker-born ironmonger who has educated himself in military science, will become Washington’s most trusted general. Washington prepares a comprehensive report to Congress, representing the deficiencies of the army with unflinching honesty — lack of powder, arms, tents, clothing, and organization. He offers an apology for the Massachusetts troops, explaining that their lack of discipline stems from their democratic habits and their belief in independence, not from any defect of courage. He corresponds with Governor Trumbull of Connecticut, one of the most reliable supporters of the Revolution. Washington establishes his headquarters at the Craigie House (the Vassall House) in Cambridge, a fine mansion that will serve as the nerve center of the Continental Army.

Chapter 44 — Questions of Military Rank — Popularity of Putnam — Arrangements at Head-Quarters — Colonel Mifflin and John Trumbull, Aides-de-Camp — Joseph Reed, Washington’s Secretary and Confidential Friend — Gates as Adjutant-General — Hazardous Situation of the Army — Strengthening of the Defences — Efficiency of Putnam — Rapid Changes — New Distribution of the Forces — Rigid Discipline — Lee and his Cane — His Idea as to Strong Battalions — Arrival of Rifle Companies — Daniel Morgan and his Sharp-Shooters — Washington Declines to Detach Troops to Distant Points for their Protection — His Reasons for so Doing

One of Washington’s earliest challenges is the question of military rank: the officers of the various New England regiments have different commissions, and precedence disputes erupt immediately. Putnam, the blunt, beloved Connecticut general, is immensely popular with the troops and the public. Washington organizes his headquarters, appointing Colonel Thomas Mifflin as one of his aides-de-camp, along with John Trumbull (the future painter of the Revolution). He takes Joseph Reed, a Philadelphia lawyer, as his secretary and confidential friend — a relationship that will prove crucial. Gates is installed as adjutant-general, bringing order to the army’s administration. The situation of the army is hazardous: the powder supply is desperately short; if the British sallied in force, the siege might collapse. Washington sets about strengthening the defences, extending the entrenchments, building redoubts, and connecting the various camps. Putnam’s energy and efficiency are invaluable in overseeing this work. A new distribution of the forces is made, creating three divisions under Ward, Lee, and Putnam. Washington insists on rigid discipline — orders are issued against swearing, drunkenness, and plundering. Lee, who has a habit of carrying a cane and delivering oracular pronouncements, confides his strategic philosophy: “Strong battalions are the best negotiators — strong battalions, and nothing else.” Rifle companies from Virginia, Maryland, and Pennsylvania arrive — among them Daniel Morgan’s company of Virginia sharp-shooters, expert marksmen in their distinctive hunting shirts who prove invaluable as skirmishers and snipers. Washington resists calls to detach troops to protect distant points, explaining his strategic rationale: the army must remain concentrated; dispersed, it would be destroyed in detail.

Chapter 45 — Washington’s Object in Distressing Boston — Scarcity and Sickness in the Town — A Startling Discovery — Scarcity of Powder in the Camp — Its Perilous Situation — Economy of Ammunition — Correspondence between Lee and Burgoyne — Correspondence between Washington and Gage — The Dignity of the Patriot Army Asserted

Washington’s strategic object is to distress the British in Boston — to cut off supplies, harass foraging parties, and make the occupation so costly and uncomfortable that Howe will be forced to evacuate the town. The British, cooped up in Boston, suffer from scarcity of fresh provisions and from sickness — smallpox and dysentery are rife. A startling discovery is made in the American camp: the army has barely thirty-six rounds of powder per man, and the total supply in the entire siege lines amounts to only about ninety-three barrels — enough for a few hours of fighting. Washington’s perilous situation is revealed starkly: if the British knew, they could march out and destroy the American army. He takes desperate measures, enforcing strict economy of ammunition — no firing without orders, no wasted volleys. The gunners are forbidden to fire at British sentries, tempting though the targets may be. A correspondence ensues between General Lee and General Burgoyne — a witty, literary exchange, with Burgoyne condescending and Lee defiant. More weightily, Washington and Gage exchange letters on the treatment of prisoners, with Washington asserting the dignity of the patriot army and protesting the British threat to treat captured Americans as traitors to be hanged. The correspondence establishes Washington’s tone: firm, dignified, unyielding on points of principle.

Chapter 46 — Dangers in the Interior — Machinations of the Johnson Family — Rivalry of Ethan Allen and Benedict Arnold — Government Perplexities about the Ticonderoga Capture — Measures to Secure the Prize — Allen and Arnold Ambitious of Further Laurels — Projects for the Invasion of Canada — Ethan Allen and Seth Warner Honored by Congress — Arnold Displaced by a Committee of Inquiry — His Indignation — News from Canada — The Revolution to be Extended into that Province — Enlistment of Green Mountain Boys — Schuyler at Ticonderoga — State of Affairs There — Election for Officers of the Green Mountain Boys — Ethan Allen Dismounted Joins the Army as a Volunteer — Preparations for the Invasion of Canada — General Montgomery — Indian Chiefs at Cambridge — Council Fire — Plan for an Expedition against Quebec — Departure of Troops from Ticonderoga — Arrival at Isle aux Noix

While the siege of Boston continues, dangers brew in the interior. The Johnson family in the Mohawk Valley — Sir John Johnson and his Loyalist followers — threaten to raise the Indians against the Revolution. The rivalry between Ethan Allen and Benedict Arnold over the laurels of Ticonderoga creates friction with the Continental Congress, which is perplexed about how to handle the capture. Congress eventually honors Allen and his lieutenant Seth Warner, recognizing the Green Mountain Boys as a separate regiment. Arnold, displaced by a committee of inquiry, is indignant — his pride stung, his ambition burning. News from Canada suggests that the province might be ripe for revolution. Congress authorizes an invasion of Canada: the Revolution is to be extended into that province. Schuyler is placed in command of the northern department and begins preparations at Ticonderoga. The Green Mountain Boys are enlisted; an election of officers confirms Warner as colonel and leaves Allen dismounted — forced to join as a volunteer, eager to redeem his reputation. General Richard Montgomery, an Irish-born former British officer who has married into the Livingstons of New York, is appointed second-in-command. Indian chiefs come to Cambridge, where a council fire is held, and Washington negotiates for their neutrality or alliance. A bold plan is hatched for a two-pronged invasion: Montgomery will advance by Lake Champlain against Montreal, while a second column, led by Benedict Arnold, will strike through the Maine wilderness directly at Quebec. Troops depart from Ticonderoga and arrive at Isle aux Noix, the assembly point on the Richelieu River.

Chapter 47 — A Challenge Declined — A Blow Meditated — A Cautious Council of War — Preparations for the Quebec Expedition — Benedict Arnold the Leader — Advice and Instructions — Departure — General Schuyler on the Sorel — Reconnoitres St. Johns — Camp at Isle aux Noix — Illness of Schuyler — Returns to Ticonderoga — Expedition of Montgomery against St. Johns — Letter of Ethan Allen — His Dash against Montreal — Its Catastrophe — A Hero in Irons — Correspondence of Washington with Schuyler and Arnold — His Anxiety about Them

Washington declines a personal challenge (likely from a British officer), refusing to engage in affairs of honor while serving as commander-in-chief. But he meditates a blow against the British that will change the course of the war: the expedition against Quebec. A cautious council of war approves the plan. Benedict Arnold is chosen as the leader — the choice reflecting Washington’s recognition of Arnold’s energy, resourcefulness, and daring. Washington gives Arnold detailed advice and instructions, emphasizing the need for secrecy and speed. Arnold’s expedition departs from Cambridge in September 1775. Meanwhile, in the north, General Schuyler advances up the Sorel River, reconnoitres St. Johns, and establishes a camp at Isle aux Noix. Schuyler falls dangerously ill and is forced to return to Ticonderoga, leaving Montgomery in command. Montgomery lays siege to St. Johns, the key to the Montreal approach. Ethan Allen, still smarting from his demotion and eager for glory, decides on his own initiative to dash against Montreal. He attempts to capture the city with a small force of Canadians and Green Mountain Boys — but his attack is badly coordinated; he is surrounded, captured, and thrown into irons, to be sent to England for trial as a traitor. The loss of Allen is a blow to American morale. Washington’s correspondence with Schuyler and Arnold reveals his deep anxiety about both prongs of the Canadian campaign — the supply problems, the lateness of the season, the uncertain loyalties of the Canadian population.

Chapter 48 — British in Boston Send out Cruisers — Depredations of Captain Wallace along the Coast — Treason in the Camp — Arrest of Dr. Church — His Trial and Fate — Conflagration of Falmouth — Irritation throughout the Country — Fitting out of Vessels of War — Embarkation of General Gage for England — Committee from Congress — Conferences with Washington — Resolutions of Congress to Carry on the War — Return of Secretary Reed to Philadelphia

The British in Boston, though besieged, still have naval power. They send out cruisers to ravage the American coast. Captain Wallace, commanding the British armed schooner, terrorizes the towns of Narragansett Bay, plundering and burning. The crisis deepens with the discovery of treason in the American camp: Dr. Benjamin Church, the director-general of the army hospital and a trusted member of the Massachusetts Committee of Safety, is revealed to have been communicating with the British, conveying intelligence through a cipher. Church is arrested, tried, and found guilty; he is imprisoned, his fate a grim warning of the dangers of espionage. In October 1775, the British burn Falmouth (now Portland, Maine), a devastating conflagration that leaves hundreds homeless in the winter cold. The burning galvanizes American resistance and leads to the fitting out of armed vessels — the beginnings of the American navy. General Gage, his health broken by the strain of the siege, embarks for England. A committee from Congress arrives at Cambridge for conferences with Washington, assessing the state of the army and the prospects of the campaign. Congress passes resolutions committing the colonies to carry on the war — the Olive Branch Petition having been rejected by the king. Washington’s secretary Joseph Reed returns to Philadelphia for personal reasons, leaving a gap in Washington’s confidential circle.

Chapter 49 — Measures of General Howe — Desecration of Churches — Three Proclamations — Seizure of Tories — Want of Artillery — Henry Knox, the Artillerist — His Mission to Ticonderoga — Re-enlistment of Troops — Lack of Public Spirit — Comments of General Greene

General Howe, now commanding the British forces in Boston, takes defensive measures — fortifying the town, demolishing buildings to clear fields of fire, and, in civilian complaints, desecrating churches by using them as barracks and stables. He issues three proclamations: one offering pardon to all who will lay down arms and return to allegiance; one threatening the severest penalties against those who persist; and one forbidding trade with the rebels. Loyalists — “Tories” — are seized and imprisoned by the patriots; others flee Boston to the safety of British lines. The critical deficiency in the American camp is artillery — the siege cannot succeed without heavy guns to bombard the British positions. Henry Knox, a Boston bookseller turned artillerist, comes to Washington with a bold proposal: bring the cannon captured at Ticonderoga across the winter wilderness to Boston. Washington approves, and Knox departs on his epic mission. The re-enlistment of troops becomes a desperate struggle: the soldiers’ enlistments expire at the end of 1775, and the army faces the prospect of melting away. Washington appeals to Congress, to the state governments, to the men themselves — but the lack of public spirit, the preference for home and farm over camp and danger, depresses him. General Greene’s comments on these difficulties are recorded: he sees the want of public virtue as the greatest threat to the Revolution.

Chapter 50 — Affairs in Canada — Capture of Fort Chamblee — Siege of St. Johns — Maclean and his Highlanders — Montgomery on the Treatment of Ethan Allen — Repulse of Carleton — Capitulation of the Garrison of St. Johns — Generous Conduct of Montgomery — Maclean Re-embarks for Quebec — Weary Struggle of Arnold through the Wilderness — Defection of Colonel Enos — Arnold in the Valley of the Chaudiere — His Arrival opposite Quebec — Surrender of Montreal — Escape of Carleton — Home-Sickness of the American Troops

The Canadian campaign unfolds on two fronts. Montgomery, having taken command after Schuyler’s illness, captures Fort Chambly with its valuable stores of powder and supplies. He presses the siege of St. Johns, where the British garrison under Major Preston holds out stubbornly. Colonel Maclean and his Highlanders attempt to relieve the fort but are repulsed. Carleton himself leads a force of regulars, militia, and Indians from Montreal to break the siege — and is driven back. Montgomery writes to the captured Ethan Allen with a mixture of sympathy and criticism of his rashness. On November 3, the garrison of St. Johns finally capitulates; Montgomery demonstrates generous and chivalrous conduct toward the defeated. Maclean re-embarks for Quebec. Meanwhile, Arnold’s expedition suffers an ordeal that becomes legendary in American history. Eleven hundred men march into the Maine wilderness in September — by the time they reach the French settlements on the Chaudiere River in November, they are a ragged, starving remnant. They have been forced to eat dogs, shoes, cartridge boxes — anything. The boats proved inadequate, the rivers treacherous, the portages brutal. Supplies spoiled. One of Arnold’s officers, Colonel Enos, turns back with several hundred men — an act of apparent desertion that leaves the remaining force even more desperate. Yet Arnold pushes on, driving his men with fierce energy. They emerge into the valley of the Chaudiere, where French-Canadian peasants feed them. Arnold arrives opposite Quebec on November 9, his force reduced to about six hundred exhausted men. He makes a show of demanding the surrender of the city. Carleton, having narrowly escaped capture when Montreal fell, has arrived in Quebec just ahead of Arnold and prepares the defenses. Surrender of Montreal follows Montgomery’s advance, but Carleton’s escape is a stroke of bad luck for the Americans. The Canadian troops, many of whose enlistments have expired, are afflicted by home-sickness and disease.

Chapter 51 — Washington’s Anticipations of Success at Quebec — His Eulogium of Arnold — Schuyler and Montgomery Talk of Resigning — Expostulations of Washington — Their Effect — Schuyler’s Conduct to a Captive Foe

Washington, following the progress of the Canadian campaign from Cambridge, allows himself to anticipate success at Quebec. He writes a remarkable eulogium of Arnold, praising his “astonishing” march through the wilderness and his determination. But difficulties multiply. Schuyler, frustrated by constant illness, disputes with Congress, and the immense difficulties of the northern command, talks of resigning. Montgomery, too, writes of his desire to resign after the Quebec campaign — he is sick of the service, disappointed by the lack of support from Congress, and disgusted by the indiscipline of the troops. Washington writes urgent, expostulatory letters to both men, begging them to remain at their posts. He appeals to their patriotism, their honor, and their sense of duty — the Revolution cannot succeed without men of their experience and character. His letters have their effect on Montgomery, who promises to see the Quebec campaign through. Schuyler’s conduct to a captured foe — a British officer of the 26th Regiment, Colonel —— — is recounted as an example of the humanity and gentlemanly conduct that Washington has insisted upon. The American treatment of prisoners stands in contrast to British threats to treat them as traitors.

Chapter 52 — Difficulties in Filling up the Army — The Connecticut Troops Persist in Going Home — Their Reception There — Timely Arrival of Spoils in the Camp — Putnam and the Prize Mortar — A Maraud by Americans — Rebuked by Washington — Correspondence of Washington with General Howe about the Treatment of Ethan Allen — Fraternal Zeal of Levi Allen — Treatment of General Prescott — Preparations to Bombard Boston — Battery at Lechmere’s Point — Prayer of Putnam for Powder

The most acute crisis of the siege: filling the army as enlistments expire. The Connecticut troops, their term of service up, persist in going home despite Washington’s desperate appeals. Irving describes their reception in their home towns — initially greeted as heroes, but the novelty soon wears off, and many will re-enlist after a taste of civilian life. A timely arrival of spoils — captured British supply ships — brings much-needed arms, powder, and clothing to the camp. Putnam, ever the practical soldier, captures a valuable brass mortar (the “Prize Mortar”) from a British vessel and presents it to the army. But a maraud by American soldiers, plundering the property of a loyalist, draws Washington’s stern rebuke, demonstrating his determination to maintain discipline even among men he respects. Washington and Howe exchange correspondence on the treatment of Ethan Allen, who has been sent to England in chains. Washington demands that Allen be treated as a prisoner of war rather than a traitor; Howe’s evasive reply underscores the dangerous legal limbo in which captured Americans find themselves. Levi Allen, Ethan’s brother, displays fraternal zeal in trying to secure better treatment for the captive. The treatment of General Prescott, a British prisoner, becomes another point of contention: the Americans treat him well, hoping for reciprocal treatment of their own captured officers. Washington prepares actively to bombard Boston. A battery is constructed at Lechmere’s Point, jutting into the bay within range of the town. But all preparations depend on powder — and Putnam’s prayer for powder, echoed by the entire army, remains unanswered as winter closes in.

Chapter 53 — Mount Vernon in Danger — Mrs. Washington Invited to the Camp — Lund Washington, the General’s Agent — Terms on which he Serves — Instructed to Keep up the Hospitality of the House — Journey of Mrs. Washington to Camp — Her Equipage and Liveries — Arrival at Camp — Domestic Affairs at Head-Quarters — Gayeties in Camp — A Brawl between Round Jackets and Rifle Shirts

Even as Washington commands the Continental Army, his heart remains at Mount Vernon. The estate is in danger — British cruisers on the Potomac could threaten the mansion. Washington invites Mrs. Washington to join him at Cambridge for the winter. He leaves the management of Mount Vernon in the hands of his kinsman Lund Washington, who serves as his agent on carefully defined terms (including a share of the profits). Washington instructs Lund to keep up the hospitality of the house — Mount Vernon must not appear to be deserted — and gives detailed directions about the plantation, the slaves, and the accounts. Mrs. Washington undertakes the journey to Cambridge with considerable equipage — her coach-and-four, her liveried servants — a reminder of the Virginia aristocracy from which she comes. She arrives in camp and establishes domestic affairs at headquarters. Her presence brings a touch of domesticity and elegance to the Craigie House. Despite the gravity of the situation, there are gayeties in camp — dinners, dances, and social gatherings among the officers and their families. A brawl breaks out between the “Round Jackets” (Virginia riflemen in their hunting shirts) and the “Rifle Shirts” (other frontier companies) — a clash of regional pride that Washington handles with firm discipline. The chapter provides a rare glimpse of Washington’s domestic character amid the hardships of war.

Chapter 54 — Affairs in Canada — Arnold at Point Levi — Quebec Re-enforced — Crossing of the St. Lawrence — Landing in Wolfe’s Cove — Arnold on the Heights of Abraham — Cautious Counsel — Quebec Aroused — The Invaders Baffled — Withdraw to Point aux Trembles — Booming of Cannon — Carleton at Quebec — Letter of Washington to Arnold

The volume closes at the end of 1775 with the American forces before Quebec, the siege of Boston ongoing, and the outcome of the Revolution still deeply uncertain.
