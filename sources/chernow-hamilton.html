Description

Ron Chernow’s comprehensive biography of Alexander Hamilton (c. 1755–1804), the first U.S. Secretary of the Treasury and one of the most influential Founding Fathers. Drawing extensively on Hamilton’s personal papers, letters, and public writings — including previously unexamined dispatches to the Royal Danish American Gazette from his King’s College days and his rediscovered “Monitor” essays in John Holt’s New-York Journal — Chernow traces Hamilton’s improbable rise from an impoverished orphan in the Caribbean to a central figure in the founding of the American republic. The biography is the product of years of archival research, including travel to Nevis, St. Croix, St. Vincent, and Scotland to uncover Hamilton’s obscure origins. Chernow consulted two of the world’s top geneticists regarding surviving locks of Hamilton’s hair and participated in the Hamilton DNA Project to investigate questions of paternity and possible African ancestry. The biography gives detailed attention to Hamilton’s role in the Revolution (as Washington’s aide-de-camp), his contributions to The Federalist Papers, his creation of the American financial system (national bank, assumption of state debts, the mint), his conflicts with Jefferson, Madison, and Adams, the Reynolds affair and other personal scandals, and his fatal duel with Aaron Burr in 1804. Included are extensive notes, a bibliography, and an index.

This is a modern work of synthesis (2004), not a period or near-contemporary source. It is included in this wiki because of its comprehensive coverage and use of primary source material discovered or made accessible after the period, including Hamilton’s undergraduate essays, his pay-book reading notes from Valley Forge, and correspondence from the Schuyler and Hamilton family collections at the Library of Congress and the New-York Historical Society.

Prologue: The Oldest Revolutionary War Widow

In her ninety-seventh year, Elizabeth Schuyler Hamilton still spoke of her husband in the present tense, a woman who had spent more than half a century as the keeper of Alexander Hamilton’s flame. She lived surrounded by relics: a marble bust by Giuseppe Ceracchi before which she placed fresh flowers daily, his love letters and sonnets, the tiny cloth bag around her neck containing fragments of the farewell note he had written on the eve of his duel with Aaron Burr, and a silver wine cooler from George Washington that Washington had given the Hamiltons during the Reynolds affair scandal as a tacit gesture of solidarity. After Hamilton died on July 12, 1804, she wore mourning clothes for the remaining fifty years of her life, never remarrying, dedicating herself entirely to preserving his legacy. She spent her final years in a house on H Street in Washington, D.C., near the White House, where she sat by the window knitting and arranging flowers, a figure few passersby recognized as the last surviving link to the glory days of the early republic. One visitor recalled that “when the game was done, she leaned back in her chair a long time with closed eyes, as if lost to all around her. There was a long silence, broken by the murmured words, ‘I am so tired. It is so long. I want to see Hamilton.‘”

Preserving her husband’s historical reputation was Eliza’s holy quest. She enlisted as many as thirty assistants to sift through his papers and spent decades trying to commission an authorized biography that would secure Hamilton’s place in the pantheon of founders — a task that fell ultimately to her fourth son, John Church Hamilton, who produced a seven-volume hagiography. The polarized verdicts on Hamilton’s character have persisted for two centuries: Thomas Jefferson called him the “American Mephistopheles,” the proponent of such devilish contrivances as banks, factories, and stock exchanges; Noah Webster contended Hamilton’s “ambition, pride, and overbearing temper” had destined him “to be the evil genius of this country”; Lord Bryce singled out Hamilton as the one founding father who had not received his due from posterity. Theodore Roosevelt declared him “the most brilliant American statesman who ever lived.”

Hamilton was the supreme double threat among the founders — at once thinker and doer, sparkling theoretician and masterful executive. The saga of his metamorphosis from an anguished clerk on St. Croix to the reigning presence in Washington’s cabinet offers both a gripping personal story and a panoramic view of the formative years of the republic. If Jefferson provided the essential poetry of American political discourse, Hamilton established the prose of American statecraft. The magnitude of his feats as treasury secretary has overshadowed many other facets of his life: clerk, college student, youthful poet, essayist, artillery captain, wartime adjutant to Washington, battlefield hero, congressman, abolitionist, Bank of New York founder, state assemblyman, Constitutional Convention delegate, orator, lawyer, polemicist, educator, foreign-policy theorist, and major general in the army.

1. The Castaways

Alexander Hamilton claimed Nevis in the British West Indies as his birthplace, although no surviving records substantiate this. The tiny island in the Leeward Islands was the crossroads of a bitter maritime rivalry among European powers vying for mastery of the lucrative sugar trade — a small revolution in consumer tastes that had turned the Caribbean into prized acreage for growing sugarcane. The West Indian islands generated more wealth for Britain than all of her North American colonies combined, and the sudden popularity of sugar, dubbed “white gold,” engendered a brutal world of overnight fortunes in which slavery proved indispensable. While other founding fathers were reared in tidy New England villages or cosseted on baronial Virginia estates, Hamilton grew up in a tropical hellhole of dissipated whites and fractious slaves, all framed by a backdrop of luxuriant natural beauty.

On both his maternal and paternal sides, Hamilton’s family clung to the insecure middle rung of West Indian life. His mother, Rachel Faucette, was the daughter of a French Huguenot physician who owned a small sugar plantation on Nevis. At sixteen, Rachel was married off to a Danish fortune-hunter named Johann Michael Lavien — a “hated marriage,” in Hamilton’s estimation — and gave birth to a son, Peter. When the marriage deteriorated, Rachel simply abandoned the house, and Lavien, seizing on a Danish law that allowed a husband to jail his wife if she was twice found guilty of adultery, had her clapped into Fort Christiansvaern, the dreaded Christiansted fort that did double duty as the town jail. No other woman was ever imprisoned there for adultery. Rachel spent several months in a dank, cramped cell measuring ten by thirteen feet, staring out at the blue-green water through a small, deeply inset window. Upon her release, her spirit unbroken, she fled the island, abandoning both Lavien and her sole son, Peter, and in doing so inadvertently doomed her unborn son Alexander to illegitimacy.

Hamilton’s father, James Hamilton, was the fourth of eleven children of the laird of Grange in Ayrshire, Scotland — a younger son with little chance of inheriting the title. He had been “bred to trade” and went to St. Kitts as a merchant, where “from too generous and too easy a temper he failed in business and at length fell into indigent circumstances.” James and Rachel met in St. Kitts, two scarred survivors united by early setbacks and vertiginous descent in social standing. Because Danish law prohibited Rachel from remarrying after her divorce, the couple lived as common-law spouses, presenting themselves as James and Rachel Hamilton. They had two sons: James, Jr., and Alexander, born on January 11, 1755. Alexander spoke of his father in a forgiving tone tinged with pity — “his character was otherwise without reproach and his manners those of a gentleman” — but he inherited his father’s pride without his indolence, and his exceptional capacity for work was its own commentary on his father’s failings.

When Alexander was about ten, James Hamilton deserted the family entirely, never to reappear. Alexander probably never again laid eyes on his vagabond father, who stayed in the Caribbean, ground down by poverty. A persistent mystery surrounds Hamilton’s paternity: the uncanny physical resemblance between Alexander and Edward Stevens, a close friend from St. Croix, led many contemporaries — including Timothy Pickering — to conclude that Hamilton was the illegitimate son of Thomas Stevens, a wealthy merchant, rather than of James Hamilton. This would explain why Stevens sheltered Hamilton after Rachel’s death but made no such gesture toward Alexander’s brother, James, Jr., and why Hamilton formed a more enduring bond with Edward Stevens than with his own brother. After Rachel died of a raging fever in February 1768, with Alexander, age thirteen, lying delirious inches from her when she expired, the two Hamilton boys were left orphaned, penniless, and plagued by a cascading series of disasters — their cousin and supposed protector committed bloody suicide, their uncle died, and the probate court ultimately disinherited them, awarding their mother’s entire estate to Peter Lavien, the half-brother they had never known. Hamilton was left “thrown upon the bounty of my mother’s relations.”

2. Hurricane

Even in the languorous tropics, Hamilton, while clerking at the mercantile house of Beekman and Cruger on St. Croix, was schooled in a fast-paced modern world of trading ships and fluctuating markets. The firm dealt in every conceivable commodity required by planters — timber, bread, flour, rice, pork, fish, iron, mules, and cattle — and Hamilton learned to handle money, chart courses for ships, and compute prices in an exotic blend of Portuguese coins, Spanish pieces of eight, British pounds, Danish ducats, and Dutch stivers. He later adverted to this period “as the most useful part of his education.” On this neutral Danish island, his fluency in French became a critical business asset for trade with the French West Indies.

On August 31, 1772, a devastating hurricane swept across St. Croix, leveling buildings, uprooting trees, and leaving a trail of destruction. In an extraordinary display of precocious literary talent, the seventeen-year-old Hamilton composed a letter describing the storm that was published in the Royal Danish American Gazette. He wrote of “fiery meteors flying about in the air — prodigious glare of almost perpetual lightning” and described the hurricane as “the forerunner of the day of judgment.” The letter was so vivid and eloquent that local leaders were stunned that a teenage clerk could have written it. A group of prominent islanders, led by the Presbyterian minister Hugh Knox, raised a subscription fund to send Hamilton to North America for a proper education. The fund’s subscribers included Nicholas Cruger and Thomas Stevens, and in October 1772, Hamilton sailed for Boston, leaving behind the squalid world of his West Indian boyhood.

Before departing, Hamilton had also published poems in the St. Croix press including “The Melancholy Hour” and “The Soul Ascending into Bliss,” and he had written to his friend Edward Stevens with a startling confession: “I contemn the grovelling and condition of a clerk or the like, to which my fortune condemns me, and would willingly risk my life, though not my character, to exalt my station. I am no philosopher, you know; but I mean to be a politician.” Most tellingly, the same letter expressed a wish for war: “I wish there was a war.” The letter captures the restless ambition and martial longings of a young man desperate to escape his origins, and it is one of the few surviving documents that give us a direct glimpse into the private thoughts of the adolescent Hamilton. His father, James Hamilton, Sr., was still alive somewhere on the island of Bequia, but his son never sought him out.

The hurricane letter was also significant because it established Hamilton’s literary reputation before he had ever set foot in North America. Editors and clergymen who read the piece recognized that St. Croix was too small to contain the young man’s talents. Hugh Knox, a Scottish-born Presbyterian minister who had studied at the College of New Jersey (Princeton), became Hamilton’s mentor and arranged for the subscription fund that paid for his passage and education. Knox had been a tutor in the West Indies for fifteen years and had seen many bright young men, but none with Hamilton’s combination of raw intelligence, ambition, and literary power. The subscription was a remarkable act of collective generosity: it reflected the islanders’ recognition that Hamilton possessed talents that deserved a larger stage. In October 1772, Hamilton boarded a ship bound for Boston, leaving the Caribbean forever. He would never again see his mother’s grave, his father on Bequia, or his brother James, who remained behind as a carpenter’s apprentice.

3. The Collegian

Arriving in Boston, and then New York, Hamilton was taken under the wing of Hercules Mulligan, a genial Irish-born tailor who introduced him to the city’s social and political life. He prepared for college at the Elizabethtown Academy in New Jersey under Francis Barber, a future Revolutionary War colonel, where he was an eager if somewhat fragile student, often driving himself to the point of illness. In 1774, he entered King’s College (later Columbia University) in New York, then situated in lower Manhattan with a bucolic Hudson River view. He threw himself into a rigorous course of study that encompassed the classics, mathematics, and modern philosophy, devouring the works of Hume, Hobbes, Montesquieu, Blackstone, and Coke. He also joined a college debating society, where Nicholas Fish later recalled that “his eloquence was of that commanding kind which carried conviction.”

As the imperial crisis with Britain deepened, Hamilton began writing political essays that brought him to public attention. Late in 1774, he published two anonymous pamphlets, A Full Vindication of the Measures of Congress and The Farmer Refuted, responding to the Tory pamphleteer Samuel Seabury, who had written under the pseudonym “A Westchester Farmer.” In combative, closely reasoned prose, Hamilton refuted Seabury’s arguments for conciliation with Britain and defended the actions of the Continental Congress. The pamphlets were so sophisticated that many readers assumed they had been written by John Jay or other older, established figures. Chernow notes that these essays were the work of a young man who had been in America barely two years and who had never formally studied law, yet they already exhibited the powerful dialectical style and mastery of argument that would characterize his later career.

Hamilton also contributed a series of fourteen essays called the “Monitor” in John Holt’s New-York Journal, and when a patriotic mob gathered at King’s College to tar and feather the Tory president Myles Cooper, Hamilton famously mounted the steps to address the crowd and bought Cooper enough time to escape out the back, saving his life. In another bold act, Hamilton led a group of students to the Battery and helped to drag away the cannon from the British warship HMS Asia, narrowly escaping a broadside that killed a nearby onlooker. These early exploits demonstrated the audacity, physical courage, and political sophistication that would mark his entire career.

4. The Pen and the Sword

With the outbreak of war, Hamilton joined the Hearts of Oak, a volunteer militia company that drilled in graveyards and St. George’s Chapel yard. The unit was eventually folded into the New York Provincial Company of Artillery, and Hamilton, through the influence of Alexander McDougall and John Jay, was commissioned as its captain. He outfitted his men in blue coats with bright brass buttons and was obsessive about drill, pay, and discipline — his pay book from this period survives, filled with his neat handwriting recording everything from troop movements to reading notes. Hamilton’s artillery company covered Washington’s retreat across New Jersey in the dark autumn of 1776, one of the few units to maintain its composure during the disastrous campaign that saw the Continental Army melting away.

At the Battle of Harlem Heights, Hamilton came to Washington’s attention for the first time. When the young captain’s artillery performed with cool precision under fire, Washington invited him to his tent for a conference. At White Plains, Hamilton’s battery held the line at Chatterton’s Hill, and General Nathanael Greene noticed the small, fierce young captain directing his guns with extraordinary skill. Hamilton’s most harrowing experience with the artillery came during the retreat across New Jersey, when an exploding cannon killed six of his men in a single blast, leaving Hamilton himself temporarily stunned and deafened. The incident was a brutal lesson in the realities of war and marked the beginning of Hamilton’s lifelong concern for the welfare of common soldiers.

It was during the long, grim winter of 1776-77 that Hamilton’s relationship with Washington deepened. Washington had been impressed by Hamilton’s intelligence, his command of languages (particularly French, which was essential for communicating with the French allies), and his executive ability. In February 1777, Washington offered Hamilton the position of aide-de-camp with the rank of lieutenant colonel. Hamilton accepted, and for the next four years he served as Washington’s chief staff officer, drafting his correspondence, managing his headquarters, and acting as his confidential emissary. It was a position of immense trust and responsibility that gave Hamilton an intimate view of the highest levels of military and political decision-making, but it also placed him under the constant, exacting scrutiny of the famously reserved commander-in-chief.

The aide-de-camp role placed Hamilton at the center of everything. He was present at Washington’s councils of war, wrote the dispatches that kept Congress informed of the army’s situation, and conducted covert correspondence with spies and agents. He also handled the endless administrative detail that kept the army functioning — requisitioning supplies, managing the movement of troops, and dealing with the constant importunities of officers seeking promotion. It was a role that required not just intelligence but immense stamina: Hamilton worked eighteen-hour days, often seven days a week, and was expected to be available at any hour. His health, never robust, suffered under the strain.

5. The Little Lion

As Washington’s aide-de-camp, Hamilton quickly became indispensable. He wrote thousands of letters for Washington, ranging from the most routine administrative orders to some of the most sensitive diplomatic and military communications of the Revolution. Chernow notes that Hamilton was Washington’s “pen and sword,” the man who translated the general’s often inchoate thoughts into polished, forceful prose. He also served as a liaison to the French allies, using his fluent French to communicate with the marquis de Lafayette, the comte de Rochambeau, and other French officers. Lafayette became a particularly close friend, and the two young men — neither yet twenty-five — formed a bond that would last throughout their lives.

Hamilton’s tenure on Washington’s staff was not without friction. The young colonel was fiercely proud and ambitious, and he increasingly chafed at the subordinate role of an aide. He wanted a field command and resented being trapped in a clerical role while others won military glory. In February 1781, Hamilton and Washington had a violent quarrel on the steps of Washington’s headquarters in New Windsor, New York, after Hamilton, kept waiting for a meeting, walked off in a huff. Washington sent his secretary, Tench Tilghman, to fetch him back, but Hamilton refused. It was the first of several tempestuous breaks between the two men, and it revealed the fierce pride that lay beneath Hamilton’s polished exterior.

Despite this rupture, Washington ultimately granted Hamilton’s wish. In July 1781, Hamilton was given command of a light infantry battalion in Lafayette’s division. At the Siege of Yorktown in October 1781, Hamilton led his men in a daring night bayonet charge against Redoubt No. 10, one of the two critical British fortifications blocking the allied siege lines. Hamilton’s men stormed the redoubt with unloaded muskets, taking it in less than ten minutes. The victory at Yorktown effectively ended the war, and Hamilton had at last earned the battlefield glory he had craved.

The four years Hamilton spent on Washington’s staff shaped his political education in fundamental ways. He saw firsthand the catastrophic consequences of a weak central government that could not raise money, pay its soldiers, or coordinate the efforts of thirteen fractious states. He experienced the frustrations of military bureaucracy and the constant infighting among senior officers. He also learned the arts of political manipulation and diplomacy, watching Washington manage a fragile coalition of politicians, generals, and foreign allies with extraordinary skill. The experience gave Hamilton an education in statecraft that no university could have provided, and it forged a bond with Washington that would survive their many disagreements and form the basis of Hamilton’s later political career.

6. A Frenzy of Valor

The Yorktown campaign was the climax of Hamilton’s military career, but the war’s final years saw him engaged in numerous other exploits. Chernow details Hamilton’s diplomatic mission to General Horatio Gates in 1780, when Washington sent him to the arrogant and insubordinate “Hero of Saratoga” to demand the return of troops Gates had detached from Washington’s army. Hamilton, though junior in rank, handled this delicate and potentially explosive assignment with remarkable tact, securing at least partial compliance from Gates. During the same period, Hamilton was deeply involved in the tangled politics of the Continental Congress, where he served briefly as a delegate and where his frustration with the weak central government under the Articles of Confederation began to crystallize.

The war also brought Hamilton into close contact with the financial underside of the Revolution. During the Valley Forge winter of 1777-78, he witnessed firsthand the catastrophic consequences of congressional impotence: the army starved while states bickered over supply quotas. This experience left an indelible mark on his thinking and convinced him of the absolute necessity of a strong central government with independent taxing power. His pay book from the period is filled not just with military notes but with his own reading: excerpts from Blackstone, Montesquieu, Hume, and other political philosophers, as well as his own thoughts on government, economics, and human nature.

Hamilton’s tenure on Washington’s staff also drew him into the treacherous world of military politics. He was involved, on Washington’s behalf, in the intrigue that led to the exposure and court-martial of General Charles Lee after the Battle of Monmouth. Lee had conducted a disorderly retreat that nearly lost the battle, and Hamilton helped gather evidence and draft charges against him. He also participated in the inquiry into the treason of Benedict Arnold and the execution of Major John André, the British spy whose graceful bearing and tragic fate deeply moved Hamilton. In a letter to Elizabeth Schuyler (whom he was courting at the time), Hamilton expressed his “bitter anguish” over André’s sentence, even as he acknowledged its necessity. The André case was one of the first episodes in which Hamilton’s compassion for an individual conflicted with his sense of duty — a tension that would recur throughout his life.

Hamilton also found time during the war to develop his ideas about the future of the American republic. In September 1780, he wrote a remarkable letter to James Duane, a New York delegate to Congress, outlining in extraordinary detail the defects of the Articles of Confederation and proposing remedies that anticipated virtually every feature of the Constitution of 1787. He called for a convention to create “a solid, coercive union,” a national bank, federal control of commerce, and a standing army. The letter is one of the most important documents of the founding period, and it demonstrates that Hamilton had developed a comprehensive vision of American nationhood years before the Constitutional Convention.

7. The Lovesick Colonel

In the winter of 1780, while camped with Washington’s army near Morristown, New Jersey, Hamilton made a journey to Albany on business for the general. There, at the Schuyler mansion called the Pastures, he met Elizabeth Schuyler, the second daughter of General Philip Schuyler, one of the wealthiest and most powerful men in New York. Eliza was a slender, dark-eyed young woman of twenty-two with a lively intelligence and a warm, unpretentious nature that stood in sharp contrast to the brittle sophistication of her elder sister, Angelica. Hamilton was instantly smitten, and by the time he returned to Morristown, he was writing her passionate love letters.

The courtship was conducted partly through letters and partly in person, with Hamilton juggling his duties as Washington’s aide with his pursuit of the general’s daughter. He wrote to Eliza in a tone of almost comical adoration, assuring her that “you are the only object in the world upon which my heart ever placed itself.” He was, Chernow notes, a “lovesick colonel” in a way that seems almost out of character for the usually self-contained young officer. Philip Schuyler at first had reservations about the match — Hamilton was an obscure, illegitimate immigrant with no fortune — but Hamilton’s brilliance and his intimacy with Washington soon won the general over. Schuyler became one of Hamilton’s most steadfast supporters.

The marriage of Alexander Hamilton and Elizabeth Schuyler took place on December 14, 1780, at the Schuyler mansion in Albany. The ceremony was small and private, but it united Hamilton with one of the most powerful families in New York. Through his marriage, Hamilton acquired not only a devoted wife but a formidable political patron in Philip Schuyler. He also gained a sister-in-law in Angelica Church, the brilliantly witty and sophisticated eldest Schuyler daughter, who had married John Church, a wealthy British merchant. Angelica became Hamilton’s lifelong confidante and, Chernow suggests, the object of a rumored but never proven romantic attachment that added a further layer of emotional complexity to Hamilton’s life.

Eliza herself was no passive partner in the marriage. She was well educated, politically astute, and fiercely loyal to her husband. She managed the household finances, raised their eight children (often alone while Hamilton was away on public business), and acted as his secretary and copyist. She was present at many of the crucial moments of his career, including the night he produced the bank opinion, when she stayed up with him copying out the text. Throughout the scandals and controversies that marked Hamilton’s public life, Eliza remained his most steadfast defender. Her devotion was absolute, and it was reciprocated: Hamilton’s letters to her are filled with tenderness and affection, even when he was betraying her trust with Maria Reynolds.

8. Glory

The Yorktown campaign of 1781 was Hamilton’s moment of military glory. Having finally wrangled a field command from Washington after their explosive quarrel, he led his light infantry battalion with a ferocity that won praise even from the famously undemonstrative Washington. The night assault on Redoubt No. 10 was a textbook operation: Hamilton’s men advanced with unloaded muskets and fixed bayonets, taking the fortification in a matter of minutes with minimal casualties. Lafayette later wrote that Hamilton “had so long and so ardently desired an occasion to distinguish himself that he would have been inconsolable if he had missed it.”

The aftermath of Yorktown, however, brought Hamilton face to face with the continuing fragility of the American cause. The Continental Army was still unpaid and mutinous; Congress was paralyzed; and the treasury was empty. Hamilton returned to his duties as a delegate to the Continental Congress, where he began to develop the political and economic philosophy that would define his later career. He submitted a remarkable letter to Robert Morris, the superintendent of finance, urging the creation of a national bank modeled on the Bank of England, a funded debt, and a system of federal taxation — all ideas that were far ahead of their time and that would form the basis of his later program as treasury secretary.

Yorktown also marked the end of Hamilton’s active military service. He resigned his commission at the end of 1781 and began to prepare for a civilian career. But the war had left an indelible mark on him. He had seen the army starve at Valley Forge, he had witnessed the incompetence of state militias and the bickering of state politicians, and he had learned firsthand the consequences of weak central authority. The experience would shape everything he did in the years to come. Hamilton never romanticized war — he knew it to be brutal and wasteful — but he also knew that a nation that could not defend itself could not survive, and that a government that could not command the resources to maintain an army was no government at all.

Hamilton’s brief military career also gave him something he had never had before: a sense of belonging. As a young immigrant, he had been an outsider in American society. The war gave him a cause, a country, and a set of comrades who would remain his friends for life. It also gave him the patronage of Washington, the most important political asset any young American could have had. The Revolution transformed Hamilton from a rootless West Indian orphan into an American patriot, and it gave him the platform from which he would help to shape the nation’s future.

In 1782, Hamilton opened a law practice in Albany, having read for the law after leaving the army. He was admitted to the bar after an accelerated course of study, and his legal career took off immediately. But he found himself increasingly drawn back into politics as the weakness of the Confederation government became ever more apparent. In the spring of 1783, he drafted a series of resolutions for Congress calling for a constitutional convention to strengthen the federal government, and he engaged in a tense correspondence with Washington about the dangers of the army’s unrest. His 1780 letter to James Duane had already sketched the outlines of a new government; now he began to work actively toward that goal.

The law practice itself was a revelation. Hamilton discovered that he had a natural talent for legal argument — the same dialectical skills that had made him a formidable polemicist served him equally well in the courtroom. His practice grew rapidly, and he soon counted many of the most prominent merchants and landowners in New York among his clients. He also began to develop the legal theories that would later inform his constitutional arguments, particularly his belief in the supremacy of national law over state statutes and his conviction that the judiciary must have the power to declare laws unconstitutional.

9. Raging Billows

The postwar period saw Hamilton grappling with the massive problems that beset the Confederation. The national government was bankrupt, the states were feuding over trade and boundaries, and the army was in a state of near-mutiny over unpaid wages. Hamilton, as a member of the Continental Congress in 1782-83, was at the center of these crises. He supported the proposal to give the army five years of full pay (commutation) as a way of settling the soldiers’ claims, but he found himself caught between the army’s justifiable anger and Congress’s empty treasury.

It was in this context that the Newburgh Conspiracy unfolded — a shadowy episode in which a group of officers, frustrated by Congress’s failure to pay them, flirted with the idea of forcing the issue by threatening military action. Hamilton was aware of the officers’ grievances, and he may have had some role in encouraging them as a way of pressuring Congress to adopt the commutation and, more broadly, to strengthen the central government. But when Washington dramatically confronted the officers at Newburgh and quashed the conspiracy with a moving plea for patience, Hamilton quickly disavowed any connection with the plotters.

The Newburgh crisis was a turning point for Hamilton. It confirmed his belief that the republican experiment would fail if the central government could not command the resources necessary to meet its obligations. In 1783, he drafted a series of resolutions calling for a general convention to revise the Articles of Confederation. Though these resolutions failed, they marked the beginning of a campaign that would culminate at Annapolis in 1786 and Philadelphia in 1787. Meanwhile, he returned to his law practice in New York, becoming one of the city’s most successful attorneys, handling cases that ranged from commercial disputes to the pioneering Rutgers v. Waddington case, in which he argued for the supremacy of federal treaty law over state statutes — an early foreshadowing of his constitutional philosophy.

10. A Grave, Silent, Strange Sort of Animal

The year 1783 saw Hamilton return to New York City as a private citizen for the first time since the war began. He established himself as a leading member of the city’s bar, arguing cases before the New York Supreme Court and rapidly building a lucrative practice. His most celebrated case was Rutgers v. Waddington, in which he represented a Loyalist merchant whose property had been seized during the British occupation. The case turned on the question of whether New York’s Trespass Act, which allowed patriots to sue Loyalists for damages suffered during the occupation, was superseded by the Treaty of Paris, which guaranteed the protection of Loyalist property. Hamilton argued that federal treaty law must prevail over state law — a position that anticipated the supremacy clause of the Constitution.

The Rutgers case was a landmark in the development of American constitutional law. The New York Mayor’s Court accepted Hamilton’s argument, effectively asserting the power of judicial review over state legislation. This was, Chernow notes, one of the first clear statements of the doctrine that would later be developed by John Marshall and that would become a cornerstone of American jurisprudence. The case also demonstrated Hamilton’s extraordinary legal talents: his mastery of precedent, his ability to synthesize complex arguments, and his willingness to take unpopular stands in defense of principle.

Despite his legal success, Hamilton was increasingly preoccupied with politics. He was elected to the New York Assembly in 1786, where he immediately began agitating for the strengthening of the federal government. He clashed repeatedly with Governor George Clinton, who embodied everything Hamilton feared about state-level demagoguery. Clinton, a short, thickset man with coarse features and a populist style, had emerged from the Revolution with unmatched popularity and had been reelected three times. Hamilton saw in Clinton the very type of local politician who would sacrifice the national interest for local advantage, and the two became implacable foes.

The New York Assembly gave Hamilton a platform to develop his nationalist program. He introduced a resolution calling for a convention of the states to consider amendments to the Articles of Confederation, and he made a powerful speech arguing that the Confederation was “neither fit for war nor peace.” The rejection of his proposals by the Clintonian majority only deepened his conviction that the state governments were the chief obstacle to effective national union. Hamilton’s experience in the Assembly also taught him important tactical lessons about legislative politics — lessons he would later apply as treasury secretary when he needed to manage Congress and build coalitions.

Hamilton’s growing political prominence coincided with a deepening of his intellectual engagement with political economy. He read extensively in the works of Adam Smith, Sir James Steuart, and other economic theorists, and he began to develop the ideas about public credit, banking, and manufacturing that would later form the basis of his Treasury program. He also corresponded with Robert Morris, the superintendent of finance, and contributed to the public debate over economic policy through a series of essays called “The Continentalist,” which argued for giving Congress independent taxing power and the authority to regulate commerce. These essays represent an important step in the evolution of Hamilton’s thinking and anticipate many of the arguments he would later make in The Federalist.

11. Ghosts

Hamilton’s obsessive secrecy about his Caribbean past is one of the most striking features of his personality. Chernow devotes considerable attention to the “ghosts” that haunted Hamilton throughout his life — the stigma of his illegitimacy, the memory of his mother’s imprisonment and disgrace, the disappearance of his father, and the early deaths that had stripped him of almost every human tie. Hamilton never outgrew the shame of his birth, and his exquisite sensitivity to slights and his compulsive need to defend his honor can be traced directly to the insecurities of his boyhood.

In the 1780s, Hamilton was confronted with evidence that the past was not entirely dead. His father, James Hamilton, was still alive on the island of Bequia, eking out a meager existence. In 1785, Hamilton received word from a Scottish relative that his father was in dire straits. He sent what money he could spare, but the two men never met again. Hamilton wrote to the relative: “You no doubt have understood that my father’s affairs at a very early day went to wreck, so as to have rendered his situation during the greatest part of his life far from eligible. This state of things occasioned a separation between him and me, when I was very young.”

More troubling was the reappearance of his half-brother, Peter Lavien, the legitimate son born to Rachel Faucette and Johann Michael Lavien. In the 1790s, Peter’s son — also named Peter — sought Hamilton out, asking for assistance. Hamilton handled the matter with a mixture of generosity and wariness, providing some financial help but keeping the relationship at arm’s length. The encounter must have stirred painful memories of the probate court in St. Croix that had branded both Alexander and James as “obscene children” born in “whoredom” and had awarded their mother’s entire estate to Peter Lavien, Sr., who hadn’t seen his mother for eighteen years. These “ghosts” from the Caribbean never entirely released their grip on Hamilton’s psyche.

After the war, Hamilton threw himself into building a law practice in New York that quickly became one of the most lucrative in the city. Operating from an office at 57 Wall Street, he commanded fees as high as £350 per case and argued before the New York Supreme Court with a mastery of precedent that left colleagues astonished. In March 1784, scarcely a year after the British evacuation, Hamilton drafted the articles of association for the Bank of New York, the city’s first financial institution, convening a meeting at the Merchants’ Coffee House to secure subscriptions. The bank opened on June 9, 1784, in a converted private home on Hanover Square, with Hamilton composing its bylaws and serving on its first board of directors. He also threw his energies into revising the sprawling, contradictory body of New York’s state laws, serving on a three-man commission with Robert Yates and John Sloss Hobart to codify the statutes — a task that consumed much of 1785 and 1786 and deepened his understanding of the legal framework that a thriving commercial republic required.

12. August and Respectable Assembly

The Constitutional Convention of 1787 was the pivotal event in Hamilton’s political career. He arrived in Philadelphia on May 18, 1787, as one of New York’s three delegates, but his position was severely compromised: the two other delegates, Robert Yates and John Lansing, Jr., were ardent foes of a strong central government and outnumbered him on every vote. Hamilton nevertheless played a crucial role in the convention, most famously on June 18, when he rose to deliver a six-hour speech (with no break for lunch) in which he outlined his own plan for a new government, one that included a president and Senate serving for life “on good behavior.”

This speech has been the source of endless controversy and misinterpretation. Hamilton’s opponents seized on it as proof of his monarchist sympathies, and the charge that Hamilton wanted to establish a monarchy in America has persisted for more than two centuries. Chernow offers a more nuanced reading: Hamilton never openly advocated a hereditary monarchy, and his proposed “elective monarch” was subject to impeachment and legislative control. He praised the British constitution as “the best in the world” — a sentiment shared by many other delegates — but his real concern was creating a government with sufficient “energy” and stability to resist the popular passions that he believed could destroy republican liberty.

Though Hamilton’s plan was supported by nobody, he subordinated his ego to the common good and campaigned vigorously for ratification of the Constitution that emerged from the convention’s compromises. He was the lone New York delegate to sign the charter, and he wrote the names of the states preceding the signatures in his own hand. As he told the convention: “No man’s ideas were more remote from the plan than his were known to be. But is it possible to deliberate between anarchy and convulsion on one side and the chance of good to be expected from the plan on the other?” It was a quintessentially Hamiltonian moment — the idealist sacrificing his own preferences for the practical good of the nation.

The convention was also the scene of Hamilton’s first significant conflict with James Madison, the man who had been his closest collaborator. Madison’s Virginia Plan, which became the basis of the Constitution, was more democratic than Hamilton’s scheme but still provided for a strong central government. Hamilton and Madison agreed on many things — the need for federal supremacy, an independent judiciary, and executive authority — but they differed on the crucial question of how much power should be vested in the people themselves. Hamilton’s distrust of direct democracy was more profound than Madison’s, and this divergence would eventually drive the two men apart. At the convention, however, they maintained a working relationship, and Madison recorded Hamilton’s speeches in his meticulous notes — a historical record that would later be used against Hamilton by his enemies.

After the convention, Hamilton threw himself into the campaign for ratification. The battle was especially intense in New York, where Governor Clinton led a formidable anti-Federalist opposition. Hamilton’s performance at the Poughkeepsie ratifying convention was one of the finest of his career: he spoke twenty-six times, far more than any other Federalist, and carried the debate for six exhausting weeks. He knew that the fate of the Constitution might depend on New York’s decision, and he was implacable in his resolve. When a friend asked what message he should convey to supporters in New York City, Hamilton retorted, “Tell them the convention shall never rise until the Constitution is adopted.” The final vote was thirty to twenty-seven — the narrowest victory margin of any state convention — and it was Hamilton’s eloquence that had made the difference.

While the Constitutional Convention of 1787 stands as the signal achievement, the road to Philadelphia began a year earlier at the Annapolis Convention of September 1786. Hamilton, then a New York delegate to Congress, took the lead in calling for a meeting of state commissioners to discuss uniform commercial regulations. Only five states — New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Delaware, and Virginia — sent representatives to the gathering at Mann’s Tavern in Annapolis, a disappointing turnout that nonetheless yielded a historic result. Hamilton drafted the convention’s report, which noted that the delegates’ limited authority prevented them from addressing “the important defects in the system of the Federal Government” and called for a new convention to meet in Philadelphia in May 1787 “to devise such further provisions as shall appear to them necessary to render the constitution of the Federal Government adequate to the exigencies of the Union.” This report, transmitted to Congress and the state legislatures, provided the constitutional bridge from the moribund Articles of Confederation to the Philadelphia convention that would create the new frame of government.

13. Publius

After the Constitutional Convention, Hamilton conceived an ambitious writing project to help elect Federalist delegates to the New York Ratifying Convention: a comprehensive explication of the entire document. The result, The Federalist Papers, was the most persuasive defense of the Constitution ever written. Hamilton dreamed up the idea, enlisted the participants, wrote the overwhelming bulk of the essays (fifty-one of the eighty-five), and oversaw the publication. For his collaborators, he recruited John Jay (who wrote only five essays before dropping out due to rheumatism) and James Madison (who wrote twenty-nine). The essays appeared under the pseudonym “Publius” — the name of the Roman founder of the republic — and were published in four of New York’s five newspapers.

Hamilton and Madison wrote with fierce deadline pressure, delivering four essays per week at roughly three-day intervals, leaving little time for revision. “All the numbers written by [Hamilton] were composed under the greatest possible pressure of business,” Robert Troup recalled. During one prodigious burst after Madison returned to Virginia, Hamilton churned out twenty-one straight essays in a two-month period. On two occasions, he published five essays in a single week, and once he published six in a single spectacular week when writing on taxation. The essays covered everything from the weakness of the Articles of Confederation to the necessity of an energetic executive, an independent judiciary, and a system of checks and balances.

In Federalist numbers 78-85, Hamilton laid the intellectual groundwork for the doctrine of judicial review, arguing that “no legislative act…contrary to the constitution can be valid” — a principle that would later be enshrined in American jurisprudence by Chief Justice John Marshall. He also argued forcefully for a bill of rights as unnecessary and even dangerous, a position that time would prove unfortunate. The essays on the executive branch (numbers 67-77) contain some of Hamilton’s most eloquent writing, including the famous assertion that “energy in the executive is a leading character in the definition of good government.” By the year 2000, The Federalist had been quoted no fewer than 291 times in Supreme Court opinions.

The collaboration between Hamilton and Madison on The Federalist was one of the most remarkable intellectual partnerships in American history. The two men shared a common purpose — the ratification of the Constitution — and their essays complemented each other beautifully. Madison brought a deep knowledge of political theory and history; Hamilton brought practical experience of government and finance. Together, they produced a work that has been recognized ever since as the definitive exposition of the Constitution. But the partnership was also the last time the two men would work together harmoniously. The philosophical differences that had emerged at the Constitutional Convention would soon widen into an unbridgeable chasm, and within five years of the publication of the last Federalist essay, Hamilton and Madison would be engaged in open political warfare.

The Federalist essays were not initially recognized as the masterpiece they would later become. They were published in newspapers and read primarily by the politically engaged elite. Their impact on the ratification struggle in New York was limited — the anti-Federalists had a majority of delegates at Poughkeepsie, and only the prior ratification of the Constitution by New Hampshire and Virginia forced the New York convention to follow suit. But the essays’ long-term influence was immense. Jefferson pronounced The Federalist “the best commentary on the principles of government which ever was written,” and the work has been cited by the Supreme Court more often than any other source except the Constitution itself.

The first Federalist essay, written by Hamilton and published on October 27, 1787, in The Independent Journal, opened with a sweeping statement of the stakes: “It has been frequently remarked that it seems to have been reserved to the people of this country, by their conduct and example, to decide the important question, whether societies of men are really capable or not of establishing good government from reflection and choice, or whether they are forever destined to depend for their political constitutions on accident and force.” Hamilton chose the pseudonym “Publius” after Publius Valerius Publicola, the Roman aristocrat who helped found the Roman Republic and whose name signified “friend of the people.” Among the most celebrated individual essays, Federalist No. 10 (Madison) dissected the dangers of faction, No. 51 (Madison) laid out the theory of checks and balances, No. 70 (Hamilton) argued for “energy in the executive” through a single president, and No. 78 (Hamilton) established the case for judicial review and life tenure for federal judges. The essays appeared on an aggressive schedule — four per week — in four New York newspapers: The Independent Journal, The New-York Packet, The Daily Advertiser, and The New-York Journal. During one prodigious stretch after Madison returned to Virginia in March 1788, Hamilton single-handedly wrote twenty-one essays in two months, including five in a single week on the taxing power and six in another week on the militia and the army. The collected essays were published in two volumes by J. and A. McLean in 1788, but their true impact emerged over time: by the year 2000, the Supreme Court had cited The Federalist 291 times, making it the most frequently quoted commentary on the Constitution.

14. Putting the Machine in Motion

With the new government established and George Washington elected as the first president, Hamilton was appointed the first Secretary of the Treasury. From the outset, he was determined to be something more than just a financial administrator: he intended to be the chief architect of the new American state. His first official act was to ask Congress to establish a system of import duties to generate revenue, and he followed this with a series of state papers that would define the American economic system for generations. The Report on Public Credit, submitted to Congress in January 1790, was a masterpiece of financial and political reasoning that laid out a comprehensive plan for funding the national debt and establishing American credit at home and abroad.

Hamilton’s funding scheme called for the federal government to assume the twenty-five million dollars in state debts left over from the Revolution, consolidating them with the national debt into a single, manageable obligation. This “assumption” plan provoked the most bitter congressional battle of the early republic. James Madison, who had been Hamilton’s closest ally during the Constitutional Convention and the Federalist collaboration, turned against him, leading the opposition in the House. The debate was so vitriolic that it didn’t seem far-fetched that the union might break up over the issue. Hamilton, desperate to salvage his plan, struck the famous “dinner bargain” with Thomas Jefferson and James Madison in June 1790: in exchange for southern votes for assumption, Hamilton agreed to support the location of the permanent national capital on the Potomac River, with a ten-year interim in Philadelphia.

The dinner deal consecrated a compromise that probably saved the union. Hamilton, the quintessential New Yorker, bargained away his city’s chance to become the political capital of the country, testifying to the transcendent value he placed on assumption. The compromise was the last time that Hamilton, Jefferson, and Madison ever cooperated to advance a common agenda. Henceforth, they found themselves in increasingly open warfare, and the battle lines of the first party system began to take shape.

The Treasury Department itself was a creation of Hamilton’s energy and vision. He organized it into a set of specialized offices — the comptroller, auditor, treasurer, and register — each with clearly defined responsibilities. He insisted on meticulous record-keeping and regular reporting, and he established procedures for the collection and disbursement of funds that were models of administrative efficiency. He also assumed responsibility for the postal service, the census, and the patent office, making the Treasury Department the largest and most powerful branch of the executive government. The department’s influence extended into every aspect of American life, from the collection of customs duties to the regulation of the merchant marine to the management of the national debt.

The Washington administration’s first years were in many ways Hamilton’s administration. Washington, who had sought the presidency reluctantly, relied heavily on Hamilton’s advice on matters ranging from foreign policy to military affairs to the etiquette of the presidential office. Hamilton drafted Washington’s inaugural address, his messages to Congress, and his major state papers. The relationship between the two men was complex — Washington was the father figure Hamilton had never had, and Hamilton was the brilliant son Washington had never fathered — but it was one of the most productive political partnerships in American history. Without Washington’s steady support, Hamilton could never have enacted his program; without Hamilton’s ideas and energy, Washington’s presidency might have been far less consequential.

Washington began forming his cabinet in the summer of 1789, appointing Thomas Jefferson as secretary of state, Henry Knox as secretary of war, Edmund Randolph as attorney general, and Hamilton as treasury secretary. Hamilton was confirmed on September 11, 1789, and took up his duties immediately, establishing the Treasury Department in a set of rented rooms in New York before moving to Philadelphia the following year. The department’s original staff was modest — a comptroller, an auditor, a treasurer, a register, and a few clerks — but Hamilton swiftly expanded it into a formidable administrative machine. On January 14, 1790, he delivered his Report on Public Credit to Congress, a forty-thousand-word document that calculated the total federal and state debt at 79million—54 million in national obligations plus $25 million in state debts. Hamilton proposed to “fund” the entire sum by pledging specific revenues to guarantee interest payments, arguing that “a national debt, if it is not excessive, will be to us a national blessing.” The report also recommended federal “assumption” of state debts, a measure that would centralize financial authority and bind state creditors to the national government. To generate revenue for debt service, Hamilton proposed excise taxes on distilled spirits, wine, tea, and coffee — what he termed “sin taxes” on “pernicious luxuries.”

15. Villainous Business

One of the most dramatic episodes of Hamilton’s Treasury tenure was the Aedanus Burke affair — a near-duel that grew out of a remark Hamilton made in a eulogy for General Nathanael Greene. Hamilton had spoken of “the mimicry of soldiership” — a phrase that Burke, a South Carolina congressman, interpreted as a slur against the southern militia. In February 1791, nearly a year after the eulogy, Burke rose on the floor of the House and, “casting his eyes on the visitors’ gallery,” blasted Hamilton in language that crossed the boundaries of political decorum: “In the face of this Assembly and in the presence of this gallery…I give the lie to Col. Hamilton.”

This blatant affront was so shocking that congressmen interrupted Burke’s outburst with loud calls for order, recognizing that Burke had violated Hamilton’s personal sense of honor. In the code of the eighteenth century, a direct challenge to one’s honor had to be settled on the dueling ground. Senator William Maclay noted in his diary “a violent personal attack on Hamilton by Judge Burke of South Carolina, which the men of the blade say must produce a duel.” Hamilton sent a short, heated letter to Burke demanding satisfaction, and for several days the town was “much agitated about a duel between Burke and Hamilton.”

The dispute was ultimately arbitrated by a party of six congressmen who orchestrated an exchange of letters: Hamilton insisted he meant no dishonor to the southern militias, and Burke accepted this statement and apologized. The affair wasn’t altogether a victory for Hamilton — in his memorial speech for Greene, he had taken gratuitous swipes at southern soldiers and not paid sufficient attention to the pieties of democratic politics. Burke made him feel the sting of public opinion, and it wasn’t the last time Hamilton would pay a price for needless indiscretion. The contretemps again demonstrated that beneath his invincible facade, Hamilton was still the hypersensitive boy from the West Indies, his combativeness driven by more than just political calculation.

The assumption debate was the most vicious congressional battle of Hamilton’s tenure. When Madison reversed his earlier support for federal assumption of state debts, Hamilton realized his entire financial system might collapse. The House of Representatives deadlocked, with southern states — many of which had already paid their own debts — refusing to shoulder the burden of northern and southern states that had not. Into this impasse stepped Thomas Jefferson, who recorded in his Anas that he “came to New York” in June 1790 and “pressed an invitation” to Hamilton and Madison to dine with him. Over that dinner at Jefferson’s Maiden Lane residence, the famous Compromise of 1790 was struck: Hamilton agreed to secure northern votes for locating the permanent national capital on the Potomac River, with a ten-year interim in Philadelphia, while Jefferson and Madison would deliver southern votes for assumption. The Residence Act passed Congress on July 16, 1790, and the Assumption Act followed on August 4. The bargain infuriated Pennsylvanians, who had hoped Philadelphia would remain the permanent capital, and it disappointed New Yorkers, who saw the city lose its chance at political preeminence. Jefferson later repudiated his role in the deal, telling President Washington that he had been “duped by Hamilton” into thinking the measure necessary for the survival of the Union.

16. Dr. Pangloss

In March 1790, Thomas Jefferson arrived in New York to assume his duties as the new secretary of state. He came fresh from five years in Paris, having witnessed the opening stages of the French Revolution, and he brought with him a vision of America as an agrarian paradise. The meeting between Hamilton and Jefferson set the stage for the most famous political feud in American history. Chernow portrays Jefferson as “Dr. Pangloss” — a congenital optimist who, despite having witnessed the French Revolution firsthand, remained blind to its murderous drift while Hamilton, who had never set foot in Europe, was much more clear-sighted about the revolutionary violence.

Jefferson’s ideological differences with Hamilton were profound. Where Hamilton wanted a powerful central government with a dynamic executive branch, a national bank, and a modern financial system, Jefferson wanted to preserve state sovereignty, limit federal power, and maintain an agrarian economy of independent farmers. Jefferson believed that Hamilton’s system would create a corrupt moneyed aristocracy that would subvert republican government, while Hamilton believed that Jefferson’s naively optimistic vision would leave America weak, divided, and vulnerable to European predation.

The personal contrast between the two men was equally stark. Hamilton was a swashbuckler who reveled in debate, opinionated and almost recklessly candid; Jefferson was courtly, guarded, and hated confrontation. Jefferson had learned the advantages of inscrutable silence — while serving with him in the Continental Congress, John Adams recalled, “I never heard him utter three sentences together.” For Hamilton, unable to govern his tongue or his pen, this habit of self-exposure placed him at the mercy of the tightly controlled Jefferson. By early 1792, any pose of civility between the two secretaries had disappeared, and Jefferson remembered them “daily pitted in the cabinet like two cocks.”

Jefferson’s agrarian vision was informed by his five years as minister to France, where he had witnessed the corruption of the ancien regime and the promise of the French Revolution. He returned to America in 1789 with a deepened suspicion of cities, banks, and commerce, and he saw in Hamilton’s programs the same “corruption” that had destroyed the French monarchy. Jefferson’s ideal America was a nation of independent yeoman farmers, each owning his own land and requiring no government beyond the most minimal. He had no use for banks, stock exchanges, or manufacturing — all of which he regarded as engines of speculation and vice.

Hamilton, by contrast, had been shaped by his West Indian experience of global commerce and by his military experience of the consequences of a weak government. He saw that America’s future lay not in isolation and self-sufficiency but in trade, finance, and industry. He believed that economic growth required the concentration of capital, the expansion of credit, and the active promotion of enterprise by the state. Jefferson’s vision was backward-looking, rooted in an idealized past that had never existed; Hamilton’s was forward-looking, anticipating the commercial and industrial America that would emerge in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

Hamilton’s Report on Manufactures, submitted to Congress on December 5, 1791, was the capstone of his state papers and the first comprehensive industrial policy proposed by any Western government. In it, Hamilton argued that the United States should not remain a purely agrarian nation but must develop domestic manufacturing to achieve economic independence from Europe. He catalogued the advantages of manufactures: they would create demand for agricultural raw materials, absorb surplus farm labor, provide employment for women and children, attract immigration of skilled artisans, and expand the range of investment opportunities for idle capital. To promote manufacturing, Hamilton proposed tariff protection for infant industries, government bounties for new inventions, premiums for quality goods, and exemptions from duties on raw materials imported for processing. Though Congress never enacted the report’s specific recommendations, it established the intellectual framework for American industrial policy and anticipated the American System of Henry Clay. Jefferson, by contrast, clung to a vision of America as “a nation of farmers,” writing that “those who labor in the earth are the chosen people of God” and warning that “the mobs of great cities add just so much to the support of pure government as sores do to the strength of the human body.”

17. The First Town in America

With the passage of the Residence Act in July 1790, the federal government moved from New York to Philadelphia, which would serve as the temporary capital for ten years while the permanent capital was built on the Potomac. Philadelphia was then the largest and most cosmopolitan city in America — praised by one British visitor as “one of the wonders of the world” — and Hamilton executed the shift with almost martial precision. He secured a two-story brick building on Third Street for the Treasury Department and took a home at Third and Walnut, just down the block from his office.

The Treasury Department under Hamilton quickly became a formidable bureaucracy, far larger than any other branch of the government. With the Customs Service under its control, the department’s payroll ballooned to more than five hundred employees, while Henry Knox had a mere dozen civilians in the War Department and Jefferson a paltry six at State. The sheer size of the Treasury Department inevitably aroused opposition, no matter how prudent Hamilton’s style. Hamilton’s power extended beyond his legion of employees: seven of every eight Treasury employees worked outside the capital, supplying Hamilton with an unending stream of intelligence about economic activity across the country.

Hamilton’s creation of the Customs Service consumed enormous amounts of his time. He oversaw the construction of lighthouses, beacons, and buoys along the Atlantic seaboard, reviewed every contract, and became expert on such minutiae as the best whale oil and wicks to brighten lighthouse beams. In constructing the new Revenue Cutter Service (later the Coast Guard), Hamilton insisted on rigorous professionalism and issued directives of breathtaking specificity, requiring that each cutter possess ten muskets and bayonets, twenty pistols, two chisels, one broadax, and two lanterns. So masterly was Hamilton’s directive about boarding foreign vessels that it was still being applied during the 1962 Cuban missile crisis.

Philadelphia in the 1790s was a city of vibrant social and economic energy. The Hamiltons moved in elite circles that included the merchant prince William Bingham and his wife, Anne Willing Bingham, who presided over lavish dinners at their three-story mansion near Third and Spruce. Hamilton was a frequent guest at these gatherings, where the conversation ranged from politics to finance to the latest theatrical productions at the Chestnut Street Theatre. The city also witnessed the birth of America’s first securities market: as Hamilton’s funding program took effect, government bonds began to trade actively on the streets around the Merchants’ Coffee House on Second Street, where auctioneers sold stocks, bonds, and bank shares alongside shipments of flour and tobacco. The speculative fever of 1791-92, driven partly by Hamilton’s bank scrip offering on July 4, 1791, saw the creation of the first organized securities trading in America. William Duer, Hamilton’s assistant treasury secretary, plunged heavily into these markets and helped trigger the Panic of 1792 when his speculation collapsed, leading to the first financial crisis of the new republic. Hamilton responded by having the Treasury purchase $150,000 in government securities to stabilize the market — the first federal intervention to prevent a financial panic.

18. Of Avarice and Enterprise

On December 14, 1790, Hamilton submitted his proposal for the Bank of the United States — the most brilliant and most controversial of all his state papers. Chernow argues that no other founding father straddled both the political revolution of the age and the parallel economic revolution of industrialism, global trade, and banking — only Franklin even came close. Hamilton was “the clear-eyed apostle of America’s economic future,” setting forth a vision that many found enthralling, others unsettling, but that would ultimately prevail. His bank proposal would serve the government and invigorate the economy, and he constantly stressed the broader public benefits.

The bank bill passed the Senate easily but provoked a furious constitutional debate in the House. Madison, now leading the opposition, argued that the Constitution did not specifically grant the federal government the power to incorporate a bank. Hamilton responded with his famous “implied powers” argument — that the necessary-and-proper clause of the Constitution gave the government the right to employ all means necessary to carry out its enumerated powers. When Washington hesitated, Hamilton produced a fifteen-thousand-word opinion that ranks as “the most brilliant argument for a broad interpretation of the Constitution in American political literature.”

Eliza Hamilton remembered the sleepless night when her husband produced this masterpiece. As an ancient lady garbed in widow’s weeds, she told a visitor: “He made your government. He made your bank. I sat up all night with him to help him do it. Jefferson thought we ought not to have a bank and President Washington thought so. But my husband said, ‘We must have a Bank.’ I sat up all night, copied out his writing, and the next morning, he carried it to President Washington and we had a bank.” Hamilton’s argument established the doctrine of implied powers that would later be invoked by Chief Justice John Marshall in McCulloch v. Maryland and that has since been used to expand federal authority in countless ways.

The bank controversy also deepened the emerging split between Hamilton and Madison. Hamilton had written the bank opinion as an official response to Washington’s request, but it was also a reply to the arguments that Madison had made in Congress. The two former collaborators were now on opposite sides of a fundamental constitutional question. Madison argued for “strict construction” — that the federal government could exercise only those powers specifically enumerated in the Constitution. Hamilton argued for “broad construction” — that the government could employ any means “necessary and proper” to achieve its constitutional ends. The debate was the opening shot in a constitutional argument that would continue for decades and that would eventually be resolved, largely in Hamilton’s favor, by the Supreme Court under John Marshall.

The bank was only one of a series of measures that Hamilton pushed through Congress in his first two years as treasury secretary. He also established the U.S. Mint, creating a decimal currency and a system of coinage that would serve as the model for American money for the next two centuries. His Report on the Mint proposed a bimetallic standard (gold and silver), a decimal division of the dollar, and a variety of coins from copper cents to gold eagles. The mint was a pet project of Jefferson’s, and Washington placed it under the State Department — a decision that Hamilton resented and that led to poor administration of the new institution.

Hamilton’s vision of a commercial republic found its most concrete expression in the Society for Establishing Useful Manufactures (SEUM), incorporated by the New Jersey legislature in November 1791. The society planned to build a manufacturing town at the Great Falls of the Passaic in northern New Jersey, where seventy-foot waterfalls could power a complex of textile mills, paper factories, and ironworks. Hamilton personally helped draft the company’s charter, recruited British textile artisans in defiance of English laws prohibiting the emigration of skilled mechanics, and selected the site that became Paterson — named to flatter Governor William Paterson, who granted the SEUM a ten-year tax exemption and monopoly privileges. The society purchased seven hundred acres and carved them into parcels for factories and a planned town. Hamilton’s Report on Manufactures, submitted to Congress that same December, provided the theoretical justification for this industrial experiment, arguing that the United States must “not be too fastidious” about borrowing manufacturing techniques from Europe. Though the SEUM failed financially — William Duer, its first governor, embezzled funds and the Panic of 1792 ruined its prospects — Paterson eventually became one of America’s great industrial cities, vindicating Hamilton’s conviction that American manufacturing could rival that of any European nation.

19. City of the Future

By the summer of 1791, after his victories on public credit, assumption, and the national bank, Hamilton had attained the summit of his power. But his vigorous reign had also made him the enfant terrible of the early republic, and a substantial minority of the country was mobilized against him. Then, in one of history’s most mystifying cases of bad judgment, he entered into a sordid affair with a married woman named Maria Reynolds that, if it did not blacken his name forever, certainly sullied it. From the lofty heights of statesmanship, Hamilton fell back into something reminiscent of the squalid world of his West Indian boyhood.

The affair began in the summer of 1791 when Maria Reynolds appeared at Hamilton’s Philadelphia home with a tale of a cruel, abusive husband who had abandoned her. Hamilton, never able to resist a maiden in distress, visited her that evening and “some conversation ensued from which it was quickly apparent that other than pecuniary consolation would be acceptable.” The affair continued through the summer and fall of 1791, while Eliza was away in Albany with the children. When James Reynolds discovered the liaison, he began an elaborate blackmail scheme that would extort more than a thousand dollars from Hamilton over the next year. Hamilton’s behavior throughout the affair was a study in self-destructive compulsion. He seemed unable to extricate himself from the Reynoldses’ web, even when it became clear that they were working together as a team — the wife as seductress, the husband as blackmailer.

Even as Hamilton indulged this disastrous affair, his imagination was conjuring up a futuristic industrial city. The Society for Establishing Useful Manufactures (SEUM) was the most ambitious industrial experiment in early American history, a plan to create an entire manufacturing town at the Great Falls of the Passaic River in New Jersey. Hamilton personally recruited British textile workers (in defiance of British laws against the export of industrial expertise) and oversaw the planning of the town of Paterson. His Report on Manufactures, submitted to Congress in December 1791, was the first government-sponsored plan for selective industrial planning in America, envisioning a diversified economy that would free America from dependence on European manufactured goods.

The SEUM ultimately failed — William Duer, its first governor, embezzled its funds and the panic of 1792 destroyed its prospects — but Paterson would eventually become one of America’s great industrial cities. The Report on Manufactures had a longer influence. Though Congress never acted on it directly, it established the intellectual framework for American industrial policy, arguing that the federal government should actively promote domestic manufacturing through tariffs, bounties, and internal improvements. The report was, in many respects, the forerunner of the American System of Henry Clay and the protective tariffs of the nineteenth century. It also cemented Hamilton’s reputation as the prophet of American industrial capitalism. Hamilton’s simultaneous engagement in the Reynolds affair and the SEUM project reveals the extraordinary range of his character. He could be simultaneously the most visionary of statesmen and the most foolish of men. The same energy that enabled him to conceive the SEUM and write the Report on Manufactures also drove him into the Reynoldses’ trap. The contrast between his public brilliance and his private weakness is one of the most striking features of his career, and it has fascinated and perplexed biographers ever since.

Even as Hamilton became entangled in the Reynolds affair, he was deeply involved in planning the new federal city that would rise on the Potomac.

20. Corrupt Squadrons

The emergence of the first political parties in American history was driven largely by the conflict over Hamilton’s programs. Chernow notes that Hamilton, despite being the inadvertent flash point for the formation of parties, dreaded factions as “the most fatal disease” of popular governments. Nevertheless, his charismatic personality and far-reaching policies unified his followers into the Federalist party, while the mounting fear of Hamilton among Jefferson, Madison, and their supporters cohered into an organized opposition that called itself the Republican party. The Federalists drew their strength from banking and merchant interests in New England; the Republicans from southern planters and small farmers.

Jefferson and Madison organized the opposition with remarkable effectiveness. They recruited the poet Philip Freneau to establish the National Gazette as a Republican newspaper, and Freneau’s savage attacks on Hamilton, while Jefferson provided Freneau with a sinecure in the State Department. Hamilton responded with a volley of pseudonymous newspaper essays, attacking Jefferson in terms that grew increasingly personal. By 1792, the two secretaries were at open war, and Washington struggled to maintain harmony in his cabinet. Washington pleaded with both men for “healing measures,” but his pleas went unheeded.

The conflict came to a head when Jefferson and Madison, through the Virginia congressman William Branch Giles, launched a formal congressional investigation of Hamilton’s conduct as treasury secretary. Hamilton, who had been exonerated of similar charges in 1792, was now subjected to a second, still more exhaustive inquiry. He responded with characteristic energy, producing a staggering volume of reports and accounting. In the end, the Republican-dominated committee confessed that all charges against Hamilton were completely baseless. The vindication was so resounding that Hamilton withdrew his standing resignation. Nevertheless, he had learned a bitter lesson about politics: “No character, however upright, is a match for constantly reiterated attacks, however false.”

Hamilton’s battles with Jefferson were not merely personal or partisan — they expressed deep and enduring divisions about the nature of American society. Hamilton believed in a strong, active federal government that would promote economic development, concentrate capital, and bind the nation together through financial and commercial ties. Jefferson believed that such a government would inevitably become a tool of the rich and powerful, undermining the liberty of ordinary citizens. Both men were sincere in their convictions, and both were right about some things and wrong about others. The contest between them defined the political landscape of the early republic and established the terms of debate for American politics for generations to come.

The newspaper wars between Hamilton and Jefferson reached their peak in 1792-93, with the Gazette of the United States (edited by John Fenno, whom Hamilton had encouraged to start the paper in 1789) battling the National Gazette (edited by Philip Freneau, whom Jefferson had hired as a State Department translator at $250 per year). Hamilton began his counterattack in July 1792 with essays signed “T.L.” accusing Jefferson of using Freneau as a tool to “vilify those to whom the administration of the government has been intrusted.” When Hamilton learned that Jefferson had given Freneau a sinecure in the State Department, he published a series of devastating articles under the signature “An American,” detailing the arrangement and accusing Jefferson of funding a propagandist with public money. The split between Hamilton and Madison, who had collaborated so closely on The Federalist, became irreparable as Madison wrote anonymous attacks in the National Gazette accusing Hamilton of creating a “government operated by corrupt influence.” By the end of 1793, Jefferson had resigned from the cabinet, and Hamilton had lost his most trusted collaborator, but the two-party system that would define American politics had been irrevocably born.

21. Exposure

The Reynolds affair, which Hamilton had hoped would remain buried, came to light in November 1792 when James Reynolds and Jacob Clingman were arrested for defrauding the government. From prison, Reynolds began to hint that he could “make disclosures injurious to the character of some head of a department.” The explosive news reached three Republican congressmen: Frederick Muhlenberg, James Monroe, and Abraham Venable. On December 15, 1792, they confronted Hamilton in his office, expecting to expose official corruption. Instead, Hamilton stunned them by confessing to an adulterous affair with Maria Reynolds.

In a scene of extraordinary drama, Hamilton gathered a batch of letters from James and Maria Reynolds and recounted the entire sordid history to the three legislators — how the husband had served as a pimp for his wife, how the blackmail payments had been made, and how the couple had ensnared him. When Muhlenberg and Venable realized the scandal involved marital infidelity, not government corruption, they “delicately urged me to discontinue it as unnecessary. I insisted upon going through the whole and did so.” The three men apologized for having invaded his privacy and swore to keep the matter confidential.

The promise of confidentiality did not hold. Monroe, trusting the task to his ally John Beckley, allowed copies of the documents to circulate among Republican insiders. By December 17, 1792, both Jefferson and Madison knew the full story. Hamilton was now a man with a terrible secret that his political enemies could use against him at any moment. He had shown, Chernow argues, “a lack of private restraint” that mirrored the compulsive quality of his public attacks on Jefferson. The Reynolds affair was to cast a shadow over the rest of his career and contributed to his decision never to seek the presidency.

22. Stabbed in the Dark

The Giles resolutions of early 1793 were the most serious attempt to drive Hamilton from office. Introduced by Virginia congressman William Branch Giles — who was acting as a surrogate for Jefferson and Madison — the nine resolutions accused Hamilton of “indecorum” in dealing with Congress, of improperly mixing foreign and domestic loans, and of maladministration. Hamilton, despite being distracted by the Reynolds crisis, produced a series of exhaustive reports vindicating every aspect of his Treasury management. All nine resolutions were voted down by the House on March 1, 1793.

Despite his vindication, Hamilton felt increasingly isolated and embattled. “The malicious intrigues to stab me in the dark,” he wrote to John Jay, “distract and harass me to a point which, rendering my situation scarcely tolerable, interferes with objects to which friendship and inclination would prompt me.” He was being badgered from all sides: by the Giles investigation, by whispering campaigns about the Reynolds affair, by the financial panic of 1792, and by the endless administrative burdens of running the Treasury Department. He told Angelica Church that he was “heartily tired of my situation and wait only the opportunity of quitting it with honor.”

The Republican effort to expel Hamilton from the cabinet failed, but it exposed the depth of the partisan divide. Jefferson, who had instigated the Giles resolutions, recognized that he could no longer remain in the cabinet while his rival held unchallenged sway over the president. In December 1793, Jefferson resigned as secretary of state and returned to Monticello. It was a bittersweet victory for Hamilton: his triumph over Jefferson was scarcely savored, as he was besieged by enemies, worried about his health, and felt unappreciated by the public. “Truly this trade of a statesman is but a sorry thing,” he confided to Church.

The Giles resolutions represented the most determined Republican effort to destroy Hamilton’s credibility. William Branch Giles introduced nine resolutions on February 23, 1793, charging Hamilton with failing to provide adequate information to Congress, misapplying foreign loans, exceeding his authority in negotiating loans, and generally conducting himself with “indecorum.” Hamilton produced a 157-page rebuttal that traced every dollar borrowed, lent, and spent since the Treasury’s creation. The House voted on each resolution separately, rejecting them by margins ranging from 26 to 30 votes against to 12 to 17 in favor. The decisive defeat was a stinging humiliation for Jefferson and Madison, who had orchestrated the attack from behind the scenes. Hamilton’s vindication was so complete that he withdrew his standing resignation, but he had spent incalculable hours defending himself when he might have been advancing his programs. The episode convinced him that remaining in office meant subjecting himself to perpetual harassment.

23. Citizen Genêt

Washington’s second term was dominated by the crisis sparked by the French Revolution and the arrival of the controversial French minister, Edmond Charles Genêt. The French Revolution forced Americans to ponder the meaning of their own revolution, and Hamilton and Jefferson drew diametrically opposite conclusions. Hamilton saw in the Parisian bloodshed a horrifying portent of what could happen if the safeguards of order were stripped away by the love of liberty, while Jefferson — despite having witnessed the events firsthand — refused to condemn the violence. The Terror, the September Massacres, the execution of Louis XVI — all of these were, for Jefferson, regrettable but necessary costs of the struggle for freedom. For Hamilton, they were proof that revolutions, once unleashed, could not be controlled.

Genêt arrived in Charleston in April 1793 and, acting more like a political candidate than a diplomat, traveled north to Philadelphia, stirring up support for the French cause. He commissioned American privateers to attack British shipping, organized Democratic-Republican societies, and — most dangerously — threatened to “appeal from the President of the United States to the People.” Hamilton, who had already argued for a proclamation of neutrality, now pressed for Genêt’s recall. Jefferson, who had initially championed Genêt, eventually turned against him, and the French government was asked to recall its insolent minister. Genêt, ironically, chose to remain in America, married George Clinton’s daughter, and lived out his life as a country gentleman in upstate New York.

The Genêt affair provided Hamilton with a platform to articulate his vision of American foreign policy. Under the pseudonym “Pacificus,” he published seven essays defending the Neutrality Proclamation and arguing for executive authority in foreign affairs. The essays infuriated Jefferson, who prodded Madison into responding as “Helvidius.” The Pacificus-Helvidius debate was the first great constitutional confrontation over the scope of executive power in foreign policy, and it established the framework for American neutrality that would guide the young republic through the European wars of the French Revolution and Napoleonic era.

Hamilton’s “Pacificus” essays are among his most important writings on foreign policy. He argued that the president, as the chief executive and commander-in-chief, possessed the inherent authority to declare neutrality, even without explicit congressional authorization. This was a bold assertion of executive power, and it provoked Madison’s “Helvidius” response, which argued that the power to define the nation’s foreign policy belonged to Congress. The debate between Hamilton and Madison was a rehearsal for the larger constitutional struggles that would define American politics for the next half-century. In the short term, Hamilton’s view prevailed — the Neutrality Proclamation stood, and the United States avoided entanglement in the European war.

Hamilton’s “Pacificus” essays, published in the Gazette of the United States from June 29 to July 17, 1793, were written at white heat as Genêt’s provocations escalated. In the first essay, Hamilton argued that the executive had the inherent power to declare neutrality, since “the executive is the department of the government in which the power of declaring war or making peace most naturally resides.” The third essay warned of the catastrophic consequences of joining France’s war: Great Britain and Spain could incite “numerous Indian tribes” to attack America’s western frontier, while “with a long extended sea coast, with no fortifications whatever and with a population not exceeding four millions,” the United States could not survive a major European conflict. Madison responded with five “Helvidius” essays, arguing that the power to define the nation’s foreign policy belonged to Congress. The debate remains a classic formulation of the opposing views on executive power in foreign affairs. Genêt was ultimately recalled by the French government after the Jacobins seized power, and he chose to remain in America, marrying Cornelia Clinton, daughter of Governor George Clinton, and living out his life as a country gentleman on a farm on the Hudson River near New York City.

24. A Disagreeable Trade

In the late summer of 1793, Philadelphia was devastated by the worst yellow fever epidemic in American history. More than four thousand people died, bringing government and commerce to a standstill. Hamilton contracted the disease in early September, and he and Eliza repaired to their summer residence outside the city. In an astonishing coincidence, Hamilton’s boyhood friend from St. Croix, Dr. Edward Stevens — who may have been his biological brother — had just arrived in Philadelphia and attended to the couple. Stevens treated Hamilton with quinine and cold baths instead of the bloodletting and purges favored by Dr. Benjamin Rush, and the Hamiltons recovered within five days.

The yellow fever epidemic had a profound effect on Hamilton. His brush with death seems to have deepened his religious feelings and his sense of the fragility of life. He published an open letter to the College of Physicians praising Stevens’s methods, which provoked a bitter medical-political dispute with Benjamin Rush, an ardent Jeffersonian. Rush accused Hamilton of introducing political bias into medical practice and insisted that Hamilton had never really had yellow fever at all — just “common remitting fevers.”

The epidemic also claimed its share of political victims. The National Gazette suspended publication, and Jefferson, who had already announced his intention to resign, finally left the cabinet on December 31, 1793. Hamilton had been granted the dearest gift of all: the exit of his nemesis from the administration. But the triumph was short-lived. The Republicans had captured majorities in Congress, and Hamilton knew that further investigations of his conduct were inevitable. He told Washington that he would stay in office only long enough to clear his name.

The yellow fever epidemic of 1793 killed more than 4,000 of Philadelphia’s 50,000 residents, roughly 8 percent of the population. Among the dead were Hamilton’s friend Dr. James Hutchinson and the British minister George Hammond’s secretary. The epidemic brought government to a standstill — Congress adjourned, Washington retreated to Mount Vernon, and Hamilton’s Treasury Department operated with a skeleton crew. After recovering from the disease, Hamilton published an open letter on September 25, 1793, in Dunlap’s American Daily Advertiser extolling Dr. Edward Stevens’s treatment with quinine, cold baths, and wine, attacking the bloodletting methods of Dr. Benjamin Rush. Rush, a prominent Philadelphia physician and Jeffersonian, published a rejoinder in the National Gazette accusing Hamilton of introducing political bias into medicine and insisting that his case had been merely “a common remitting fever,” not yellow fever at all. The medical controversy mirrored the political divide: Rush was a signer of the Declaration of Independence and a committed Republican, while Stevens was Hamilton’s lifelong friend from St. Croix. The epidemic did not end until the first hard frost in October, and it permanently changed Philadelphia, driving many permanent residents to new settlements outside the city.

25. Seas of Blood

Hamilton’s final years in the Treasury Department were dominated by foreign policy and the crisis with Great Britain. In 1793 and 1794, the British navy seized more than 250 American merchant ships and began impressing American sailors — actions that threatened to drag the United States into a war with Britain that Hamilton had spent his entire career trying to avoid. Despite his famous pro-British sympathies, Hamilton did not behave like a pawn of British interests. He drew up contingency plans for raising a twenty-thousand-man army and constructing six frigates (the birth of the U.S. Navy). He was shocked by British arrogance and wrote bitterly of “the pains taken to preserve peace” being undermined by British stupidity.

To avert war, Washington dispatched Chief Justice John Jay as a special envoy to London in May 1794. Hamilton drafted Jay’s instructions and served as the intellectual architect of what became the Jay Treaty. The treaty was deeply controversial: while it secured British evacuation of the northwest forts and established “most favored nation” trading status with Britain, it said nothing about impressment or compensation for stolen slaves, and it was widely seen as a capitulation to British interests. Hamilton defended the treaty in a series of essays under the pseudonym “Camillus,” arguing that it was the best that could be obtained and that any alternative would lead to war. The “Camillus” essays were among Hamilton’s most formidable polemical works, demonstrating his ability to explain complex policy issues to a broad audience.

The Jay Treaty debate was the most divisive political event of the 1790s. Jay was burned in effigy from Georgia to Massachusetts, and Hamilton was pelted with stones when he tried to defend the treaty at a public meeting in New York. But the treaty passed the Senate by the narrowest possible margin, and it helped to preserve the peace with Britain that Hamilton considered essential to America’s commercial prosperity. In the process, Hamilton cemented his reputation as the leading exponent of a foreign policy based on national interest rather than ideological affinity — a philosophy that would later be called Hamiltonian in its realism.

The treaty also marked the final break between Hamilton and the emerging Democratic-Republican opposition. Jefferson wrote from Monticello that the treaty was “an alliance between England and the Anglomen of this country against the people of the United States.” Madison denounced it as a betrayal of republican principles. The debate over the treaty accelerated the polarization of American politics and helped to define the party identities that would persist for the next generation. Hamilton, who had always believed that foreign policy should be based on national interest rather than ideological affinity, was now the undisputed leader of the pro-British, commercial Federalists, while Jefferson and Madison led the pro-French, agrarian Republicans.

Hamilton’s role in the Jay Treaty was decisive. He drafted the instructions that Chief Justice John Jay carried to London in May 1794, outlining a negotiation strategy that prioritized commercial access to British markets and the evacuation of the northwest military posts. When the treaty arrived in Philadelphia in March 1795, Hamilton immediately recognized its political toxicity: it secured British evacuation of forts at Detroit, Michilimackinac, and Niagara and granted “most favored nation” trading status, but it said nothing about impressment of American sailors, compensation for slaves carried off during the Revolution, or the seizure of American ships. Hamilton defended the treaty in a series of twenty-eight essays under the pseudonym “Camillus,” published in the New-York Evening Post beginning in July 1795. The essays were so exhaustive and compelling that even John Adams admitted they were “a measure of his talents.” When Hamilton attempted to defend the treaty at a public meeting in New York’s Liberty Pole, he was pelted with stones and forced to retreat. Nevertheless, the Senate ratified the treaty on June 24, 1795, by a vote of twenty to ten — exactly the two-thirds majority required — and Washington signed it. The treaty preserved the peace with Britain that Hamilton considered essential to American prosperity.

26. The Wicked Insurgents of the West

The Whiskey Rebellion of 1794 was the most serious domestic challenge to the authority of the new federal government. Farmers in western Pennsylvania, angered by Hamilton’s excise tax on distilled spirits, attacked tax collectors, burned down the house of the local inspector, and threatened the garrison at Pittsburgh. When it became clear that the state of Pennsylvania could not suppress the uprising, Hamilton urged Washington to raise a federal militia of twelve thousand men — a larger army than Washington had usually commanded during the Revolution.

The rebellion was a defining moment for Hamilton, who saw it as a test of the Constitution itself. Writing as “Tully,” he argued that “the most sacred duty of government is an inviolable respect for the Constitution and laws” and that the government must show itself capable of enforcing its authority. He personally accompanied the army on its march to western Pennsylvania, despite the fact that his wife was suffering a difficult pregnancy at home. His absence, Hamilton later believed, contributed to Eliza’s miscarriage, a tragedy that filled him with guilt. Hamilton’s letters to Eliza during this period show a man torn between his public duty and his private anguish.

The Whiskey Rebellion ended without significant bloodshed. The mere sight of the federal army was enough to disperse the insurgents. Washington pardoned the two convicted leaders, a clemency that Hamilton opposed, fearing it would encourage future lawlessness. The episode demonstrated that the new federal government could enforce its laws, but it also deepened the partisan divide. Jefferson and Madison saw the rebellion as “Hamilton’s insurrection” — proof that the treasury secretary was a power-hungry man who lusted for a military confrontation. “So it may be said,” Timothy Pickering later observed, “that the whiskey drinkers made Mr. Jefferson the President of the United States.”

The Whiskey Rebellion also had important consequences for Hamilton’s thinking about the nature of government. He had always believed that a strong central government was necessary to maintain order and protect property rights. The rebellion confirmed these beliefs and deepened his conviction that the federal government must have the power to enforce its laws, if necessary by military force. This view put him increasingly at odds with the Jeffersonian Republicans, who saw the use of federal force against American citizens as a dangerous precedent — a step toward the kind of centralized tyranny that the Revolution had been fought to overthrow. The debate over the Whiskey Rebellion was, in essence, a debate about the fundamental nature of the American union.

Hamilton’s ride at the head of the federal army into western Pennsylvania in October 1794 was among the most dramatic moments of his career. Though he was secretary of the treasury, not secretary of war, he insisted on accompanying the 12,950-man militia force as a civilian adviser to General Henry “Light-Horse Harry” Lee. Hamilton personally drafted the general orders for the march, specifying the precise quantity of ammunition (sixty rounds per man) and provisions (three days’ cooked rations) that each soldier should carry. The army marched in two columns from Cumberland, Maryland, and Carlisle, Pennsylvania, crossing the Allegheny Mountains in driving rain and snow. When they reached the town of Parkinson’s Ferry (now Monongahela), the insurgents had already melted away. In an act of clemency that Hamilton opposed, Washington later pardoned the two convicted leaders of the rebellion, John Mitchell and Philip Vigol, who had been sentenced to death for treason. Hamilton argued that leniency would set a dangerous precedent, but Washington’s instinct for national reconciliation prevailed. The rebellion’s collapse demonstrated the new government’s authority but created a lasting grievance among western farmers that fueled the Republican opposition.

27. Sugar Plums and Toys

In January 1795, Hamilton resigned as secretary of the treasury and returned to private life. His resignation marked the end of an era. He had been the dominant figure in Washington’s administration, the architect of American fiscal policy, and the driving force behind the creation of the modern American state. His farewell report on government finance charted a wide-ranging course for the future, and he left the Treasury Department as a model of administrative efficiency that would be emulated by governments around the world. Washington paid tribute to his departing secretary in the warmest possible terms, writing that “in every relation which you have borne to me, I have found that my confidence in your talents, exertions, and integrity has been well placed.”

Hamilton returned to his law practice in New York, but he did not retire from politics. He continued to advise Washington’s cabinet (which was now filled with his protégés, including Oliver Wolcott, Jr., at Treasury and Timothy Pickering at State), and he wrote a steady stream of newspaper essays on public issues. He also began work on an ambitious project to establish a newspaper that would serve as the voice of the Federalist party — the New-York Evening Post, which would become one of America’s most influential newspapers. The Post was founded in 1801 and was edited by William Coleman, a Hamilton protégé, but Hamilton was the paper’s guiding spirit and occasional contributor.

His private life, however, was shadowed by the Reynolds affair. The secret was known to too many people, and Hamilton lived in constant fear that it would be made public. In 1797, the journalist James Thomson Callender — a Scottish radical who had once been a Jefferson ally — published a pamphlet exposing the affair, based on documents that had been leaked to him by John Beckley. The scandal forced Hamilton to publish a 95-page pamphlet, the Observations on Certain Documents, in which he confessed to the adultery in excruciating detail while denying — truthfully — any official corruption. It was a devastating blow to his reputation from which he never fully recovered. The pamphlet was a characteristically Hamilton solution — full disclosure as a form of self-justification — but it was a catastrophic error. It gave his enemies all the ammunition they needed and ensured that the scandal would never be forgotten.

Hamilton’s return to private life was thus a time of mixed fortunes. He was financially successful, professionally respected, and domestically happy — but he was also a marked man, his reputation permanently tarnished, his political ambitions permanently frustrated. He had achieved extraordinary things, but he had also made terrible mistakes, and he knew it. The Reynolds affair, Chernow suggests, was the great tragedy of Hamilton’s life — not just because it hurt him politically, but because it represented a failure of self-control that was deeply at odds with his own principles.

Hamilton’s resignation took effect on January 31, 1795. His farewell report to Congress, submitted a week earlier, outlined the government’s financial position in precise detail: total revenues of 6.5million,expendituresof5.8 million, and a surplus that had already retired nearly $2 million of the debt. He recommended a comprehensive plan for extinguishing the entire national debt within thirty years through a combination of a sinking fund, post-office revenues, and careful management. Washington’s response was heartfelt: he told Hamilton that “I cannot suffer you to close your public service without expressing my thanks for your able and faithful services” and concluded that “in every relation which you have borne to me, I have found that my confidence in your talents, exertions, and integrity has been well placed.” Hamilton’s successor at Treasury, Oliver Wolcott, Jr., had been his protégé and continued to consult him on major policy decisions.

28. Spare Cassius

The remainder of Hamilton’s career was marked by a series of increasingly bitter political battles. He remained the intellectual leader of the Federalist party, but his influence was waning as the party’s internal divisions grew. The presidential election of 1796 saw Washington succeeded by John Adams, who had never liked Hamilton and who increasingly resented his behind-the-scenes influence. Hamilton, for his part, held Adams in low esteem, regarding him as vain, erratic, and dangerously self-centered.

The Adams administration was torn apart by the Quasi-War with France (1798-1800), a naval conflict that brought the two former allies to the brink of war. Hamilton, who had been appointed inspector general of the army with the rank of major general, was determined to build a professional military establishment that would give America the strength to command respect from European powers. He spent much of 1798 and 1799 organizing and training the new army, lobbying Congress for expansion of the military, and clashing with Adams over strategy.

The breach between Hamilton and Adams became irreparable in 1799, when Adams — acting without consulting his cabinet — sent a new peace mission to France, effectively repudiating Hamilton’s military preparations. Hamilton responded with a devastating pamphlet, published in 1800, denouncing Adams as unfit for the presidency. The pamphlet was a catastrophic political error: it split the Federalist party, ensured Jefferson’s election (after the tied electoral vote was resolved in the House), and effectively ended Hamilton’s political career. The election of 1800 — which Jefferson called “the Revolution of 1800” — marked the triumph of Jeffersonian Republicanism and the eclipse of Hamiltonian Federalism.

During the Quasi-War with France (1798-1800), Hamilton was appointed inspector general of the army with the rank of major general, second in command to the aging Washington. He threw himself into the task with characteristic energy, designing a military academy at West Point (a plan that would later materialize), drafting uniforms, writing drill manuals, and personally inspecting troops. He established his headquarters at 39 Broadway in New York and lobbied Congress for an army of 40,000 men, clashing with President Adams, who favored a smaller force. The breach with Adams became irreparable when Adams sent a peace mission to France in February 1799 without consulting Hamilton or the cabinet, effectively ending the military buildup. Hamilton’s pamphlet attacking Adams in 1800 — the Letter Concerning the Public Conduct and Character of John Adams — ran to fifty-four pages and catalogued the president’s alleged “vanity without bounds” and “eccentric and violent” temper. The pamphlet split the Federalist party and ensured the election of Jefferson, an outcome Hamilton regretted but had helped to bring about.

29. The Man in the Glass Bubble

With the election of Jefferson to the presidency in 1801, Hamilton retired from public life more or less permanently. He returned to his law practice with renewed energy, taking on a wide range of cases and becoming one of the most successful lawyers in New York. He also devoted considerable energy to his family, building a country house called the Grange in upper Manhattan, designed in the Federal style, where he hoped to create a pastoral retreat for his wife and children. The Grange was Hamilton’s dream of a settled, domestic existence — the kind of life he had never known as a child.

But Hamilton could not entirely resist the pull of politics. He continued to write pseudonymous essays for the New-York Evening Post, criticizing Jefferson’s policies and warning against the dangers of “Jacobinism.” He remained a towering figure in the popular imagination, alternately revered and reviled. His enemies, now in power, continued to hound him: Jefferson’s administration investigated his conduct as treasury secretary once again, and the Reynolds scandal was periodically resurrected in the press. Hamilton was, in Chernow’s phrase, “a man in a glass bubble” — his every action scrutinized, his every word quoted, his reputation subject to constant assault.

The period was also marked by personal tragedy. In November 1801, Hamilton’s eldest son, Philip, a brilliant nineteen-year-old recent graduate of Columbia College, was killed in a duel with a young New York lawyer named George Eacker. Philip had been defending his father’s honor after Eacker delivered a Fourth of July oration attacking Hamilton. The duel was fought at the same spot in Weehawken, New Jersey, where Hamilton himself would die three years later. Hamilton was devastated by his son’s death, and neither he nor Eliza ever fully recovered from the blow. Philip had been the pride of his father’s life — brilliant, handsome, and promising — and his death was a wound that never healed.

The death of Philip Hamilton was a turning point in Hamilton’s life. It deepened his religious faith, darkened his outlook on human nature, and intensified his already acute sensitivity to questions of honor. It also made him more vulnerable to Burr’s challenge when it came. Having lost one son to a duel, Hamilton knew the cost of honor better than most men. And yet, when Burr demanded satisfaction, Hamilton found that he could not refuse — not because he lacked the courage to say no, but because he lacked the will to live with the consequences of refusal.

Hamilton returned to his law practice in 1795 after resigning from the Treasury, and his legal career flourished as never before. He argued several landmark cases before the New York Supreme Court, including Hollingsworth v. Virginia and United States v. Moore, and was retained by prominent merchants and shipping firms. His most famous post-cabinet case was People v. Crosswell (1804), in which he argued for the importance of truth as a defense in libel cases — a position that laid the groundwork for modern American libel law. In 1801, he began construction of the Grange, a Federal-style country house in the Harlem Heights section of Manhattan, named after the Hamilton ancestral estate in Scotland. The house, designed by Hamilton himself, featured thirteen trees to represent the original states and cost approximately $24,000 to build. His last major public act before the duel was his influential role in the creation of the New-York Evening Post, which began publication on November 16, 1801, with William Coleman as editor. Hamilton personally recruited investors for the paper, which he intended as a bulwark against what he called the “licentiousness of the press” practiced by Republican newspapers.

30. Flying Too Near the Sun

The rivalry between Hamilton and Aaron Burr is one of the great tragedies of American political history. Burr was a man of genuine talent — brilliant, charming, and ambitious — but he was also, in Hamilton’s view, utterly unprincipled. Hamilton had blocked Burr’s political ambitions repeatedly: in 1791, when Burr defeated Philip Schuyler for the Senate; in 1792, when Burr attempted to challenge John Adams for the vice presidency; in 1796, when Hamilton helped deny Burr the vice presidential nomination; and in 1800, when Hamilton’s influence in the House helped throw the presidential election to Jefferson over Burr, despite the fact that Jefferson was Hamilton’s political enemy.

The 1800 election created a permanent enmity between Hamilton and Burr. When the electoral college produced a tie between Jefferson and Burr, the election was thrown into the House of Representatives. Hamilton, despite his intense dislike of Jefferson, used all his influence to ensure that Jefferson was elected, believing that Burr was a dangerous opportunist who would destroy the republic. “If there be a man in the world I ought to hate,” Hamilton wrote, “it is Jefferson. But the public good must be paramount to every private consideration.” He described Burr as “a man of talents of a certain kind, but of a character altogether unfit for the trust” — a judgment that Burr never forgave.

In the following years, Hamilton continued to thwart Burr’s ambitions. In 1801, when Burr hoped to become president (as Jefferson’s successor), Hamilton campaigned against him. In 1804, when Burr ran for governor of New York, Hamilton actively opposed him, and Burr was decisively defeated. It was during this gubernatorial campaign that Burr learned of a remark attributed to Hamilton at a dinner party — that Burr was “a dangerous man” who should not be trusted with power. The remark, which may have been embellished or even invented by Hamilton’s enemies, was the final provocation that pushed Burr toward a fatal confrontation.

The Hamilton-Burr rivalry was rooted in fundamental differences of character. Hamilton was a man of strong convictions, passionate and principled; Burr was an opportunist who seemed to believe in nothing except his own advancement. Hamilton saw Burr as a threat to the republic — a man who would sacrifice the public good to his own ambition. Burr saw Hamilton as the embodiment of everything that had blocked his path to power — the elitist, the moralist, the man who used his influence to destroy others. The duel was the tragic culmination of a conflict that had been building for more than a decade.

The Reynolds pamphlet of 1797 — James Thomson Callender’s The History of the United States for the Year 1796 — exposed the Hamilton-Reynolds affair to the public in sensational detail. Callender, a Scottish radical who had fled to America to avoid prosecution for sedition, had obtained copies of the Reynolds documents from John Beckley, the Republican clerk of the House of Representatives. When Hamilton learned that Callender intended to publish the story, he decided on a strategy of preemptive disclosure. On August 25, 1797, he published Observations on Certain Documents, a ninety-five-page pamphlet that reproduced the Reynolds correspondence and confessed in excruciating detail to his eighteen-month adulterous affair with Maria Reynolds, while emphatically denying — truthfully — any official corruption or misuse of Treasury funds. The pamphlet was a catastrophic error in judgment: it satisfied nobody, convinced nobody of his innocence, and ensured that the scandal would never die. Jefferson wrote to Madison that the pamphlet had “completely demolished” Hamilton’s reputation. Even Hamilton’s friends were aghast: Fisher Ames called it “the most unexpected and shocking event I have known.”

31. An Instrument of Hell

Hamilton’s attitude toward dueling was complex and deeply contradictory. He had been involved in or connected to numerous affairs of honor throughout his career, and he had served as a second in several duels. He had also written extensively against the practice. In 1801, after Philip’s death in the Eacker duel, Hamilton had resolved never to fight a duel himself. But his son’s death also deepened his sensitivity to questions of honor, and he found it impossible to reject a challenge without appearing cowardly.

When Burr’s challenge came in June 1804, Hamilton was placed in an impossible position. The “interview” — as duels were euphemistically called — was the result of a long chain of mutual provocations. Burr had demanded satisfaction for a remark that Hamilton had allegedly made at a dinner party in 1804, suggesting that Burr was a dangerous man unworthy of public trust. Hamilton, who could not deny having expressed such an opinion of Burr, was trapped. To accept the challenge was to risk death or the loss of his reputation if he killed Burr. To refuse was to destroy his honor and his political effectiveness forever.

Hamilton spent his last weeks putting his affairs in order. He wrote a farewell letter to Eliza, his will, and a statement explaining his intentions — a document that Chernow calls his “Apologia.” In it, Hamilton stated that he had resolved to throw away his first fire, deliberately missing Burr, in the hope that Burr would do likewise and that the affair would end without bloodshed. He also expressed his religious faith and his hope for forgiveness. “Conscious of my own innocence,” he wrote, “I trust in the mercy of the Almighty.” He went to the meeting at Weehawken on the morning of July 11, 1804, accompanied by his friend Nathaniel Pendleton.

Hamilton’s written “Apologia,” prepared in the weeks before the duel, is one of the most remarkable documents in American history. In it, Hamilton stated his determination to “reserve and throw away my first fire” and his hope that Burr, seeing that Hamilton meant him no harm, would “also reserve his fire.” Hamilton wrote that he had “a strong repugnance to taking the life of another” and that his religious principles forbade dueling. Yet he also acknowledged that “what men of the world denominate honor” made it impossible for him to refuse the challenge. The document was sealed and entrusted to his friend Nathaniel Pendleton, with instructions that it be made public only if Hamilton died. It was published after his death and served as his final testament. In the same period, Hamilton wrote farewell letters to his wife Eliza and his children, settling his financial affairs and asking his executors — including Robert Troup and Rufus King — to provide for his family. His estate was deeply encumbered by debt; the Grange, his beloved country house, was valued at 15,000butcarriedamortgageof7,000.

32. Reign of Witches

The duel itself was over in seconds. Hamilton and Burr took their positions ten paces apart. At the signal, Hamilton fired first — and, as he had promised, his shot went wild, striking a branch above Burr’s head. Burr paused for a moment, then fired. His bullet struck Hamilton in the lower abdomen, shattering his liver and lodging in his spine. Hamilton crumpled to the ground, his pistol discharging involuntarily as he fell. “I am a dead man,” he whispered to his second.

Burr was immediately hustled away by his own second, William P. Van Ness, and fled to New Jersey (and later to South Carolina) to avoid prosecution for murder. Hamilton was carried back across the Hudson to the home of his friend William Bayard on Jane Street in Greenwich Village, where he spent the next thirty-one hours in excruciating pain. He was attended by Dr. David Hosack, who had witnessed the duel from a nearby hiding place. Hamilton’s wife Eliza, his seven surviving children, and his friends gathered at the house to say their farewells.

Hamilton’s death was an event of national mourning. Newspapers across the country published black-bordered editions, and eulogies poured forth from Federalist and even some Republican papers. The funeral procession in New York City was the largest in American history up to that time, with thousands of citizens lining the streets. The New York Common Council voted to wear mourning badges for six weeks. Even his enemies expressed regret: Jefferson, in a letter to his daughter, noted only that Hamilton “was a man of great talents” before moving on to other topics. The nation had lost one of its most brilliant and controversial founders.

33. Works Godly and Ungodly

The duel with Burr was the culmination of a life marked by extraordinary achievement and equally extraordinary self-destruction. Chernow devotes considerable attention to Hamilton’s last years, which were marked by a deepening religious faith and a turn toward works of Christian charity. Hamilton had always been a man of faith — he had served as a vestryman at Trinity Church and regularly attended services — but in the aftermath of Philip’s death and his own narrow escape from the yellow fever epidemic, his religious convictions grew more intense.

Hamilton also devoted himself to educational causes. He had been a trustee of Columbia College since 1784, and he helped to found the New York Manumission Society, which worked for the abolition of slavery in New York State. He used his legal skills to defend the rights of free blacks and to advance the cause of gradual emancipation. He was also instrumental in the founding of Hamilton College in upstate New York, originally chartered as the Hamilton-Oneida Academy, which was intended to educate both white and Native American students.

But even in his final years, Hamilton could not escape controversy. His involvement in the Reynolds scandal, which had been revived by James Callender in 1797, continued to damage his reputation. The Observations on Certain Documents, in which Hamilton had confessed to the adultery, was a disastrous miscalculation: it satisfied nobody, convinced nobody of his innocence, and only deepened the public’s conviction that something was rotten in the Treasury Department. Hamilton had hoped that full disclosure would end the matter, but he had underestimated the power of scandal.

34. In an Evil Hour

Hamilton’s relationship with President John Adams was one of the most disastrous in American political history. Adams had been vice president under Washington and was elected president in 1796, but he never commanded the respect or loyalty that Washington had enjoyed. Hamilton, who had never held elective office and who had been out of the cabinet for two years, nonetheless continued to exercise enormous influence over Adams’s cabinet — most of whom remained loyal to Hamilton rather than to the president.

The breach between Hamilton and Adams became irreparable during the Quasi-War crisis of 1798-99. Adams, fearing war with France, sent a peace mission to Paris against the advice of his cabinet, effectively repudiating Hamilton’s military preparations. Hamilton responded by publishing a pamphlet, Letter Concerning the Public Conduct and Character of John Adams, Esq., President of the United States, in which he launched a devastating personal attack on the president. The pamphlet, Chernow argues, was one of the greatest political blunders in American history — it split the Federalist party, ensured Jefferson’s election, and destroyed any chance Hamilton had of ever attaining the presidency.

The election of 1800 was the death knell of Hamiltonian Federalism. The Federalists never held the presidency again after Adams’s defeat, and the party’s influence waned steadily in the years that followed. Hamilton, who had sacrificed his own party to prevent Burr from becoming president, was left with nothing but the wreckage of his ambitions. He wrote to Gouverneur Morris in the aftermath of the election: “The Constitution is a frail and worthless fabric. I will never again stake my reputation upon the virtue of the people.”

35. Gusts of Passion

Hamilton’s temper — his “gusts of passion” — was both his driving force and his undoing. Throughout his career, he was prone to outbursts of rage that alienated friends and emboldened enemies. His quarrel with Washington in 1781, his break with Madison over the assumption bill, his newspaper war with Jefferson, his open contempt for Adams — all of these can be traced to a temperament that was incapable of restraint and indifferent to consequences.

Chernow argues that Hamilton’s passionate nature was inextricably linked to his genius. The same fierce energy that drove him to write The Federalist Papers in six months, to produce the state papers that created the American financial system, and to organize an army in the space of a few months also drove him into the Reynolds affair, into the pamphlet war with Adams, and finally into the fatal meeting at Weehawken. He was, in Chernow’s phrase, “a man of deep and often ungovernable emotions” who never learned to control the fires that burned within him.

Hamilton’s relationships with women illustrate this duality. His love for Eliza was genuine and enduring, but it coexisted with a sexual compulsion that led him into the disastrous affair with Maria Reynolds. His friendship with Angelica Church was marked by a flirtatiousness that has led to centuries of speculation about whether the relationship was ever consummated. Even after the Reynolds affair had nearly destroyed his marriage, Hamilton continued to display a susceptibility to female charm that his friend Gouverneur Morris wryly noted.

36. In a Very Belligerent Humor

The election of 1804 — the year of Hamilton’s death — found Hamilton in what Chernow calls “a very belligerent humor.” The Federalist party was in steep decline, and Hamilton was increasingly marginalized even within his own party. His opposition to Burr’s candidacy for governor of New York was the final act in a long campaign to block Burr’s advancement. Hamilton wrote letters to fellow Federalists warning that Burr was “an unprincipled man” who would do anything to advance his ambitions.

Burr’s defeat in the gubernatorial election of 1804 was the breaking point. Burr knew that Hamilton had been instrumental in his defeat, and he brooded over the many slights and injuries he had suffered at Hamilton’s hands over the previous decade and a half. When he learned of the dinner-party remark in which Hamilton had allegedly described him as “a dangerous man,” Burr decided that the time had come to demand satisfaction.

The correspondence between Burr and Hamilton in the weeks leading up to the duel reveals the tortured logic of the code of honor. Burr demanded an apology for the “despicable opinion” that Hamilton had expressed of him; Hamilton, who could not deny having expressed such an opinion, could not apologize without sacrificing his own honor. The exchange of letters grew increasingly acrimonious, and both men knew that the only possible resolution was a meeting on the “field of honor.” Hamilton, despite his principles, could not bring himself to refuse.

37. Deadlock

The correspondence preceding the duel was a masterpiece of indirection and mutual provocation. Burr, writing to Hamilton, demanded an apology for the “injurious expressions” that Hamilton had used against him. Hamilton, in his response, tried to evade the challenge by suggesting that his remarks about Burr had been about Burr’s political, not personal, character. But Burr was not satisfied: he insisted that Hamilton’s “studied and repeated slanders” had injured his honor and required a full retraction.

Hamilton’s friends — including Robert Troup, Nathaniel Pendleton, and Rufus King — tried desperately to find a way out of the deadlock. They suggested that Hamilton could deny having used the offensive words; they proposed that Burr’s letter be interpreted as a general statement of dissatisfaction rather than a specific challenge; they even considered whether a duel could be avoided by having Hamilton sign a statement disavowing any intention to slander Burr’s character. But none of these expedients worked.

The negotiations reached an impasse. Burr’s letters grew increasingly intransigent, and Hamilton’s position became untenable. Hamilton believed that any apology would be taken as an admission of cowardice, and he could not bring himself to make one. “It is impossible for me,” he wrote, “to give any explanation that would satisfy you. I can only appeal to your own knowledge of the facts.” Burr, who knew perfectly well that Hamilton could not retract without losing face, pressed the matter to its fatal conclusion.

38. A World Full of Folly

Hamilton spent his final days in a state of remarkable composure. He wrote his will, penned farewell letters to his wife and children, and settled his financial affairs. He also prepared a written statement (his “Apologia”) in which he explained his decision to participate in the duel and his intention to throw away his shot. The document reveals Hamilton’s profound religious faith and his conviction that he was acting in accordance with divine will.

On the morning of July 11, 1804, Hamilton rose early and went to his office to make final arrangements. He then met with several friends, including Nathaniel Pendleton, who would serve as his second. At about seven o’clock, Hamilton, Pendleton, and Dr. David Hosack rowed across the Hudson River to Weehawken, New Jersey — the same spot where Hamilton’s son Philip had been killed three years earlier. They arrived to find Burr and his second, William Van Ness, already on the ground.

The accounts of what happened next are contradictory. What is certain is that both men fired, and that Hamilton fell. Hamilton’s shot went wild; Burr’s struck him in the abdomen. Hamilton is said to have exclaimed, “I am a dead man” as he fell. He was taken back across the river to the home of William Bayard, where he received the last rites of the Episcopal Church and died the following afternoon. His last words were, “I have a tender reliance on the mercy of the Almighty.”

39. Pamphlet Wars

The death of Alexander Hamilton did not end the war over his reputation. In the immediate aftermath of the duel, Burr was vilified as a murderer and forced to flee New York to avoid prosecution. But as the years passed, the Jeffersonian version of American history — which portrayed Hamilton as a monarchist, an elitist, and a would-be Caesar — gained ascendancy. Jefferson, who outlived Hamilton by twenty-two years, devoted considerable energy to shaping the historical record, and his version of events has proved remarkably durable.

The duel also had profound consequences for the code of honor in American society. The deaths of both Philip and Alexander Hamilton in duels at the same spot in Weehawken led to a growing public revulsion against the practice. In the years after 1804, state after state passed laws against dueling, and the practice gradually died out in the North. But it survived in the South and West for another half-century, claiming many more victims.

Hamilton’s reputation has experienced a remarkable revival in recent decades. The publication of his complete papers in the late twentieth century, along with biographies like Chernow’s, has rehabilitated his image and restored him to his rightful place among the first rank of the founders. The musical Hamilton, which premiered in 2015 and was based partly on Chernow’s biography, introduced Hamilton to a new generation as a figure of almost mythic proportions.

40. The Price of Truth

Hamilton had always insisted on the importance of truth — or at least, of what he believed to be the truth — regardless of the consequences. In his political essays, in his legal arguments, and in his personal life, he displayed a compulsive need to set the record straight, to correct false impressions, to tell the story as he saw it. This relentless honesty — or, as his enemies would have it, this compulsive self-justification — was both his greatest strength and his most damaging weakness.

The Observations on Certain Documents, in which Hamilton confessed to the Reynolds affair, is the most striking example of this compulsion. No other founder would have responded to a sex scandal by publishing a ninety-five-page pamphlet detailing every sordid detail of his adultery. Hamilton insisted that this was the only honorable course — that he had a duty to “remove the veil” from the truth. But his critics, then and since, have seen the pamphlet as evidence of an almost pathological lack of discretion.

The same compulsion drove Hamilton’s newspaper wars with Jefferson and Adams. He could not let an attack go unanswered, could not let a false statement stand uncorrected, could not refrain from publishing his views in the most emphatic possible terms. “He did not know when to stop,” Chernow writes, “and the resulting excesses led him into irremediable indiscretions.” It was this quality, as much as any other, that ensured that Hamilton would never be president and that his reputation would suffer in the historical judgment of his enemies.

41. A Despicable Opinion

The fatal remark that led to the duel with Burr was, Chernow argues, the product of a long history of mutual antagonism. Hamilton had been blocking Burr’s ambitions for fifteen years, and Burr had finally decided that enough was enough. The remark itself — that Burr was “a dangerous man” who should not be trusted with power — was mild by the standards of the political invective of the 1790s. But it was the culmination of a long pattern of slights and injuries that Burr could no longer tolerate.

Burr’s decision to challenge Hamilton to a duel was a carefully calculated act. He knew that Hamilton could not refuse a challenge without losing the honor that was essential to his identity. He also knew that Hamilton was morally opposed to dueling and would therefore have a psychological disadvantage. And he knew that the legal consequences of the duel — for the survivor — would be severe. By forcing Hamilton to the field of honor, Burr was not just defending his own reputation; he was exacting a terrible revenge for a decade and a half of political humiliation.

The duel destroyed Burr’s career. He was indicted for murder in both New York and New Jersey, and he fled to South Carolina to avoid prosecution. He spent the next several years in a series of increasingly desperate schemes — the Burr conspiracy, the attempt to carve out an empire in the Southwest — that culminated in his trial for treason in 1807. He was acquitted, but his reputation was ruined, and he spent the rest of his life in obscurity and disgrace. He died in 1836, having outlived his famous antagonist by thirty-two years.

42. Fatal Errand

The journey to Weehawken on the morning of July 11, 1804, was the final chapter of a life that had been marked by extraordinary drama. Hamilton’s last hours were a testament to his character: he made his peace with God, wrote his farewells, and faced his death with the same composure that had marked his conduct on the battlefield.

The medical treatment Hamilton received after the duel was primitive by modern standards. Dr. Hosack, who had been hiding in the bushes during the duel, rushed to his side and administered a mixture of opium and wine. Hamilton was rowed back across the river and taken to the home of William Bayard, where he was placed on a sofa in the parlor. The bullet had shattered his liver and lodged in his spine, causing excruciating pain. Hosack later recalled that Hamilton bore the suffering “with great fortitude and composure.”

Eliza was summoned, and the family gathered for a final farewell. Hamilton asked for the Episcopal communion and received the last rites. He died at approximately two o’clock in the afternoon on July 12, 1804, less than thirty-one hours after being shot. He was forty-nine years old. His last coherent words were addressed to Eliza, who had been brought to his bedside: “Remember, my Eliza, you are a Christian.” He had made his peace with God and with the world.

43. The Melting Scene

Hamilton’s funeral was the largest and most elaborate ever seen in New York City. The Common Council voted to wear mourning badges for six weeks; all flags were lowered to half-staff; and the ships in the harbor fired minute guns. The funeral procession, which stretched for more than a mile, included representatives of every branch of government, the clergy, the bar, the military, and the city’s various civic and charitable organizations. It was, Chernow writes, “a melting scene” of public grief that testified to the extraordinary impact Hamilton had made on the city and the nation.

The reaction to Hamilton’s death was not, however, universal in its grief. Jeffersonian newspapers in the South celebrated the demise of the “monarchist” and the “Angloman.” The Richmond Examiner expressed the hope that Hamilton’s death would “convince our citizens that the system of measures which has so long convulsed the nation was never so much the offspring of the head of the President as of his late worthy and subaltern Secretary.” But even Hamilton’s enemies acknowledged the magnitude of the loss.

Hamilton’s legacy was contested from the moment of his death. The Federalist party, deprived of its intellectual leader, quickly fragmented and disappeared as a national force within a decade of his death. The Jeffersonian Republicans, who dominated American politics for the next quarter-century, wrote the history of the period in their own image. Hamilton was reduced, in the popular imagination, to a caricature — the aristocratic pretender, the tool of monied interests, the would-be Caesar. It would take nearly two centuries for historians to begin the work of recovering the full complexity of his character and achievements.

Epilogue: Eliza

Elizabeth Schuyler Hamilton survived her husband by half a century, dying on November 9, 1854, at the age of ninety-seven. She had devoted the remaining fifty years of her life to preserving Alexander Hamilton’s legacy — collecting his papers, commissioning biographies, and defending his reputation against the slings and arrows of partisan historians. She wore mourning clothes for the rest of her life, never remarried, and was always referred to, even in old age, as “Mrs. Hamilton.” Her devotion was legendary: she had kept a tiny cloth bag containing fragments of the farewell note Hamilton had written on the eve of his duel, and she often showed visitors the marble bust of her husband by Giuseppe Ceracchi, before which she placed fresh flowers daily.

Eliza lived long enough to see her children grow up and to witness the transformation of the United States from a fragile coastal republic to a continental power. She was present at the cornerstone-laying of the Washington Monument in 1848, and she was visited by many of the great figures of the mid-nineteenth century, including Millard Fillmore, who escorted her to the White House. She also lived long enough to see the beginnings of the historical rehabilitation of her husband’s reputation. She had spent decades trying to commission a definitive biography, a task that ultimately fell to her son John Church Hamilton, whose seven-volume hagiography was less a work of scholarship than an act of filial piety.

In her final years, Eliza lived with her daughter, also named Eliza Hamilton Holly, in a house on H Street in Washington, D.C., near the White House. She was nearly blind and deaf but remained mentally sharp. She continued to receive visitors and to speak of her husband in the present tense. Her most poignant recorded words, spoken as she was playing backgammon one night, were these: “I am so tired. It is so long. I want to see Hamilton.” She was buried next to her husband and her son Philip in the graveyard of Trinity Church in lower Manhattan, where she had finally rejoined the family she had spent so many years preserving in memory.

The story of Eliza Hamilton is a coda to the story of her husband — a testament to the enduring power of the love that had bound them together, and to the resilience of a woman who refused to let her husband’s memory be destroyed by his enemies. Chernow’s biography is in many ways a tribute to Eliza as well as to Hamilton: without her efforts, many of Hamilton’s papers would have been lost, and the man himself might have been remembered only through the distortions of his political opponents. The biography is, finally, an act of justice — the justice that Eliza demanded, that her daughter demanded, and that generations of historians have sought to deliver.
