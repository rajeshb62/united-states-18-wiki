[source] Benjamin Franklin: Une vie américaine

Title (original): Benjamin Franklin: An American Life Author: Walter Isaacson Translator (French edition): Mathieu Fleury Publisher (French edition): Alisio, 2017 Format: EPUB Language: French (translation)

Description

Walter Isaacson’s biographical study of Benjamin Franklin (1706–1790), translated from the original English into French as Benjamin Franklin: Une vie américaine. The work traces Franklin’s life from his Boston childhood, through his rise as a printer and publisher in Philadelphia, his scientific experiments (electricity, the lightning rod, the Franklin stove), his political career in colonial Pennsylvania, his decade-long missions to London and Paris, his central role in the American Revolution and the drafting of the Declaration of Independence, and his diplomatic triumph in securing the French alliance. The biography concludes with Franklin’s service at the Constitutional Convention and his final years.

This is a modern work of synthesis (original English edition 2003; French translation 2017), not a period or near-contemporary source. It is included in this wiki because of its comprehensive coverage and use of primary source material including the Yale edition of Franklin’s Papers.

Main Text

Chapitre 1: Benjamin Franklin et l’invention de l’Amérique / Benjamin Franklin and the

Invention of America

Isaacson opens with a portrait of Franklin as the most approachable and human of the Founding Fathers — a figure who, unlike the marble dignity of Washington or the intellectual austerity of Jefferson and Adams, “makes a wink at us across the centuries.” The chapter frames Franklin as America’s first great publicist, a man who spent his life carefully crafting his own persona: the industrious young printer hauling paper through Philadelphia streets so everyone would see his work ethic, the rustic philosopher wearing a fur cap in Paris to project New World simplicity, and the self-described “B. Franklin, Printer” who never forgot his artisan origins even when dining with kings. Isaacson argues that Franklin’s most interesting creation was himself — a new archetype of the American citizen.

The chapter establishes the central tension that runs through the biography: Franklin represents the pragmatic, middle-class, democratic strand of the American character, standing in contrast to the religious fervor of Jonathan Edwards and the Calvinist tradition. Franklin’s moral philosophy was built around practical questions of how ordinary people could live useful, virtuous, and meaningful lives. Isaacson previews Franklin’s signature achievements — the lightning rod, bifocals, the Franklin stove, public libraries, volunteer fire departments, the Albany Plan for colonial union, the French alliance, and the compromise that saved the Constitutional Convention — while noting that his most significant legacy is the set of middle-class values (industry, frugality, honesty, civic engagement) that became central to the American identity.

The chapter also explores the tension between the romantic myth of emigration for religious freedom and the economic realities that drove families like the Franklins across the Atlantic. Franklin himself, analyzing his father Josiah’s motives, concluded that “the love of independence, combined with intellectual vigor and practical details,” mattered as much as doctrinal concerns. The book’s French title, “Une vie américaine,” signals Isaacson’s core argument: Franklin’s life was not just an American story but the American story — the self-made man, the practical genius, the democratic citizen who believed in the wisdom of ordinary people.

Isaacson further explores how Franklin’s vision of America was built on a distinctly middle-class ethic that rejected both the aristocratic pretensions of Europe and the theocratic dogmatism of Puritan New England. Franklin’s philosophy, Isaacson writes, “was not a quest for salvation or transcendence, but for a useful, virtuous life in this world.” This pragmatic morality — emphasizing industry, frugality, honesty, and civic engagement — became the blueprint for a new kind of democratic citizenship. Franklin’s “way to wealth” was also a way to social harmony: he believed that a society of independent, self-improving individuals would naturally tend toward justice and prosperity. Isaacson highlights the paradox that Franklin, often dismissed as a mere materialist, was in fact deeply concerned with moral and spiritual questions, but approached them through the lens of utility rather than revelation. His religion was one of good works, not divine grace, which placed him squarely in the Enlightenment tradition and made him a target for Romantics who craved spiritual depth.

Chapitre 2: Voyage d’un pèlerin, Boston, 1706–1723 / Pilgrim’s Journey

The narrative traces Franklin’s origins to the village of Ecton in Northamptonshire, England, where his forebears had been small farmers and tradesmen for three centuries. The name “Franklin” itself derives from the Middle English “frankelyen,” meaning a free man of the independent middle class — neither aristocrat nor serf. Franklin’s earliest documented ancestor, Thomas Francklyne (born ca. 1540), kept his English Bible hidden under a chair during Queen Mary I’s bloody campaign to restore Catholicism, overturning the chair on his knee to read aloud and flipping it back to hide the forbidden text at the approach of an informer. This dissenting lineage — practical, defiant, and resourceful — shaped Franklin’s instinctive resistance to established authority. Thomas’s son Henry spent a year in prison for writing a poem that “offended a man of importance.” The next generation, Thomas II, was a blacksmith who also dabbled as a wood-turner, gunsmith, writer, and amateur astronomer, building a clock that ran until his death. The Franklins were artisans and readers, social and civic-minded, a family that produced dissenters but not zealots.

His father Josiah, a dyer and tallow chandler, emigrated to Boston in August 1683 at age twenty-five with his wife Anne Child and their three children, paying £15 for a nine-week frigate passage — the equivalent of six months of a laborer’s wages. In Boston, Josiah set up soap and candle-making on Milk Street, a shrewd choice: only one tallow merchant was recorded in the Boston register before his arrival. The family lived in a two-story clapboard house measuring just twenty-seven by eighteen feet, facing the South Church, Boston’s most liberal Puritan congregation. Josiah rose in the community, becoming a deacon — a moral police responsible for ensuring faithful attendance at Sunday services and watching for “night prowlers, drunkards, Sabbath-breakers” — and later a constable, one of eleven men charged with oversight. He sold candles to the watchmen he supervised, an early instance of the Franklin talent for combining public virtue and private profit. Benjamin’s mother Abiah Folger came from Nantucket Quaker stock; her father Peter Folger was a poet who wrote protest verse against the Massachusetts theocracy in both English and the Algonquin language. Peter Folger had led a rebellion against the colony’s Puritan leaders after they attempted to disarm Nantucket’s residents, a defiant strain that Benjamin would inherit.

Benjamin, born on January 17, 1706 (Old Style), was the youngest son of the youngest son for five generations, a pattern that pushed him toward self-reliance since younger sons could not inherit. His early education at Boston Latin School was cut short after just one year when Josiah, calculating that a Harvard education for a minister’s career would cost too much for too little return, transferred him to George Brownell’s school for writing and arithmetic. Young Ben showed an early talent for practical jokes and athletic feats — he once led other boys into the marshes near Mill Pond to steal building stones for a wharf they built for fishing, and when caught, argued that the utility of the wharf justified the appropriation. He also invented a method of swimming using kite power, lying on his back and letting a kite pull him across a pond, a precursor to his lifelong fascination with harnessing natural forces. The chapter details his childhood apprenticeship to his older brother James, a fierce-tempered printer who founded the New England Courant, one of America’s first independent newspapers. Young Benjamin secretly contributed the “Silence Dogood” letters — satirical essays written under a widow’s pseudonym — which became an instant sensation. When James discovered the ruse, he beat his brother, deepening a rift that would never fully heal. Isaacson explores how this fraternal conflict taught Franklin two lasting lessons: the injustice of arbitrary authority and the power of writing with ironic disguise.

The chapter also recounts the famous “whistle” story: when a seven-year-old Benjamin, delighted by the sound of a friend’s whistle, paid all his copper coins for it, only to discover he had paid four times its value. His brother’s mockery taught him never to pay more than something was worth — a lesson he recalled at age seventy when reflecting on those who sacrifice friendships, pleasures, or peace of mind for superficial gains. The chapter concludes with Franklin’s escape from Boston at age seventeen, boarding a ship for New York with little more than the coins his father had lent him, having sold his books to raise cash. He had begun the “pilgrim’s journey” that would define his life.

Chapitre 3: Artisan, Philadelphie et Londres, 1723–1726 / Craftsman

Franklin arrived in Philadelphia in October 1723, a grubby runaway with three loaves of bread tucked under each arm, and was spotted by a young woman named Deborah Read standing in her family’s doorway — amused by his grotesque appearance. “She thought I made most awkward, ridiculous appearance,” Franklin would later recall, “and she laughed at me.” This scene, recounted in his Autobiography decades later, captures the mythic quality of his rise from obscurity. The chapter follows his early employment under Samuel Keimer, an eccentric, disorganized printer with a long beard who insisted on composing type for a whole elegy before breakfast. Keimer was a man of peculiar religious views who observed the Sabbath on the seventh day and wore his beard long in the Hebrew fashion; Franklin, always pragmatic, pretended to adopt Keimer’s sectarian diet while secretly devising rationalizations to abandon it. Franklin’s growing circle included James Ralph, a brilliant poet whose writing he helped get published, and John Collins, a sober journeyman companion. His charisma and intellectual curiosity soon drew the attention of Governor Sir William Keith, who flattered the young printer with promises of patronage.

Keith sent Franklin to London with letters of credit that never materialized — leaving the eighteen-year-old stranded in a strange city with empty pockets. Yet Franklin turned the disaster into an education, spending eighteen months working at Palmer’s and then Watts’s printing houses, where he gained advanced technical skills and observed the workings of a sophisticated commercial metropolis. He became a vegetarian to save money for books, describing how the sight of cod being cut open and smaller fish found in their stomachs led him to rationalize: if fish eat other fish, humans can eat fish — “so convenient a thing it is to be a reasonable creature.” At Watts’s printing house, Franklin found the workers drank prodigious amounts of beer, believing it necessary for strength; he persuaded them he was stronger on water and bread alone, a lesson in the power of temperance over custom. In London he fathered an illegitimate son, William, about whose mother the identity remains obscure — some historians speculate she was a servant woman, others a woman of higher station whose name was suppressed. Franklin raised the boy himself, sending him to the best schools and eventually securing him the governorship of New Jersey — a relationship that would end in bitter political estrangement during the Revolution.

The chapter ends with Franklin’s return voyage to Philadelphia in 1726, where he kept a “Plan of Conduct” journal revealing his determination to methodically build a life of industry, frugality, and moral purpose. The journal’s entries — organized around virtues and duties to order, resolution, moderation, and sincerity — foreshadow the systematic self-improvement program of his later years. Isaacson emphasizes that this formative period, from runaway apprentice to seasoned journeyman, established the self-discipline and practical philosophy that would guide Franklin’s entire career. He had learned that the world did not owe him anything; success would have to be created, not assumed. One of the journal’s most striking entries anticipates his entire moral system: “I resolved to speak the truth in every instance, to seek the useful in all things, and to live with sincerity toward all men.” This disciplined self-examination, written at age twenty, reveals the astonishing clarity of purpose that would guide Franklin through the next six decades of his life.

Chapitre 4: Imprimeur, Philadelphie, 1726–1732 / Printer

Back in Philadelphia, Franklin briefly worked as a shopkeeper for the merchant Thomas Denham, learning accounts and customer relations. But Denham’s sudden death in 1728 threw him back into the printing trade with Keimer. Recognizing his own superior abilities and Keimer’s incompetence, Franklin planned a secret defection with Hugh Meredith, a journeyman who had money but little talent. Franklin became the first American to cast type — a font he would later call Franklin Gothic. When Keimer discovered the plot, he cursed Franklin roundly; Franklin, in characteristic style, quoted a Biblical verse about the patience of Job and walked out. The partnership with Meredith proved precarious — Meredith drank heavily — but Franklin’s energy and reputation soon attracted the colony’s official printing contracts, including the government’s business. In 1730, he bought out Meredith and became sole proprietor of his own printing shop.

The same year, he began publishing the Pennsylvania Gazette, which under his editorship became the most successful newspaper in the colonies, known for its wit, clear prose, and occasional satirical hoaxes. Franklin’s journalistic style was a deliberate contrast with both his brother James’s combative Courant and Andrew Bradford’s stuffy American Weekly Mercury. He refused to put his name on the masthead, preferring to let his writing speak for itself, and cultivated a persona of reasonable moderation. The chapter details Franklin’s social rise through the Junto, a mutual-improvement club he founded that became the seedbed of his civic projects. Members swore an oath to be “sincere, just, and impartial” in their debates, and discussions followed a strict format of questions — “Have you observed any defect in the laws of your country?” — designed to encourage useful knowledge over abstract speculation. The Junto met every Friday evening in a local tavern, and members were required to present at least one essay per quarter on any subject of moral, political, or natural The Junto’s model of collective self-improvement became Franklin’s template for every civic institution he would later create. The original twelve members represented every trade in the city — a printer, a surveyor, a shoemaker, a cabinetmaker, a merchant, a clerk, a bailiff, and several others — demonstrating Franklin’s belief that useful knowledge was not the province of the educated elite alone. Each meeting began with a series of standing questions designed to stimulate practical thinking: “Have you met with any thing in the author you last read, remarkable, or suitable to be communicated to the Junto?” and “Has any citizen in your way lately done any thing worth remembering?” The Junto’s influence extended far beyond Franklin’s immediate circle; it was the prototype for the American Philosophical Society, subscription libraries, and countless other voluntary associations.

His courtship of Deborah Read culminated in a common-law marriage in 1730 arrangement, as Deborah was “as well as I could wish.” The marriage endured for forty-four years, marked by genuine partnership and affection but also by long separations. Deborah ran the printing shop during Franklin’s absences, managing accounts and supplies with competence that Franklin openly acknowledged. In 1732, Franklin launched Poor Richard’s Almanack, writing under the pseudonym Richard Saunders. The almanac became an instant phenomenon selling ten thousand copies annually — a remarkable number for a colonial population of roughly a million. Its blend of practical advice, weather predictions, and witty maxims — “Early to bed and early to rise makes a man healthy, wealthy, and wise” — established Franklin as America’s first homegrown literary celebrity and made the fortune that would allow him to retire from active business at forty-two. The almanac’s success was built on Franklin’s understanding of his audience: ordinary farmers and tradesmen who appreciated both practical utility and wry humor. The character of Poor Richard — a shiftless, feckless almanac-maker whose wife berates him — was a brilliant comic invention that allowed Franklin to dispense moral advice without seeming preachy.

Chapitre 5: Citoyen d’un peuple, Philadelphie, 1731–1748 / Citizen of a People

With his printing business thriving, Franklin turned to large-scale civic improvement. He believed that “man is a social animal” and that the best way to improve the world was through voluntary associations of ordinary citizens working together. In 1731, he organized the Library Company of Philadelphia, America’s first subscription library, which allowed ordinary tradesmen access to books that had previously been the preserve of the wealthy. Each member contributed forty shillings to start and ten shillings annually — a model that could be replicated in any community. The Library Company became a model for similar institutions throughout the colonies and bore a motto Franklin chose: “To communicate knowledge is to increase it.”

Over the next two decades, Franklin launched or helped create an astonishing array of civic institutions: the Union Fire Company in 1736 (the first volunteer fire department in America, with thirty members who agreed to respond to fires with leather buckets and cloth bags for salvage), improved night watch and street lighting (oil lamps on public streets, the first such system in the colonies), the Academy of Philadelphia (which became the University of Pennsylvania, chartered in 1749), and the Pennsylvania Hospital (the first hospital in the colonies, chartered in 1751). Each project followed Franklin’s characteristic method: identify a public need, draft a proposal, circulate it quietly among friends, call a public meeting, and let others take the credit. Alexis de Tocqueville would later marvel at this American genius for voluntary associations — a tradition Franklin essentially invented for the new nation. Franklin’s approach was explicitly designed to avoid the appearance of personal ambition: “I therefore put myself as much as I could out of sight, and stated it as a scheme of a number of friends.”

The chapter also explores a darker episode: a Freemason prank involving a staged spectacle that went tragically wrong when a young man named Rees died of cardiac arrest during a mock initiation ceremony. Franklin was accused by rival printer Andrew Bradford of complicity, though he seems to have been only tangentially involved. The incident illustrates both the rough-and-tumble nature of eighteenth-century civic life and the personal rivalries Franklin navigated. Another important episode is the Hemphill affair: Franklin championed the Presbyterian minister Samuel Hemphill, who preached salvation through good works rather than divine grace — a theology that aligned with Franklin’s own deism. When the Presbyterian synod censured Hemphill for heresy, Franklin defended him in pamphlets, accusing the clergy of “malice and envy.” Even after Hemphill was caught plagiarizing his sermons, Franklin refused to abandon him, arguing that borrowed good sermons were preferable to original bad ones. The affair ended Franklin’s attendance at Presbyterian services forever.

Franklin also developed his famous “project for moral perfection” during this period, creating a list of thirteen virtues — temperance, silence, order, resolution, frugality, industry, sincerity, justice, moderation, cleanliness, tranquility, chastity, and humility — and tracking his daily progress with a chart in a small booklet. When a Quaker friend pointed out that Franklin was guilty of pride, he added humility as the thirteenth virtue, wryly noting: “I cannot boast of much success in acquiring the reality of this virtue, but I had some success in acquiring the appearance of it.” The booklet became so full of erasures that he transferred the chart to an ivory tablet, which he would show to French ladies fifty years later, prompting one to exclaim her joy at touching “that precious little tablet.” By 1748, at age forty-two, Franklin turned his printing house over to foreman David Hall under a a profit-sharing agreement so he could devote himself fully to science, politics, and philosophy. He had achieved material independence, social respect, and leisure for higher pursuits — the trifecta of his youthful ambition. Franklin’s retirement from printing was carefully staged: he kept his name on the shop and continued to receive a share of profits, ensuring that his public identity as “B. Franklin, Printer” remained intact even as he devoted himself to scientific experiments and political causes. The arrangement with David Hall was a model of Franklin’s business acumen: Hall would run the shop and receive half the profits, while Franklin continued to own the building and equipment, drawing an annual income of approximately £500 — a comfortable middle-class living that left him free to pursue whatever caught his curiosity.

Chapitre 6: Scientifique et inventeur, Philadelphie, 1744–1751 / Scientist and Inventor

Franklin’s scientific career began in earnest in the mid-1740s, sparked by a demonstration of “electrical fire” by the traveling lecturer Archibald Spencer. Franklin’s insatiable curiosity drove him to acquire the best electrostatic equipment available and to conduct a series of experiments that would transform human understanding of electricity. Working with a network of correspondents across Europe and America linked by the Quaker botanist Peter Collinson, he developed a unified theory: electricity was a single fluid that existed in all matter, with “plus” and “minus” charges — a framework he invented. He coined the term “battery” to describe a linked series of Leyden jars and demonstrated the principle of conservation of charge. His electrical demonstrations became legendary: he killed a turkey with a shock, electrified a fence to amuse friends, and created a mechanical spider that moved between charged wires. In France, electrical experimenters were called “franklinistes.” The turkey-killing experiments were particularly elaborate: Franklin organized a “party of pleasure” on the riverbank where the turkey would be killed by electric shock, roasted on an electrified spit, and the company would drink from electrified cups to the health of “all the famous electricians of England, France, and Germany.” The turkeys proved harder to kill than chickens, requiring a large battery connected in series, but the resulting meat was “remarkably tender,” making Franklin the pioneer of an early form of electric cooking.

The kite experiment of 1752, Isaacson notes, was more controlled than the popular image suggests. Franklin flew the kite from inside a shed, using a key to draw sparks from the string, proving that lightning was electrical. The practical fruit was the lightning rod — a pointed iron conductor that could protect buildings from strikes. The invention made Franklin internationally famous when it was tested successfully in France before he had even performed his own kite experiment. Franklin refused to patent any of his inventions, writing: “As we enjoy great advantages from the inventions of others, we should be glad of an opportunity to serve others by any invention of ours.” His electrical theories were so clearly written that they could be understood by both scientists and laymen; the chemist Sir Humphry Davy later noted that Franklin “wrote for the neophyte as much as for the philosopher, presenting reports as amusing as they were clear.”

The chapter also covers the Franklin stove — a more efficient heating device designed to produce less smoke and fit into existing fireplaces. The stove was engineered with an ingenious system of convection: heat and smoke rose to warm an iron plate, then traveled down through a conduit, along an underground channel, up the chimney and out. Despite the governor of Pennsylvania’s offer of a lucrative patent, Franklin refused it, writing: “As we benefit greatly from the inventions of others, so we should be proud to serve others generously through our own inventions.” The stove initially sold well at £5 each, but the design proved impractical — the underground conduits had to be warm for convection to work, which made lighting difficult — and production ceased within two decades. Today’s “Franklin stove” is a much simplified version of the original. The chapter also covers bifocal glasses (cutting two pairs of lenses horizontally and combining them for both distance and reading), the glass harmonica (a musical instrument of spinning glass bowls that became all the rage in Europe; Mozart and Beethoven composed for it), and his studies of heat conduction by color. Franklin also invented the first urinary catheter used in America, designing a flexible tube with a wire for rigidity during insertion, at his brother John’s request. His studies of storms on the Atlantic coast led him to the discovery that nor’easters actually travel from southwest to northeast — the foundation of modern weather forecasting. He conducted oil-on-water experiments inspired by the Roman naturalist Pliny the Elder, turning them into a parlor trick with a hollow cane that could calm rippling ponds.

Chapitre 7: Politicien, Philadelphie, 1749–1756 / Politician

As Franklin’s scientific reputation grew, so did his political ambitions. He was elected to the Pennsylvania Assembly in 1751 and immediately became the leader of the anti-Proprietary party, which resented the Penn family’s control over the colony and their exemption from taxation. Long before his formal political career, Franklin had shaped public opinion: his 1747 pamphlet “Plain Truth,” published under the persona of “a Tradesman,” rallied the colony to form a voluntary militia against French and Spanish privateers who had sailed up the Delaware River. The move annoyed pacifist Quaker leaders but proved effective when privateers threatened Philadelphia itself. Franklin’s rhetoric appealed directly to the “middling people” who had the most to lose from disorder and the least patience for aristocratic privilege. The militia association Franklin organized enrolled over ten thousand men, elected its own officers, and built batteries along the Delaware River — all without any legislative authorization, a remarkable demonstration of Franklin’s faith in voluntary associations.

The chapter follows Franklin’s political rise through the Albany Congress of 1754, where he proposed the “Albany Plan of Union” — the first serious proposal for unifying the British colonies under a central government with a president-general appointed by the Crown and a grand council elected by the colonial assemblies. To promote the plan, Franklin published his famous “Join, or Die” cartoon of a segmented snake, the first political cartoon in American history. Though the plan was rejected by both colonial assemblies (who feared losing autonomy) and the British Crown (who feared colonial unity), it established Franklin as the leading theorist of colonial union and anticipated the federal structure of the future United States. Franklin’s disappointment at the Albany Plan’s rejection was profound; he later wrote that the colonies’ refusal to unite amounted to “a refusal to accept a government that would have been a great blessing for them,” and he predicted that the alternative would be eventual separation from Britain — a prediction that proved prophetic.

Franklin’s educational vision took shape in his 1749 pamphlet “Propositions for the Education of Youth in Pennsylvania,” proposing a non-sectarian, practical curriculum focused on mathematics, science, history, and modern languages rather than classical Latin and Greek. He raised £2,000 for the Academy of Philadelphia, which opened in the building originally constructed by the evangelist George Whitefield for his revival meetings. Franklin’s appointment as Deputy Postmaster General for the American colonies in 1753 gave him a continent-wide network for correspondence and intelligence that proved invaluable for both his scientific and political work. The chapter does not shy from Franklin’s involvement with slavery: approximately six percent of Philadelphia’s population was enslaved, Franklin himself owned slaves for domestic work (whom he sold in 1751 after concluding they were unproductive), and his newspaper carried advertisements for slave sales. During the French and Indian War, Franklin organized Pennsylvania’s defenses, building forts along the frontier — a practical military effort that contrasted sharply with the pacifism of the colony’s Quaker leadership. He personally commanded the construction of forts on the Lehigh River and devised a system of supply wagons that kept the frontier forces provisioned throughout the campaign.

Chapitre 8: Eaux troubles, Londres, 1757–1762 / Troubled Waters

In 1757, the Pennsylvania Assembly dispatched Franklin to London as its colonial agent to petition the Crown against the Proprietors’ power. The crossing nearly killed him: his ship almost wrecked on the Scilly Isles in fog while evading French privateers. Franklin, grateful to survive, wrote that “if I were a Catholic, I would surely vow to build a chapel there to some saint. But as I am not, if I must make any vow, I vow to build a lighthouse.” He arrived expecting a mission of five months; it stretched to five years. He took lodgings at 36 Craven Street, a three-story house near the Strand in London, with landlady Margaret Stevenson, a sensible widow in her fifties, and her eighteen-year-old daughter Polly. Between them developed an almost familial relationship; London friends treated them as a couple, inviting both to dinner and inquiring after each other’s health in letters. Franklin became deeply attached to the Stevenson household, writing Polly scientific letters on subjects ranging from barometers and heat absorption to the formation of waterspouts, and inventing a phonetic alphabet for her amusement.

Through Peter Collinson — the Quaker botanist who had published Franklin’s electrical letters — he was introduced to the Royal Society, the Honest Whigs club of liberal intellectuals, and a dazzling circle: the physician Sir John Pringle, the printer William Strahan (his warmest London friend), Joseph Priestley (who would later isolate oxygen), and the economist David Hume. The Honest Whigs met at the London coffeehouse on St. Paul’s Churchyard, where they discussed science, politics, and religion over wine and tobacco. Franklin thoroughly enjoyed London’s intellectual society, attending coffeehouse debates and serving as the unofficial spokesman for American interests. He and Polly exchanged more letters than he sent to his own family; she received eight of his scientific letters on barometers, heat absorption by colors, electricity conductivity, and the formation of waterspouts, later published in a revised edition of his electrical writings. He wrote to her in a phonetic alphabet he invented and wore garters she had knitted for him. Through Strahan, Franklin also met Samuel Johnson, though the two great men did not get along — Johnson was a Tory and a skeptic of American pretensions, while Franklin was a Whig and a colonial champion. Franklin also became a regular at the Royal Society’s meetings, where he presented papers on the nature of electricity, heat absorption by colors, and the inefficiency of London street-lighting compared to Philadelphia’s. His scientific reputation grew steadily; the Royal Society awarded him the Copley Medal in 1753 for his “curious experiments and observations on electricity,” making him one of the most honored scientists of his generation.

Meanwhile, his wife Deborah refused to cross the Atlantic, terrified of the sea. Strahan wrote her warning letters about “the temptations of London,” comparing Margaret Stevenson’s attentions unfavorably to a wife’s. Franklin reassured Deborah coolly: “Be satisfied, my dear, that even if I am not a man without sense, God grants me his protection and I will do nothing that does not suit the character of an honest man and one who loves his family.” His cousin-gift shipments to Philadelphia included porcelain, silver ladles, fruit-parers, carpets, curtains (chosen by Mrs. Stevenson), and knitted garters from Polly. Despite his diplomatic efforts, Franklin’s mission against the Penns achieved almost nothing; Thomas Penn had been quietly undermining him at court for years. Franklin’s frustration with the Penns deepened his conviction that the British aristocracy had little regard for colonial interests. He spent his final months in London touring English gardens and country estates, cultivating friendships that would serve him in the turbulent decade to come. When he finally sailed for Philadelphia in 1762, he left with love for London’s intellectual energy mixed with growing resentment at its dismissive treatment of American interests.

Chapitre 9: Les pénates, Philadelphie, 1763–1764 / Home

Franklin returned to Philadelphia in 1763 to a hero’s welcome and immediately threw himself into a 1,600-mile inspection tour of the colonial postal system from Virginia to New Hampshire. He was thrown from his horse, dislocating his shoulder twice, and spent two months convalescing in Boston at the home of his beloved sister Jane Mecom, his lifelong confidante. Jane was Franklin’s closest family connection; their correspondence spanned decades and reveals a warmth and intimacy that Franklin rarely showed others. Despite the pain, he modernized the service, establishing night-riding express routes so that a letter from Philadelphia to Boston could receive a reply within six days, and a packet service to the West Indies. His report demonstrated attention to every practical detail — the condition of each post road, the honesty of each postmaster, the safety of each river crossing. During this tour, Franklin took in the orphaned illegitimate son of his deceased postal partner William Hunter, remarking that as the father of an illegitimate son himself, he knew the hardships such children faced.

The homecoming was bittersweet. His newly built house on Market Street — designed from London with detailed instructions about paint colors, hardware, a copper boiler, and even the cupboard hinges — was comfortable but unfamiliar; he had never lived in it. Deborah had aged and her health was declining. Politically, Franklin found Pennsylvania in turmoil. The Paxton Boys, a vigilante group of Scots-Irish frontiersmen, massacred six peaceful, Christian Conestoga Indians on December 14, 1763, and fourteen more two weeks later — the latter group killed inside a correction house where they had been placed for their own protection. When the Paxton Boys marched on Philadelphia threatening to slaughter the 140 Indians sheltered there, Franklin penned A Narrative of the Late Massacres, calling the killers “barbarians” and asking whether “the color of their skin and the ink of their hair” justified mass murder. The pamphlet remains one of the most moving and morally outraged pieces Franklin ever wrote. He distributed it throughout the city, using his printing skills to rally public opinion against the vigilantes, and helped organize the defenses alongside Governor John Penn.

He organized the city’s defenses alongside Governor John Penn, who ran to Franklin’s door at midnight for counsel and even offered Franklin control of the militia. Franklin refused the command but took a musket. When Penn later pardoned the Paxton Boys and offered bounties for Indian scalps, Franklin’s outrage became implacable: “These horrors render his government and his person most contemptible.” The clash between Franklin and the Paxton Boys represented the collision of two Americas — Franklin’s cosmopolitan, Enlightenment- inspired vision of a tolerant, orderly society versus the violent frontier individualism of the Scots-Irish settlers. The Assembly voted to send him back to London in 1764 to petition for royal government instead of Proprietary rule. Deborah broke down in tears, begging him to stay. He left anyway, promising a short trip. It would be ten years before he saw Philadelphia again. As Isaacson notes, Franklin’s attachment to home was always provisional; he made his domestic happiness wherever he happened to be, and at this moment London held more of his heart than Philadelphia. Franklin’s relationship with his sister Jane Mecom, maintained through decades of correspondence, provides a poignant counterpoint to his public life. Jane was the only family member who consistently called him “Brother” with unaffected warmth; their letters discuss everything from politics and religion to family gossip and recipes. Franklin sent her books, money, and advice throughout his life, and she in turn provided the emotional anchor that his peripatetic career otherwise lacked.

Chapitre 10: Agent provocateur, Londres, 1765–1770 / Agent Provocateur

Franklin’s second London mission began in 1764 and lasted a decade, during which he straddled American rights. The Stamp Act of 1765 shattered his hopes for imperial harmony. Franklin initially underestimated colonial anger, even recommending his friend John Hughes as stamp distributor for Pennsylvania — a catastrophic error that nearly destroyed his reputation. Mobs threatened his Market Street house; Deborah, with characteristic courage, refused to flee. She sent their daughter Sally to New Jersey for safety but stayed herself with her cousin Josiah Davenport and a score of armed men. “I ordered the men to set up some sort of defense upstairs; I wanted to face the bandits alone,” she wrote to Franklin. “I told them that you, my husband, never wanted to injure anyone, as I had never harmed them.” The house was saved by the “White Oak Boys,” Franklin supporters who threatened to destroy the home of any rioter who damaged Franklin’s.

Franklin fought back with his pen, writing under the pseudonym “Homespun” and other aliases. One piece proposed replacing tea with roasted corn; “Pacificus Secundus” satirically proposed that fifteen thousand troops at twelve million pounds per year could enforce martial law. His masterpiece was the testimony before the House of Commons on February 13, 1766, where he faced 174 questions in a single afternoon. Asked how the colonies would react to military enforcement, he replied: “Suppose a military force were sent to America. It will find no man in arms. What will it do then? It cannot force a man to buy a stamp if he chooses to do without it.” The Stamp Act was repealed, and Franklin’s reputation was restored — temporarily. A boat named The Franklin fired cannon salutes in Philadelphia harbor, and taverns offered free drinks in his honor. Franklin’s testimony was published widely in both America and Britain; it was considered a masterpiece of parliamentary lobbying and established him as America’s most effective voice in London. Yet the repeal did nothing to resolve the underlying constitutional question of Parliament’s authority over the colonies, and Franklin knew it. He began to shift his position, arguing that the colonies were not subject to Parliament’s taxing power but only to the Crown — a legal theory that would later underpin American arguments for independence. The same year, Franklin was elected a foreign member of the Royal Society of Sciences in Paris, cementing his reputation as an international intellectual figure.

At home, his daughter Sally fell in love with Richard Bache, a charming but financially troubled merchant. Franklin’s son William investigated and reported Bache was a “fortune hunter”; Franklin reluctantly approved the marriage but sent stern lectures about frugality. Franklin himself escaped to France in the summer of 1767, visiting Paris and Versailles with Sir John Pringle, calling Louis XV and Queen Marie “the best sovereigns in the world.” His letters to Polly from Paris described French women’s makeup techniques — “cut a three-inch circle in paper, place it on your cheek, and with a brush dipped in rouge, color both the skin and the paper” — and the superiority of Parisian street-cleaning to London’s. In Paris, Franklin was presented at court and attended a scientific demonstration at the Académie des Sciences, where he was treated as a celebrity. Despite the gaiety, the political situation worsened: the Townshend Acts deepened the crisis, and Franklin’s warnings that Britain was losing the affection of the colonies went unheeded. By 1768, Franklin was writing essays arguing that Parliament had no right to tax the colonies, positioning himself increasingly as a radical voice for American rights.

Chapitre 11: Rebelle, Londres, 1771–1775 / Rebel

The chapter opens with Franklin’s escape from political pressures into travels across the British Isles in 1771. He visited the industrial Midlands, observing ironworks, the Bridgewater Canal (an engineering marvel carrying coal barges over the river Irwell), and a silk mill where children as young as five to seven years worked twelve-hour days. The sight deepened his conviction that America’s free labor system was morally superior to the exploited labor of the Old World. He visited Joseph Priestley and conducted experiments on oil and water using a hollow cane filled with oil, which he would tap to calm rippling ponds — a parlor trick that served as a metaphor for his diplomatic method of smoothing troubled waters. In Scotland and Ireland, he saw the desperate poverty of tenant farmers and contrasted it with America’s prosperous, independent middle class. A tense encounter with Lord Hillsborough in Ireland confirmed Franklin’s view that the British aristocracy had no genuine interest in American welfare. Franklin also toured the Scottish universities at St. Andrews and Edinburgh, where he received an honorary degree and formed lasting friendships with David Hume and other intellectual luminaries of the Scottish Enlightenment.

At the estate of Bishop Jonathan Shipley at Twyford in Hampshire, Franklin began writing his Autobiography, addressed as a letter to his son William. Isaacson devotes significant attention to this masterpiece: its deceptive simplicity, its ironic self-awareness, its celebration of middle-class virtues, and its careful omissions — notably the identity of William’s mother and the full extent of Franklin’s youthful indiscretions. The Autobiography, Isaacson argues, is both a genuine work of self-examination and a masterfully constructed persona, revealing Franklin as his own greatest literary creation. It remains the classic American success story, the blueprint for the self-made man. Franklin wrote it in three distinct periods — 1771 at Twyford, 1784 in Passy, and 1788 in Philadelphia — and each section reflects a different phase of his life and perspective, giving the work a layered, polyphonic quality that continues to fascinate readers.

The political narrative accelerates with the Hutchinson Letters Affair of 1772–73. Franklin obtained copies of letters from Massachusetts Governor Thomas Hutchinson claiming there must be “an abridgment of what are called English liberties” in the colonies. He sent them to Boston, where they were published, triggering a firestorm. In January 1774, he was summoned to the Cockpit in Whitehall and subjected to a vicious hour-long public dressing-down by Solicitor General Alexander Wedderburn, who called him a thief and a man of “no honor.” Franklin stood silent throughout — “like a rock,” witnesses said — giving no sign of emotion. The humiliation turned him irrevocably toward rebellion. The next day he was dismissed from his post as Deputy Postmaster General, a position he had held for twenty years. He departed London in March 1775, after news of the Boston Tea Party and the Coercive Acts. He was sixty-nine years old and would never see England again. The Stevenson women wept as his household at 36 Craven Street was packed up after fifteen years. Even in his final days in England, Franklin continued to write, producing a satirical “Edict by the King of Prussia” that imagined the Prussians imposing the same taxes on Britain that Britain imposed on America — a piece so convincing that some readers took it for a genuine document.

Chapitre 12: Indépendance, Philadelphie, 1775–1776 / Independence

Franklin sailed from London in March 1775, arriving in Philadelphia on May 5, to find the colonies already at war — Lexington and Concord had been fought on April 18. The church bells of Philadelphia were ringing. George Washington had just arrived for the Continental Congress, and the two met immediately, beginning a warm and mutually respectful relationship. Franklin’s return voyage had been remarkably productive: he conducted systematic Gulf Stream temperature measurements with his grandson Temple, recording surface temperatures, depths, and current speeds with meticulous care. NASA would later study these logs and confirm their remarkable accuracy — Franklin had created the first scientific chart of the Gulf Stream, a navigation aid that would cut Atlantic travel time by weeks. He also wrote a heartbreaking, unsent letter to William Strahan: “You are a member of that Parliament which has condemned my country to destruction. I call heaven to witness that I consider you my enemy.” It was never mailed; the two men later reconciled, and Strahan sent Franklin a gift of Stilton cheese in Paris.

The Continental Congress seated Franklin as its oldest delegate, the only one with international fame. But the first weeks were uncertain: many suspected the silent old man might still be a British spy. His longtime ally Joseph Galloway chose the loyalist side. Most painfully, Franklin’s son William, the royal governor of New Jersey, refused to join the patriot cause. The family drama climaxed at a tense meeting at Galloway’s estate in Trevose, where Franklin revealed his support for independence and William, devastated, chose loyalty to the Crown. He served on the Committee of Secret Correspondence (forerunner of American foreign policy), inspected the colonial military, and worked on the Committee of Five drafting the Declaration of Independence. Though Jefferson wrote the document, Franklin’s editorial hand improved several passages, including the substitution of “We hold these truths to be sacred and undeniable” with “We hold these truths to be self-evident.” Franklin also contributed the famous story of the hatter’s sign — a joke about a man who kept simplifying his shop sign until nothing was left — as a warning against excessive revision. Beyond the Declaration, Franklin’s other congressional duties were staggering for a man of seventy: he served on the Committee of Secret Correspondence (forerunner of the CIA), the Committee on the Navy, the Committee on Trade and Manufactures, and the Committee on the Treasury, often attending multiple committee meetings in a single day.

On July 4, 1776, at age seventy, Franklin signed the Declaration with the others. But the Chapter ends on a somber note: the British were massing in New York, Washington’s army was crumbling, and Franklin’s family was irreparably split. William was arrested as a loyalist and imprisoned in Connecticut. Despite private pleas from Franklin’s daughter Sally, he refused to intervene. The warmth that had once existed between father and son had frozen into something as cold and permanent as winter ice. Franklin’s famous remark — “There is no good friend, no good father, no good son except in the eyes of the public” — took on a tragic dimension as his private life disintegrated alongside the public drama of revolution. Still, Franklin’s final act before leaving for France in October 1776 was to loan Congress £3,000 from his personal savings, a gesture of faith that was never fully repaid.

Chapitre 13: Courtisan, Paris, 1776–1778 / Courtier

Franklin’s mission to France began with a harrowing winter crossing of the Atlantic aboard the Reprisal, a ship so poorly provisioned that the salted beef caused him to break out in boils. He arrived in Nantes in December 1776 exhausted, ill, and feverish, yet within weeks the seventy-year-old Franklin had become the toast of Paris. His fur cap, his plain brown coat, his refusal to wear a wig — all played perfectly to French fantasies of the “noble savage” and Rousseau’s natural wisdom. Medallions, prints, and busts of his face appeared everywhere; ladies wore their hair in the “coiffure à la Franklin” style; snuffboxes, chamber pots, and even wallpaper patterns bore his image. John Adams, arriving later, noted with sharp envy that Franklin’s face was “as well known as that of the moon.” The mania was so intense that when Franklin complained to the Comtesse Diane de Polignac about her incessant flattery, she commissioned a Sèvres porcelain chamber pot with Franklin’s image at the bottom — a witty insult that Franklin reportedly found hilarious.

Franklin established himself at Passy, a suburban village on a hill overlooking Paris, where he presided over a de facto American embassy in a sprawling complex with gardens, terraces, and a thousand-bottle wine cellar. The household was a study in controlled chaos: a corrupt commissioner (Silas Deane), a secretary who was a British spy (Edward Bancroft), a crooked cook, and a mercenary landlord (Chaumont, who also outfitted American ships with uniforms and saltpeter) all plagued him. Yet he navigated the French court with extraordinary skill, charming the cautious foreign minister Vergennes and the reformer Turgot while outmaneuvering British spies who dogged his every step. Bancroft, Franklin’s secretary, was in fact a double agent reporting to the British ambassador; Franklin may have suspected this but calculated that allowing Bancroft to report selected information was a useful channel for disinformation. The American commission was famously dysfunctional: the corrupt but effective Silas Deane and the paranoid Arthur Lee, who suspected Franklin of conspiring with everyone. Lee’s campaign against Franklin was relentless; he accused Franklin of “wallowing in luxury” and wrote to his brothers that “the old doctor is mixed up in the pillaging.” Franklin’s response to Lee’s abuse was one of the most devastating letters he ever wrote, accusing Lee of an “infirmity of the mind” that would “end in madness,” though he wisely never mailed it.

Franklin understood that celebrity was a form of diplomatic power. He used France’s fascination with American liberty to extract loans, arms shipments, and ultimately a formal alliance. The Treaty of Alliance, signed in February 1778, was the greatest diplomatic triumph of the American Revolution. It secured the French navy, troops, and supplies that would prove decisive at Yorktown. Franklin had achieved what seemed impossible: he had turned a bankrupt rebellion into a cause worthy of the most powerful monarchy in Europe. When the treaties were signed, Franklin wore the same suit he had worn at the Cockpit humiliation four years earlier — a subtle gesture of triumph that the French court entirely missed but that Franklin’s inner circle understood.

Chapitre 14: Bon vivant, Paris, 1778–1785 / Bon Vivant

With the French alliance secured, Franklin relaxed into the pleasures of Parisian life — and the chapter revels in them. His relationship with the priggish John Adams, who arrived to replace Deane and was scandalized by Franklin’s seeming indolence, provides much of the narrative tension. Adams’s journal entries fume with frustration: Franklin rises late, receives a constant parade of visitors, dines out every night, and seems incapable of focused work. But Isaacson shows that Franklin’s method was deliberate — the bon vivant who never appeared busy was working relentlessly through charm, conversation, and social networks. His posture of leisurely wisdom was itself a mastery of diplomatic theater. Adams, by contrast, believed in visible exertion and resented that Franklin’s effortless manner seemed to accomplish more than his own desperate industry. When a Frenchman asked Adams if he was surprised that Franklin never attended religious services, Adams replied that Franklin “adores only the splendor of nature” — a remark that captured both his envy and his grudging admiration.

The chapter offers vivid portraits of Franklin’s French circle. Madame Brillon, a beautiful thirty-something musician and salonnière, carried on a lifelong flirtation with Franklin; he wrote her playful, intimate letters and composed music for her on his glass harmonica. Madame Helvétius, the sixty-year-old widow of the Enlightenment philosopher, received a marriage proposal from Franklin that she declined with gentle amusement — Franklin proposed they marry, joking that the philosopher Helvétius would not object from heaven since he was enjoying paradise with Franklin’s own deceased wife Deborah. Voltaire and Franklin had a legendary public meeting in 1778 before the French Academy: the two patriarchs of the Enlightenment embraced, and Voltaire gave Franklin’s grandson Temple his blessing in English, saying “God and Liberty.” The crowd was so moved that some wept, and the press compared the scene to Solon embracing Sophocles. Both Franklin and Voltaire also joined the Masonic Lodge of the Nine Muses in April 1778, a lodge that Helvétius had envisioned before his death and that became a meeting ground for philosophers and free-thinkers opposed to Church and monarchical orthodoxy. The Marquis de Lafayette, Franklin’s protégé, carried the American cause into the French court.

Yet beneath the gaiety, Franklin was managing the war’s grinding logistics: securing loans from French bankers, outfitting ships for privateers, supplying Washington’s army with uniforms and weapons, and dealing with the chaotic John Paul Jones, whose spectacular victory in the Bonhomme Richard (named in honor of Franklin’s Poor Richard) over the British Serapis is narrated in vivid detail. Jones’s famous cry — “I have not yet begun to fight!” — became a legend of the war. The battle lasted three hours, with the Bonhomme Richard sinking moments after the Serapis surrendered; Jones lost half his crew to death or wounds. By the Yorktown victory in 1781, Franklin’s popularity in France was at its absolute zenith. Even his enemies conceded that no other American could have secured the alliance, held the diplomatic line, and kept the cause alive through seven years of war.

Chapitre 15: Pacificateur, Paris, 1778–1785 / Peacemaker

This chapter focuses on the peace negotiations that would end the Revolutionary War. Franklin was appointed sole Minister Plenipotentiary to France in 1779, giving him supreme authority over American diplomacy — though the appointment inflamed Adams’s jealousy and Arthur Lee’s paranoid accusations of collaboration. The vote in Congress was twelve to one, the sole dissenter representing Pennsylvania, where Franklin’s enemies were concentrated. The chapter covers Franklin’s management of the complex triangular negotiations between the United States, France, and Britain. His strategy was masterfully patient: he knew time was on America’s side as British war fatigue grew, and he refused to be rushed into a settlement that would betray the French alliance — the bedrock of his policy. Lord Shelburne’s government sent secret envoys to sound out terms; Franklin received them but kept the French informed of every contact.

The negotiations that produced the Treaty of Paris of 1783 tested Franklin’s diplomatic skills to their limit. He had to balance America’s need for favorable terms — territorial boundaries extending to the Mississippi River, fishing rights off Newfoundland, evacuation of British troops from American soil — with France’s interests and his own personal commitment never to sign a separate peace. Adams and John Jay, suspicious of French motives, pushed for direct negotiations with Britain, violating the spirit of the alliance. Franklin, the master of compromise, navigated between them all, ultimately securing an extraordinarily favorable treaty: full independence, vast western territories, the richest fishing grounds in the world, and the Mississippi River as America’s unambiguous western boundary. During the negotiations, Franklin kept a detailed journal of every meeting, noting each diplomat’s temperament, arguments, and possible weaknesses — a methodical approach that reflected his lifelong habit of systematic observation.

The preliminary articles were signed in November 1782, the definitive treaty in September 1783. Franklin, now seventy-seven, had completed his last great service to his country. Turgot’s famous epigram captured the achievement: Eripuit cœlo fulmen sceptrumque tyrannis — he snatched the lightning from the sky and the scepter from tyrants. Exhausted and suffering from gout and bladder stones, Franklin could finally think of home. He had been in Europe for nearly a decade; at seventy-seven, he knew he had no more Atlantic crossings left in him. The chapter also covers Franklin’s relationship with the Marquis de Lafayette, who bombarded him with plans for a joint invasion of England, and the escapades of John Paul Jones, whom Franklin had to restrain from plundering British estates. One of the chapter’s most amusing episodes involves the gardener’s wife at Chaumont’s estate accusing Jones of attempted rape; Franklin, writing to Jones, noted that the woman was “the most repulsive, dirty, and wrinkled old woman in all of Passy,” and the accusation quickly became a joke among the household — though Jones took it seriously enough to provide a detailed written defense that Franklin found both comic and touching.

Chapitre 16: Sage, Philadelphie, 1785–1790 / Sage

Franklin returned to Philadelphia in September 1785, after nine years abroad, to a hero’s welcome — cannons fired, church bells rang, and a crowd carried him to his door. He was eighty years old, suffering from gout and kidney stones that made movement agonizing, but he remained intellectually indefatigable. The chapter details his final prodigy of invention: during the ocean voyage home, he produced forty pages of maritime studies covering hull design, wave resistance, ship compartmentalization (a technique essential for modern shipbuilding), and Gulf Stream temperature measurements that NASA later confirmed as remarkably accurate. Back in Philadelphia, he built a magnificent new library wing on his Market Street house holding 4,276 volumes — the largest private collection in America — and added a mechanical book-extending arm, a fan-driven rocking chair, and an improved printing press for his grandson Benny. Despite his physical suffering, Franklin’s intellectual curiosity was undimmed; he conducted experiments on the effect of opium on pain perception and corresponded with scientists across Europe and America.

The chapter’s political centerpiece is the Constitutional Convention of 1787. Franklin, at eighty-one the oldest delegate, played a crucial mediating role at critical moments. When the Convention was deadlocked over representation between large and small states, he proposed the Connecticut Compromise that gave the House proportional representation and the Senate equal representation for each state. When the final draft was imperfect — he had “doubts” about several parts and feared the executive branch had too much power — he urged unanimous acceptance with a deeply moving speech asking delegates to “doubt a little of their own infallibility” and accept the document as the best they could achieve. The speech, delivered by James Wilson because Franklin was too weak to stand, is considered one of the Convention’s defining moments. Franklin also proposed opening each session with a prayer, a motion that was quietly tabled when the delegates realized they had no funds to pay a chaplain.

The chapter also covers Franklin’s last political battles: his presidency of the Pennsylvania Abolition Society, his final published work (a 1790 attack on slavery presented as a Quaker memorial to Congress demanding an end to the slave trade), and his famous will, whose codicil created a trust fund lending money at five percent interest to young married artisans in Boston and Philadelphia. Franklin’s last public act was to sign a petition to Congress for the abolition of slavery, submitted on February 12, 1790, just two months before his death.

Chapitre 17: Épilogue / Epilogue

The epilogue traces the fates of Franklin’s descendants after his death on April 17, 1790, at age eighty-four. His funeral was the largest Philadelphia had ever seen: twenty thousand people lined the streets in mourning. But the most tragic figure in the aftermath was William Franklin, the loyalist son. Franklin’s will disinherited him, leaving only worthless Canadian land tracts with a withering clause: “The part he acted against me in the late war, which is of public notoriety, will account for my leaving him no more of an estate he endeavored to deprive me of.” William insisted he had repaid any debt a hundredfold through his sacrifice of all his New Jersey property — the patriot government confiscated his entire estate. He never returned to America, spending his last twenty-five years in England in bitterness and loneliness. He married Mary D’Evelyn, an Irishwoman who nursed him through illness, but she died before him, leaving him “that solitude which I loathe and which my nature cannot bear.”

The other descendants had mixed fates. Temple Franklin, Franklin’s grandson and heir to his manuscripts, lived an aimless life in Paris, fathered yet another illegitimate daughter (Ellen) with his stepmother’s sister, and published a disorderly, incomplete collection of Franklin’s works in 1817. His manuscripts were later found in a London tailor’s shop in 1840, being used as pattern paper for cutting cloth — a near-loss of an irreplaceable legacy. Sally and Richard Bache inherited the Market Street houses, on condition that Richard “free Bob, his Negro” — an emancipation that went badly when Bob, unable to support himself, begged to be taken back as a slave, a request Franklin’s executor had to awkwardly refuse. Benny Bache, Franklin’s fiery grandson, founded the American Aurora — the most radical Jeffersonian newspaper of its era — attacked Washington as a monarchist and Adams as a tyrant, was arrested for sedition under the Alien and Sedition Acts, and died of yellow fever at the age of twenty-nine. Benny’s newspaper career, though brief, was spectacular: he published leaked copies of the Jay Treaty before the Senate had ratified it, turned the Aurora into the most widely read newspaper in the nation, and was personally beaten on a Philadelphia street by a Federalist congressman whom he had criticized in print.

The chapter ends on a hopeful note with the afterlife of Franklin’s trust fund codicil. By 1990, the combined funds had grown to approximately $2.3 million, funding scholarships and institutions exactly as Franklin had envisioned — giving industrious young people from backgrounds as diverse as Franklin’s own the start in life he had once needed. Isaacson also notes the irony: by 1990, a 5% perpetual loan to a young married artisan was a quaintly inadequate sum, but the principle Franklin had championed — that modest capital, wisely deployed, could transform lives — remained as relevant as ever.

Chapitre 18: Conclusions / Conclusions

Isaacson’s concluding chapter steps back to assess Franklin’s character and legacy through two centuries of criticism and celebration. He positions Franklin within the enduring American cultural divide between the Puritan tradition (Jonathan Edwards, the Mather family) and the Enlightenment tradition — between salvation through divine grace and salvation through good works, between hierarchy and democratic equality, between religious fervor and practical tolerance. Franklin, Isaacson argues, represents the pragmatic, middle-class, civic-minded strand of American identity that has been alternately celebrated and reviled by every generation since his death. The battle over Franklin’s legacy, Isaacson suggests, is really a battle over what kind of nation America should be.

The chapter surveys the shifting judgments of history. The Romantics — Keats, Carlyle, Emerson, Thoreau, Melville — scorned Franklin’s lack of poetry and spirituality. Keats called him “a man of a thousand low susceptibilities.” Thoreau refused to bring the Autobiography to Walden Pond, finding its gospel of thrift antithetical to his project. Emerson was ambivalent: “more useful, more moral, and more pure than Socrates” but “of a stubborn frugality, a dull and unheroic citizen.” D. H. Lawrence’s famous 1923 essay — “I do not like him” — rewrote Franklin’s thirteen virtues into a parody of romantic self-expression, calling him “the first American” as an insult. Lawrence objected specifically to Franklin’s elimination of mystery and passion from life, preferring a more primal, instinctual humanity. The Gilded Age industrialists — Andrew Carnegie, who credited Franklin with inspiring his entire philanthropic philosophy, and Thomas Mellon, who said the Autobiography was “the pivot of my life” — revered him as the patron saint of self-made success.

The chapter continues through Max Weber’s The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism, which made Franklin and Poor Richard the prime exhibit for the “philosophy of avarice,” though Weber misread Franklin’s earnestness as mere instrumentality. The Depression revived Franklin’s reputation as a symbol of thrift and community; Carl Van Doren’s 1938 biography won the Pulitzer Prize and launched the modern Franklin revival. H. W. Brands’s The First American (2000) and Edmund S. Morgan’s Benjamin Franklin (2002) offered balanced scholarly assessments. In the late twentieth century, Franklin became a benign folk figure — the kite-flying eccentric — even as management gurus and self-help authors commercialized his system. David Brooks caught the contemporary mood: Franklin would “feel instantly at home in our office towers,” sharing America’s passion for technology and progress. But Brooks also noted the paradox: Franklin’s moral system was designed for a world of scarcity and self-denial, while modern America is a world of abundance and self-expression — raising the question of whether Franklin’s virtues are still relevant.

Isaacson’s own assessment is deeply sympathetic. Franklin was not a poet or a mystic, but he was something more valuable: a man who combined scientific genius with practical wisdom, who believed in the decency and intelligence of ordinary people. His faith in “the common man” was, in its own way, a noble and transformative ideal. He helped create a nation where talent could rise regardless of birth. As historian Henry Steele Commager wrote: “In Franklin could converge the virtues of Puritanism without its defects, the illumination of the Enlightenment without its heat.” Franklin’s life, with all its contradictions and compromises, points toward a democratic vision built on pragmatic idealism: a world where virtue is its own reward, where cooperation matters as much as competition, and where the measure of a person is not birth or wealth but what they contribute to the common good. Isaacson closes with a plea for Franklin’s kind of humanism — a philosophy that embraces both reason and good-heartedness, that values both individual achievement and social responsibility, and that remains, two centuries later, the most hopeful vision of what America might become.
