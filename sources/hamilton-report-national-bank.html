Report on a National Bank

Hamilton’s great report on the establishment of a national bank, one of the four pillars of his financial program alongside the Reports on Public Credit, the Mint, and Manufactures.

Submitted to the House of Representatives, December 13, 1790 Alexander Hamilton, Secretary of the Treasury

Contents

Hamilton’s report on the establishment of a national bank opens with an extended theoretical justification of banking institutions before presenting the detailed plan. The structure is as follows:

Preliminary Observations: The Utility of Banks

Hamilton first establishes the principles and benefits of banks in general, arguing from the experience of the principal and most enlightened commercial nations, which have universally found admission and patronage for public banks. He identifies three principal advantages:

Firstly — Banks increase the active capital of a country. Hamilton explains the mechanism with precision: a bank can lend and circulate a greater sum than the amount of its coin capital through three converging circumstances. First, a bank lends not only its own stock but the money deposited with it, which depositors are unlikely to all withdraw simultaneously — experience proves that money “much oftener changes proprietors than place,” and what is drawn out is generally speedily replaced, making deposits an effective fund. Second, a bank lends its credit through bank notes, which circulate as money, “performing in every stage the office of money” until extinguished by a discount with someone who has a payment to make to the bank. Third, there is always a large quantity of gold and silver in the bank’s repositories beyond its own stock, placed there partly for safe-keeping and partly for accommodation. Through these mechanisms, “large sums are lent and paid, frequently through a variety of hands, without the intervention of a single piece of coin.”

The ability to circulate a greater sum than actual capital is acquired gradually and must be preceded by a firm establishment of confidence — confidence that may be “bestowed on the most rational grounds, since the excess in question will always be bottomed on good security of one kind or another.” Every well-conducted bank carefully requires such security before advancing money or credit.

The consequence for national wealth is profound: money that would otherwise lie idle while awaiting employment is instead deposited or invested in bank stock, where it “administers to the wants of others” while remaining accessible to its owner. This yields extra profit from what others pay for the use of it, and “keeps the money itself in a state of incessant activity.” In the “almost infinite vicissitudes and competitions of mercantile enterprise,” there is never danger of the money remaining idle in the vaults. This additional employment given to money, and the bank’s faculty to lend and circulate a greater sum than its stock in coin, constitute “to all the purposes of trade and industry, an absolute increase of capital.” Bank paper or credit effects purchases as effectually as an equal sum of gold and silver. Thus banks “become nurseries of national wealth” — a consequence “as satisfactorily verified by experience, as it is clearly deducible in theory.”

Secondly — Banks give greater facility to government in obtaining pecuniary aids, especially in sudden emergencies. The capitals of a great number of individuals are collected to a point and placed under one direction. The mass formed by this union is “magnified by the credit attached to it.” While this mass is always ready and can be put in motion at once in aid of the government, the bank’s own interest in preserving its relationship with the government constitutes “a sure pledge for its disposition to go as far in its compliances, as can in prudence be desired.” There is, Hamilton notes, “an intimate connection of interest between the government and the bank of a nation.”

Thirdly — Banks facilitate the payment of taxes. This advantage is produced in two ways. First, those who have access to the bank can borrow to meet tax obligations punctually — an accommodation “sensibly felt in the payment of the duties heretofore laid, by those who reside where establishments of this nature exist.” Second, banks increase the quantity of circulating medium and quicken circulation. Hamilton illustrates: when payments must be made between different places, if no private bills or bank notes with currency in both places exist, coin must be remitted — “attended with trouble, delay, expense, and risk.” But if bank notes are current in both places, transmission by post or other convenient conveyance answers the purpose, and these notes are frequently returned, obviating the transportation and re-transportation of metals. The metals themselves continue in activity, administering to ordinary circulation. Whatever enhances the quantity of circulating money “adds to the ease with which every industrious member of the community may acquire that portion of it, of which he stands in need.”

Reply to Objections Against Banks

Hamilton addresses six principal charges against banks, arguing that on close and candid survey they are either without foundation, proceed from other or partial causes, or are more than counterbalanced by opposite advantages:

1. That banks serve to increase usury. Hamilton acknowledges the kernel of truth: the punctuality in payments that banks necessarily exact has sometimes obliged those who have adventured beyond both capital and credit to resort to usurers for aid at any price. But experience and practice gradually bring a cure. A general habit of punctuality among traders is the natural consequence of the necessity of observing it with the bank — a circumstance that “more than compensates for any occasional ill.” Traders can calculate their expectations with greater certainty and are in proportionably less danger of disappointments that might compel borrowing at usury. The directors of a bank, though initially tempted to extend accommodation beyond strict prudence to build business, grow more circumspect as affairs become established and the evils of too great facility are experimentally demonstrated. They observe more narrowly the operations and pursuits of those with whom they deal; they “economize the credit they give to those of suspicious solidity; they refuse it to those whose career is more manifestly hazardous.” In the course of practice, “the interest will make it the policy of a bank, to succour the wary and industrious; to discredit the rash and unthrifty; to discountenance both usurious lenders and usurious borrowers.” Most fundamentally, banks’ property of increasing the quantity and quickening the circulation of money tends to abridge usury: if usury prevails according to the proportion of demand for borrowing to the quantity of money at market, whatever renders supply more equal to demand must counteract the progress of usury.

2. That banks tend to prevent other kinds of lending. The objection holds that because bank stock yields profits exceeding the legal rate of interest, money-lenders prefer investing in it rather than lending at interest, with additional motives of prompt command of capital and frequent returns. Hamilton gives this charge only qualified admission, particularly in reference to the American situation. First, the great bulk of bank stock will consist of the funds of men in trade (who cannot spare capitals for long-term mortgages) and monied foreigners (who would not be subjected to the casualties and delays of lending in a distant country). Second, there will always be a considerable proportion of cautious money-lenders who prefer mortgages on real estate, which they consider more secure than bank stock. Moreover, the supposed effect is temporary: a bank’s capital is restricted within a defined limit; once subscriptions are completed, no additional capital can be invested. Stock may change hands, but the money the buyer takes from the common mass, the seller receives and restores to it. Future surpluses must take their natural course, and lending at interest goes on as if there were no bank — indeed, “it must flow in a more copious stream,” since the bank furnishes extraordinary supply for borrowers within its sphere, leaving larger supply for borrowers elsewhere.

3. That banks furnish temptations to over-trading. Hamilton concedes that by affording additional aid to mercantile enterprise, banks may induce merchants to adventure beyond the prudent point. But “the very statement of the thing shows that the subject of the charge is an occasional ill, incident to a general good.” Credit of every kind is chargeable with the same inconvenience; it even applies to gold and silver when abundant. “Would it be wise on this account to decry the precious metals; to root out credit; or to prescribe the means of that enterprise, which is the main spring of trade, and a principal source of national wealth, because it now and then runs into excesses?” The evil is to be compared with the good, and the new energies derived to commercial enterprise from banks are “a source of general profit and advantage, which greatly outweigh the partial ills.”

4 & 5. That banks aid ignorant adventurers and give fraudulent traders fictitious credit. These charges, considered together, have some foundation but far less than pretended. The practice of giving fictitious credit is “one of those evils which experience, guided by interest, speedily corrects.” The bank itself is in jeopardy of suffering, giving it the strongest inducement to be on guard. The directors, selected from the class of traders, are expected to possess accurate knowledge of the characters and situations of those with whom they deal, supplemented by the course of dealing itself — “in most cases a good index of the state in which those persons are.” The artifices and shifts employed by those in desperate circumstances are “so many prognostics, not difficult to be interpreted.” Hence banks are often the first to discover unsoundness and, by withholding credit, announce to the public that such characters are not entitled to it. And even when banks are betrayed into error, “they more frequently enable honest and industrious men, of small or perhaps of no capital, to undertake and prosecute business, with advantage to themselves and to the community” and assist merchants of capital and credit who meet unforeseen shocks to make head against misfortunes — “circumstances which form no inconsiderable encomium on the utility of banks.”

6. That banks tend to banish gold and silver from the country. This is the most serious charge and the most extensively examined. The force of the objection rests on banks being an engine of paper credit that furnishes a substitute for the metals. Hamilton notes it is an objection that lies not peculiarly against banks but against every species of paper credit. The common answer — that it is immaterial what serves as money, and that national wealth is measured by productions of labour and industry rather than precious metals — is not destitute of solidity but not entirely satisfactory. Hamilton offers a more conclusive answer: a nation without mines must derive precious metals from others in exchange for the products of its labour and industry. The quantity it possesses is regulated by the favourable or unfavourable balance of trade. Well-constituted banks favour the increase of precious metals because they augment the active capital of a country — generating employment, animating and expanding labour and industry, creating more products for exportation, and thus conducing to a favourable balance of trade.

Hamilton addresses counter-objections: that bank paper, by substituting for specie, counteracts the necessity for metals that might restrain exportation in case of a wrong balance, and that it retards economizing reforms. He acknowledges some truth but argues these are exceptions rather than overthrows. The support of industry is “probably in every case, of more consequence towards correcting a wrong balance of trade, than any practicable retrenchments in the expenses of families or individuals.” Moreover, bank circulation is always in a compound ratio to the fund upon which it depends and the demand for it; as that fund is itself affected by the exportation of metals, there is no danger of overstocking as with government paper emissions. Comparisons between different countries to illustrate the objection are fallible, depending on “an infinite variety of facts and combinations” — the mass and value of productions, the nature of foreign establishments, the kind of wars, trade privileges — rendering the investigation “too complex to justify any reliance on the vague and general surmises” hazarded on the point.

Temporary exportation for particular purposes is indeed facilitated by banks, but “their utility is in nothing more conspicuous, than in these very cases.” Banks enable the government to pay foreign debts and meet external exigencies; they enable the merchant to support credit when special circumstances prevent remittances; they enable enterprises that ultimately tend to augment the species of wealth in question. Gold and silver may often be employed in procuring commodities abroad which, in a circuitous commerce, “replace the original fund, with considerable addition.”

Historical Context: The Post-War Situation

Hamilton argues that many observed evils attributed to banks were actually caused by the general devastation of personal property occasioned by the late war. The war naturally produced on one hand a great demand for money, and on the other a great deficiency of it to answer the demand. Injudicious laws that grew out of the public distresses, impairing confidence and causing part of the inadequate sum to be locked up, aggravated the evil. The dissipated habits contracted by many during the war, plunging them into expenses beyond their incomes; the number of adventurers without capital who rushed into trade; the employment of considerable sums in speculations upon the public debt, which from its unsettled state was incapable of becoming a substitute — all these circumstances concurring necessarily led to usurious borrowing. Those who erroneously blamed banks might have avoided their mistake “by turning their eyes towards places where there were none; and where, nevertheless, the same evils would have been perceived to exist, even in a greater degree.”

These evils have either ceased or been greatly mitigated. Hamilton looks for their more complete extinction from “that additional security to property, which the Constitution of the United States happily gives” — a circumstance of “prodigious moment in the scale both of public and private prosperity”; from the attraction of foreign capital under that security; from the consistency and stability the public debt is fast acquiring; and from the more copious circulation to be created by a well-constituted national bank.

The establishment of banks is recommended by reasons of a peculiar nature. Previously to the Revolution, circulation was carried on largely by paper emitted by local governments — in Pennsylvania alone the quantity was near a million and a half of dollars. This auxiliary is now at an end, and there has been a deficiency of circulating medium. Evidence includes the vast tracts of waste land and the little advanced state of manufactures. Progressive settlement draws off circulating money, diverts labour from furnishing materials for foreign trade, and consumes rather than produces surplus for exportation. Manifestations include greater prevalence of direct barter in interior districts and difficulty in advantageous alienation of improved real estate.

Hamilton notes the constitutional prohibition on state paper emissions and warns against federal paper money: though emissions under general authority “might have some advantages not applicable, and be free from some disadvantages” of state emissions, they are “of a nature so liable to abuse — and it may even be affirmed, so certain of being abused — that the wisdom of the government will be shown in never trusting itself with the use of so seducing and dangerous an expedient.” In times of tranquillity it might have no ill consequence, “but in great and trying emergencies, there is almost a moral certainty of its becoming mischievous.” The stamping of paper is so much easier than laying taxes that a government would rarely fail to indulge too far. A crucial difference between government paper and bank paper is that in the first case there is no standard for the quantity that will satisfy or surcharge circulation; in the latter, “that standard results from the demand.” If more is issued than necessary, it returns upon the bank — a limitation in the nature of things, whereas government discretion is the only measure.

Deficiencies of the Existing Bank of North America

Hamilton surveys the three existing banks — North America (Philadelphia), New York, and Massachusetts — and finds only the first has had a direct relation to the federal government. The Bank of North America originated in a resolution of Congress of May 26, 1781, founded on a proposition of the Superintendent of Finance, carried into execution by an ordinance of December 31, 1781. Its aid during the remaining period of the war “was of essential consequence.” However, the directors have since accepted a new charter from Pennsylvania materially variant from the original one, “which so narrows the foundation of the institution, as to render it an incompetent basis for the extensive purposes of a national bank.”

Hamilton identifies several critical deficiencies:

  • Capital too small. The Pennsylvania charter limits capital to 2million,whereastheoriginalcongressionalordinancecontemplated10 million. So small a capital “promises neither the requisite aid to government, nor the requisite security to the community.” It may answer local accommodation but is “an inadequate foundation for a circulation co-extensive with the United States; embracing the whole of their revenues, and affecting every individual into whose hands the paper may come.”

  • Discretionary capital increases. The charter leaves capital increases to stockholder discretion. It is “naturally to be expected that the allurements of an advanced price of stock and of large dividends may disincline those who are interested to an extension of capital.” Public utility is thus made subordinate to private profit. Hamilton insists: “Public utility is more truly the object of public banks, than private profit. And it is the business of government, to constitute them on such principles, that while the latter will result, in a sufficient degree, to afford competent motives to engage in them, the former be not made subservient to it.”

  • No principle of rotation in directors. Hamilton argues that the reasons against rotation in political constitutions do not apply to banks. The administration of a bank “is regulated by a few simple fixed maxims, the application of which is not difficult to any man of judgment.” Rotation lessens “the danger of combinations among the directors, to make the institution subservient to party views” and renders “public confidence more firm, stable, and unqualified.” The necessary secrecy of directors’ transactions “gives unlimited scope to imagination to infer that something is, or may be, wrong,” making periodical changes a needed check. The head of the direction should be excepted, and periodical exclusion ought not to reach the whole board at once.

  • Improper voting rule. The original one-vote-per-share rule allows a few major stockholders to monopolize power; the new charter’s silence on the point might imply equal votes, which would be “a rule in a different extreme, not less erroneous.” A prudent mean is needed. The Bank of North America’s by-law attempts such a mean, but “a like majority with that which enacted this law, may at any moment repeal it.”

  • No safeguards against foreign influence. It is “scarcely reconcilable with a due caution, to permit that any but citizens should be eligible as directors of a national bank, or that non-resident foreigners should be able to influence the appointment of directors by the votes of their proxies.” However, existing foreign stockholders’ rights must be preserved.

Hamilton emphasizes: “Such a bank is not a mere matter of private property, but a political machine of the greatest importance to the state.”

Principles of Organization for a New National Bank

Branch banking. Hamilton acknowledges that the situation of the United States naturally inspires a wish for a plurality of branches. Besides more general accommodation, it would lessen the danger of a run upon the bank. However, various considerations discourage this idea: the complexity would inspire doubts deterring adventurers, and the practicability of safe administration “cannot be made so manifest in perspective, as to promise the removal of those doubts.” The most advisable course is to insert a provision that may lead to branches hereafter, if experience demonstrates utility. The argument against immediate branches is that each must be under a distinct, though subordinate, direction with considerable discretion; as the whole institution’s property would be liable for every part’s engagements, “the mismanagement of either branch might hazard serious disorder in the whole.”

Land as bank fund. Another wish — that the bank could lend to proprietors of land — yields to the difficulty of accomplishment. Land is “alone an unfit fund for a bank circulation.” If notes issued upon it were not payable in coin on demand, “this would amount to nothing more than a repetition of the paper emissions, which are now exploded by the general voice.” If notes are payable in coin, the land must first be converted into it by sale or mortgage — the difficulty of effecting this is “the very thing which begets the desire of finding another resource.” Neither is a mixed fund of coin and land free from impediments: these two species of property do not generally unite in the same hands. “Will the monied man consent to enter into a partnership with the landholder, by which the latter will share in the profits which will be made by the money of the former? The money, it is evident, will be the agent or efficient cause of the profits — the land can only be regarded as an additional security.”

Public vs. private direction. Hamilton firmly rejects the idea of the bank’s profits redounding to the immediate benefit of the state, however desirable it appears. “To attach full confidence to an institution of this nature, it appears to be an essential ingredient in its structure, that it shall be under a private, not a public direction; under the guidance of individual interest, not of public policy.” A government-directed bank would be “liable to being too much influenced by public necessity.” The suspicion of this “would most probably be a canker that would continually corode the vitals of the credit of the bank.” It would be “little less than a miracle, should the credit of the bank be at the disposal of the government, if in a long series of time, there was not experienced a calamitous abuse of it.” The keen, steady sense of their own interest in the directors — “pointing invariable to its true pole, the prosperity of the institution” — is the only security that can always be relied upon.

Hamilton distinguishes the European precedents: the Bank of Amsterdam is under municipal rather than governmental direction, and is a bank of deposit rather than of loan or circulation — “less liable to abuse, as well as less useful.” The state may be a holder of part of the stock and a sharer in profits, but “ought not to desire any participation in the direction.” However, the government owes itself and the community the right of ascertaining the state of the bank as often as necessary, excluding all pretension to control. This right “stands upon the clearest reasons,” for if bank paper is to be permitted in all the revenues and transactions of the country, “it becomes, in either view, a national concern of the first magnitude.”

The Plan

Hamilton presents the specific plan of 24 articles:

  • Capital stock: 10million,dividedinto25,000sharesof400 each. Subscriptions open on the first Monday of April next. Bodies politic as well as individuals may subscribe.

  • Payment structure: Each share payable one-fourth in gold and silver coin, three-fourths in that part of the public debt bearing an accruing interest of 6% per annum. Payments made in four equal parts at six-month intervals. Failure in any subsequent payment forfeits dividends accrued prior to the delay.

  • Incorporation: Subscribers incorporated until final redemption of the part of stock consisting of public debt.

  • Property limit: Corporation capacity limited to $15 million including original capital. Lands and tenements permitted only for immediate accommodation of the institution, bona fide mortgages, or satisfaction of debts previously contracted.

  • Debt restriction: Total debts (except deposits) shall never exceed capital stock. Directors under whose administration excess occurs are personally liable. Dissenting directors may excuse themselves by giving notice to the President and stockholders.

  • Trading restrictions: The company may sell or demise lands and public debt, but “shall trade in nothing, except bills of exchange, gold and silver bullion, or in the sale of goods pledged for money lent.” Interest rate limited to 6% on loans or discounts.

  • Governmental loans: No loan for the use of the federal government or any state exceeding $50,000, or of any foreign power, unless authorized by a law of the United States — serving as “a barrier to executive encroachments and to combinations inauspicious to the safety, or contrary to the policy of the Union.”

  • Management: Twenty-five directors, one of whom shall be president. Annual election on the first Monday of January by plurality of stockholders’ suffrages. Directors choose president from among their number.

  • Voting rights: A graduated scale: for one to two shares, one vote; for every two shares above two up to ten, one vote; for every four shares above ten up to thirty; for every six shares above thirty up to sixty; for every eight shares above sixty up to one hundred; for every ten shares above one hundred. Maximum thirty votes for any person, co-partnership, or body politic. Shares must be held three months to confer suffrage. Only resident stockholders may vote by proxy.

  • Rotation: Not more than three-fourths of directors (exclusive of president) eligible for the next succeeding year. The president may always be re-elected. Only citizen stockholders eligible as directors.

  • Stockholder rights: Any sixty or more stockholders holding two hundred shares or upwards may call a general meeting with six weeks’ notice.

  • Dividends: Half-yearly dividends of such profits as directors deem advisable. Every three years directors must lay before stockholders a detailed statement of debts remaining unpaid after treble the original credit term and surplus profits after losses and dividends.

  • Receivability: Bills and notes payable on demand in coin receivable in all payments to the United States.

  • Government oversight: The Treasury head shall be furnished, not exceeding once a week, with statements of capital stock, debts due, monies deposited, notes in circulation, and cash in hand, with right to inspect general accounts — “provided that this shall not be construed to imply a right of inspecting the account of any private individual.”

  • Exclusive privilege: No similar institution shall be established during the bank’s continuance.

  • Offices (branches): Directors may establish offices for discount and deposit within the United States.

  • Government subscription: The President authorized to subscribe up to $2 million on behalf of the United States, to be paid out of borrowed monies, borrowing an equal sum from the bank, reimbursable in ten years.

Explanation of Key Provisions

Public debt as capital. The combination of public debt in the capital is the principal provision requiring explanation. The chief object is to enable creation of a capital sufficiently large for extensive circulation. To collect $10 million in gold and silver in this country “may, without hesitation, be pronounced impracticable” — hence the necessity of an auxiliary, which the public debt presents. This part of the fund will be always ready to come in aid of specie, commanding a ready sale and expeditiously convertible into coin if exigency requires. This quality of prompt convertibility distinguishes it from land. The quarter-yearly receipts of interest will be an actual addition to the specie fund during intervals between dividends. The debt will produce a direct annual revenue of 6% from the government, entering into the half-yearly dividends. Hamilton notes the important precedent: the Bank of England “in its first erection, rested wholly on that foundation” — subscribers to a loan of £1,200,000 sterling were incorporated as a bank, with the debt created by the loan and its interest as the sole fund.

Interest rate limitation. Hamilton notes that different rates prevail in different parts of the Union, and as bank operations may extend throughout, some rule is necessary. He questions whether the limitation ought to be five rather than six per cent, as the former “would yield an ample dividend; perhaps as much as the latter, by the extension which it would give to business.” The natural effect of low interest is to increase trade and industry. “The difference of one per cent. in the rate at which money may be had, is often capable of making an essential change for the better, in the situation of any country or place.” However, Hamilton defers to practical considerations: the persons whose funds must constitute the stock might be diffident of profits below the accustomed rate, and “the hazard of contrary reasoning among the mass of monied men is a powerful argument against the experiment.”

Government subscription. The main design of authorizing a $2 million government subscription is to enlarge the specie fund and enable earlier extension of operations. Though borrowing with one hand what is lent with the other, the disbursement will be progressive, and bank notes may be thrown into circulation instead of gold and silver. Annual reimbursement of part of the sum borrowed will finally operate as an actual investment of specie. Moreover, as far as the dividend on stock exceeds interest paid on the loan, “there is a positive profit.”

Conclusion and Submission

Hamilton concludes by recommending that if the Bank of North America shall come forward with propositions for grafting onto that institution the characteristics necessary for a national bank, “there are, in his judgment, weighty inducements to giving every reasonable facility to the measure.” The pretensions of that institution from its original relation to the government and the services it has rendered “are such as to claim a disposition favourable to it, if those who are interested in it are willing, on their part, to place it on a footing satisfactory to the government.” Its co-operation would materially accelerate the accomplishment of the great object, and the collision that might otherwise arise “might, in a variety of ways, prove equally disagreeable and injurious.”

The report concludes: “All which is humbly submitted. ALEXANDER HAMILTON, Sec’ry of the Treasury.”
