Report on Public Credit

Submitted to the House of Representatives, January 9, 1790 Alexander Hamilton, Secretary of the Treasury

Contents

Hamilton’s first and most consequential report as Secretary of the Treasury, laying out the financial foundation of the new federal government. The report is a single continuous document without chapter divisions, but proceeds through the following major subjects:

I. The Necessity of Public Credit

Hamilton opens by recounting the House resolution of September 21, 1789, which directed him to prepare “a proper plan for the support of the Public Credit.” He describes his anxieties about the task — “difficulty of the task, from a well-founded diffidence of his own qualifications” — and his conviction that adequate provision for public credit is “a matter of high importance to the honour and prosperity of the United States.”

He sets out three “plain and undeniable truths”: (1) that exigencies requiring borrowing will inevitably occur in national affairs; (2) that loans in times of public danger, especially foreign war, are an indispensable resource even for the wealthiest nations; and (3) that in a country “possessed of little active wealth, or in other words little monied capital,” the necessity for that resource is proportionately urgent. When a nation’s credit is questionable, it must pay “an extravagant premium, in one shape or another, upon all the loans it has occasion to make” — and the same disadvantage extends to everything bought on terms of future payment. From this constant borrowing and buying dear, the expenses of a nation over time are vastly augmented by an unsound state of public credit.

Hamilton argues that on the “due observance at the present critical juncture, materially depends, in his judgment, the individual and aggregate prosperity of the citizens of the United States: their relief from the embarrassments they now experience; their character as a people; the cause of good government.”

II. The Principle of Good Faith

The means of maintaining public credit is “by good faith, by a punctual performance of contracts. States, like individuals, who observe their engagements, are respected and trusted.” Every breach of the public engagements, whether from choice or necessity, is hurtful to public credit in different degrees. Where necessity truly exists, the evils can only be palliated “by a scrupulous attention on the part of the government, to carry the violation no further than the necessity absolutely requires.”

Hamilton warns that governments should examine necessity carefully before yielding to it — “there is great reason to believe, that they exist far less frequently than precedents indicate; and are oftenest either pretended through levity, or want of firmness, or supposed through want of knowledge.” He notes that creditors of a nation are “generally speaking, enlightened men” who “will understand their true interest too well to refuse their concurrence in such modifications of their claims, as any real necessity may demand.”

Beyond political expediency, he invokes “considerations of still greater authority”: the “immutable principles of moral obligation” and the “intimate connexion between public virtue and public happiness.” The debt of the United States has a special character — “It was the price of liberty. The faith of America has been repeatedly pledged for it, and with solemnities, that give peculiar force to the obligation.” He acknowledges regret that the debt has not been kept — the necessities of war, “conspiring with inexperience in the subjects of finance,” produced direct infractions, and the subsequent period was “a continued scene of negative violation, or non-compliance.” But he notes that the last seven years had exhibited earnest efforts to retrieve national credit, and the “embarrassments of a defective constitution” that defeated those efforts “have ceased.”

He points to evidence of restored confidence: from January to November 1789, public securities rose thirty-three and a third per cent, and from that period rose fifty per cent more. Intelligence from abroad announced proportionately favourable effects. Hamilton lists the ends to be secured: “To justify and preserve their confidence; to promote the increasing respectability of the American name; to answer the calls of justice; to restore landed property to its due value; to furnish new resources both to agriculture and commerce; to cement more closely the union of the states; to add to their security against foreign attack; to establish public order on the basis of an upright and liberal policy.”

Hamilton observes that the present depreciated state of landed property is a serious calamity — cultivated land values have fallen since the Revolution from 25 to 50 per cent, with even steeper declines in the southern states. This decrease “ought, in a great measure, to be attributed to the scarcity of money.” A funded debt, he argues, would augment the monied capital of the country and have a proportional effect in raising land values, as demonstrated by the “most decisive experience in Great-Britain.”

IV. The Plan for Funding

The Discrimination Debate

Hamilton devotes extensive attention to the question “whether a discrimination ought not to be made between original holders of the public securities, and present possessors, by purchase.” Those advocating discrimination argue for paying full nominal value to original holders but only the purchase price plus interest to those who bought at depreciated prices. The argument is that “it would be unreasonable to pay twenty shillings in the pound, to one who had not given more for it than three or four,” and that it would “aggravate the misfortune of the first owner, who, probably through necessity, parted with his property at so great a loss, by obliging him to contribute to the profit of the person who had speculated on his distresses.”

Hamilton rejects this doctrine as “equally unjust and impolitic; as highly injurious, even to the original holders of public securities; — as ruinous to public credit.” His reasoning proceeds on several grounds:

First, breach of contract. The nature of the contract from its origin is that the public will pay the sum expressed in the security “to the first holder, or his assignee.” The intent in making securities assignable is that the proprietor may sell for what it is worth in the market and the buyer may be safe in the purchase. “Every buyer, therefore, stands exactly in the place of the seller — has the same right with him to the identical sum expressed in the security.”

Second, the purchaser is a fair purchaser. “Whatever necessity the seller may have been under, was occasioned by the government, in not making a proper provision for its debts. The buyer had no agency in it, and therefore ought not to suffer.” The buyer paid what the commodity was worth in the market, took the risk of reimbursement — “a hazard which was far from inconsiderable, and which, perhaps, turned on little less than a revolution in government” — and gave a fair equivalent.

Third, original holders’ claims are against the government alone. Those who sold from necessity “have not only nothing to object to the persons who relieved their necessities by giving them the current price of their property, but they are even under an implied condition to contribute to the reimbursement of those persons.”

Fourth, practical impossibility. Not all original holders sold from necessity — some sold from “want of confidence” or for “allurements of some profitable speculation.” How can these classes be discriminated? “How shall it be known, whether, if the purchaser had employed his money in some other way, he would not be in a better situation?” Questions “multiply themselves without end, and demonstrate the injustice of a discrimination, even on the most subtile calculations of equity.”

Fifth, constitutional prohibition. Article VI of the Constitution provides that “all debts contracted, and engagements entered into, before the adoption of that Constitution, shall be as valid against the United States under it, as under the confederation” — which Hamilton interprets as a “constitutional ratification of the contracts respecting the debt, in the state in which they existed under the confederation.” Under that standard, “the rights of assignees and original holders must be considered as equal.”

Hamilton cites the circular address of Congress of April 26, 1783, which declared that “To discriminate the merits of these several descriptions of creditors, would be a task equally unnecessary and invidious. If the voice of humanity plead more loudly in favour of some than of others, the voice of policy, no less than of justice, pleads in favour of all. A WISE NATION will never permit those who relieve the wants of their country, or who rely most on its faith, its firmness, and its resources, when either of them is distrusted, to suffer by the event.”

Arrears of Interest

Hamilton argues that arrears of interest “have pretensions at least equal to the principal.” The liquidated debt originated from two sources: loans (which were payable at fixed periods that have long since elapsed, with creditors preferring to continue their money at interest rather than receive it in depreciated currency) and services performed / articles supplied (which ought to have been paid at the time but were converted by government acts into capital bearing six per cent interest). Regarding arrears, “these are now due, and those to whom they are due, have a right to claim immediate payment.” The equivalent for their detention “ought to be a real and fair one. And what other fair equivalent can be imagined for the detention of money, but a reasonable interest?”

The Six Loan Proposals

Hamilton proposes a loan to the full amount of the debt (both union and state) upon the following terms, allowing creditors to choose:

First plan: For every $100 subscribed, the subscriber receives two-thirds funded at six per cent interest, redeemable at the pleasure of the government by payment of principal, and the other third in lands in the Western Territory at twenty cents per acre. Hamilton defends the land valuation — the general price of western lands had been a dollar per acre in public securities, but those securities were then worth less than three shillings in the pound. Twenty cents per acre is an equitable proportion between the value at the time and likelihood of increase. The public retains the advantage of any fall in market interest rates.

Second plan: The whole sum funded at four per cent, irredeemable by any payment exceeding five dollars per annum on account of both principal and interest, with 15.80inlandascompensationforthereductionofinterest.Thisplanisgroundedonthesuppositionthatinterestwillfalltofivepercentinfiveyearsandtofourpercentintwentyyears.Thepresentvalueofthecompensationiscalculatedat15.792 — a fraction less than the sum proposed. Should interest fall faster than anticipated, the creditor stands to gain.

Third plan: 66.67fundedimmediatelyatsixpercent,irredeemablebyanypaymentexceeding4.67 per annum, with $26.88 funded at the same rate at the end of ten years. This plan proceeds on the same supposition of a successive fall in the rate of interest. Hamilton is sincere in affirming that this plan “will be likely to prove, to the full, as beneficial to the creditors, as a provision for his debt upon its present terms.”

Fourth and fifth plans: Life annuities at four per cent, with payment deferred — on a single life contingency of living to a given age not less than ten years distant, or on the survivorship of the youngest of two persons. A hundred dollars advanced on the life of a person of eleven years would produce an annuity of 10commencingattwenty−one,18 at thirty-one, 37atforty−one,or78 at fifty-one. These deferred annuities afford “a comfortable provision for the evening of life, or for wives who survive their husbands.”

Sixth plan: Five per cent interest on subscriptions payable half in specie and half in debt, irredeemable by any payment exceeding six dollars per annum. This plan is “highly advantageous to the creditors, who may be able to make that payment; while the specie itself could be applied in purchases of the debt, upon terms, which would fully indemnify the public for the increased interest.” Foreign holders of domestic debt may particularly embrace this arrangement.

The Tontine Proposal

As an auxiliary experiment, Hamilton proposes a tontine consisting of six classes by age: 20-and-under (8.40annuitypershare),21–30(8.65), 31–40 (9.00),41–50(9.65), 51–60 (10.70),andabove60(12.80). Each share is $200, with an indefinite number of shares per class. The annuities of those who die are equally divided among survivors until four-fifths are dead, at which point the principle of survivorship ceases and each annuitant enjoys their dividend as a several annuity during life.

Guiding Principle

Hamilton emphasizes that offering various propositions is vital: “Different tempers will be governed by different views of the subject.” He insists that creditors’ consent must be voluntary in fact as well as in name — “every proposal of a change ought to be in the shape of an appeal to their reason and to their interest; not to their necessities.” Any surplus revenue after satisfying interest on new loans and current service should be divided among non-subscribing creditors, though limited to four per cent. Quarter-yearly interest payments are proposed to facilitate circulation and give creditors effectively a higher rate — six per cent paid quarterly is truly worth 6.135per100.

V. Assumption of State Debts

Hamilton presents a forceful argument for federal assumption of state debts. His reasoning proceeds on multiple grounds:

Unity of purpose: State debts were incurred for the same object — the Revolution. “The defence of each part, is that of the whole; and unless all the expenditures are brought into a common mass, the tendency must be, to add to the calamities suffered by being the most exposed to the ravages of war, an increase of burthens.”

Economy and efficiency: Whether provision is made wholly by the United States or partly by the states, no greater total revenue is required. But a single general plan “issuing from one authority” avoids the competition for resources inevitable with separate plans. Separate provisions would produce “interfering regulations, and thence collision and confusion.” The most productive objects of revenue are not numerous — either one side engrosses them or both resort to the same objects in different modes, causing over-accumulation.

Constitutional constraints: The Constitution requires that “all duties, imposts, and excises, shall be uniform throughout the United States.” If a single state has already imposed a duty on an article, the general government would be constrained either to refrain from further imposition or confine itself to the difference. Thus “the preoccupancy of an article by a single state, would tend to arrest or abridge the impositions of the union on that article.”

Unity of creditor interest: If all public creditors receive their dues from one source “distributed with an equal hand,” their interest will be the same and they will unite in support of fiscal arrangements. Distinct provisions would create “distinct interests, drawing different ways” and produce “mutual jealousy and opposition.”

State creditors’ vulnerability: State creditors would likely fare worse than union creditors without assumption — a principal branch of revenue is exclusively vested in the union, and a state is always checked in imposing taxes on consumption goods from the “want of power to extend the same regulation to the other states.” Their dissatisfaction would “operate injuriously, both for the creditors and the credit of the United States.” Hence it is even in the interest of union creditors that state debts be assumed.

Objection and response: The principal objection is that assumption would increase the difficulty of an equitable settlement between states and the United States. Hamilton thinks such discussion premature. He proposes, as a framework, that each state be charged with advances from the US treasury (with six per cent interest) and the amount of its assumed securities, and credited for monies paid and articles furnished to the United States during the war. The whole to be liquidated to specie value with six per cent interest. Commissioners with equitable discretion would superintend the arrangement. He expresses little doubt that balances would appear in favour of all states against the United States.

VI. Means of Providing for the Debt

Hamilton estimates the total annual sum needed at 2,239,163forinterest(ontheforeigndebtatexistingratesandthedomesticdebtatfourpercentonthenewcapitalformed),plus600,000 for current service — total $2,839,163. He proposes to raise this through:

Import duties on wines (Madeira, Sherry, and other wines at rates from 20 to 35 cents per gallon), distilled spirits (graded by proof, from 20 to 30 cents per gallon), teas (Hyson at 40 cents/lb, other green at 24 cents, Souchong at 20 cents, Bohea at 12 cents), and coffee (5 cents/lb).

Excise taxes on domestic distilled spirits — on spirits distilled from foreign materials (molasses, sugar) at 11 to 20 cents per gallon by proof; on spirits from domestic materials in cities, towns, or villages at 9 to 17 cents per gallon; and on stills used in rural areas for domestic materials at 60 cents per gallon of still capacity per year.

Hamilton argues for carrying these duties “as high as will be consistent with the practicability of a safe collection,” noting that these articles are “in reality, luxuries — the greatest part of them foreign luxuries; some of them, in the excess in which they are used, pernicious luxuries.” The consumption of ardent spirits “is carried to an extreme, which is truly to be regretted, as well in regard to the health and the morals, as to the economy of the community.” Should higher duties decrease consumption, “the effect would be, in every respect desirable.”

Collection mechanism: Hamilton proposes an interior inspection system — pursuing each article from importation “into the hands of the dealers in it” — as the only way to reconcile high duties with secure collection. Under the existing system, “the evasion of the duties depends, as it were, on a single risk” — landing goods in defiance of customs officers — and “where the inducement is equivalent to the risk, there will be found too many who are willing to run it.” An interior scrutiny so greatly enhances the risk of detection that “few, in comparison, will venture to incur it. Indeed every dealer, who is not himself the fraudulent importer, then becomes, in some sort, a centinel upon him.”

He includes strong protections against official misconduct — officers are made criminally punishable and answerable in damages, and even where seizures are made with probable cause, if the article is acquitted, compensation for injury and detention is made from the public treasury.

The estimated net product of the proposed duties is about $1,703,400, which, together with existing duties on imports and tonnage, should complete the sum required. Hamilton acknowledges the uncertainty of data in “so unexplored a field” and recommends an auxiliary resource for the first year.

VII. A Sinking Fund

The report concludes with a plan for a sinking fund — a designated fund to purchase and retire public debt securities in the open market. Hamilton proposes that the net product of the post-office (which the postmaster-general estimates at not less than $100,000 annually, and which must be a growing fund from its nature) be vested in commissioners — comprising the Vice-President (or President of the Senate), the Speaker of the House, the Chief Justice, the Secretary of the Treasury, and the Attorney-General — to be applied to discharging the public debt, either by market purchases or principal payments, until the whole debt is discharged.

Hamilton explicitly addresses the maxim that “public debts are public benefits”: “Persuaded as the Secretary is, that the proper funding of the present debt will render it a national blessing; yet he is so far from acceding to the position, in the latitude in which it is sometimes laid down … a position inviting to prodigality, and liable to dangerous abuse, that he ardently wishes to see it incorporated, as a fundamental maxim, in the system of public credit of the United States, that the creation of debt should always be accompanied with the means of extinguishment. This he regards as the true secret for rendering public credit immortal.”

He further proposes that the commissioners be authorized (with the President’s approval) to borrow up to 12million,tobeappliedto:(1)payinginterestandinstalmentsoftheforeigndebt(3,491,923); (2) covering any deficiency in funds for domestic debt interest; (3) refinancing the five per cent portion of the foreign debt at a lower rate; and (4) purchasing public debt in the market while it remains below true value. This last measure would become “altogether unexceptionable” after funding is in place — it would raise stock value in favour of creditors, and the difference between true value and purchase price would be “clear gain to the public.” Hamilton argues that if the government does not undertake this operation, foreigners will reap the profit: “By the government’s entering into competition with them, it will not only reap a part of the profit itself, but will contract the extent, and lessen the extra profit of foreign purchases.”

He concludes by urging no further delay — delay would “sink the price of stock” and tempt foreign speculation, and “millions would probably be lost to the United States.” The report is submitted with the hope “that an effectual plan for that purpose may, during the present session, be the result of the united wisdom of the legislature.”
