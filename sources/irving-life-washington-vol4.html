Irving — The Life of George Washington, Vol. IV

Washington Irving (1783–1859) — The Life of George Washington. — In Four Volumes. Volume IV. Desmond Publishing Co., Boston, 1899.

This is Volume IV (the concluding volume) of Washington Irving’s five-volume biography of George Washington, first published 1855–1859. It covers the period from the Siege of Yorktown (1781) through Washington’s death (1799) — the closing of the Revolutionary War, the formation of the American republic under the Constitution, Washington’s two terms as president, the rise of partisan politics, and his final retirement at Mount Vernon.

The volume contains 456 page-files (OCR’d from the University of Florida library copy), extracted as 25,468 lines (~1.2 MB) of plain text.

Scope: Covers 1781–1799 — the siege and surrender of Yorktown, the dissolution of the Continental Army, Washington’s retirement to Mount Vernon, the framing and ratification of the Federal Constitution, his unanimous election as first President, the establishment of executive departments under Hamilton and Jefferson, the rise of the two-party system, the French Revolution’s impact on American politics, the Genet affair, Jay’s Treaty, the Whiskey Rebellion, Washington’s Farewell Address, the Quasi-War with France, his final appointment as Commander-in-Chief, and his death.

Chapter-by-Chapter Summary

CHAPTER I — The Siege of Yorktown (pp. 13–20)

Irving opens with Cornwallis aroused from his dream of security by the appearance of Count de Grasse’s fleet within the Chesapeake capes on August 28, 1781. Three French ships anchor at the mouth of York River, and 3,300 land forces under the Marquis de St. Simon are transported up James River to join Lafayette. Cornwallis finds his retreat to the Carolinas cut off — York River blocked by French ships, James River filled with transports. He strengthens his works and sends expresses to Clinton. De Grasse, eager to return to the West Indies, urges an immediate attack, but Lafayette wisely waits for Washington and Rochambeau. Admiral Graves arrives with 20 British sail; De Grasse puts to sea with 24 ships. The naval action on September 7 is indecisive, but De Barras arrives with siege artillery, and Graves eventually withdraws to New York. Washington hurries troops forward — “Every day we now lose is comparatively an age” — and on September 14 arrives at Williamsburg.

CHAPTER II — The Surrender of Yorktown (pp. 21–25)

Irving narrates the formal siege operations beginning September 28. The allied armies of 16,000 men — 9,000 French and 7,000 Americans — invest Yorktown. Washington fires the first gun on October 9. The bombardment devastates the British works. On October 14, American and French troops storm Redoubts 9 and 10 in a brilliant night assault led by Hamilton for the Americans and Lafayette’s aide for the French. Cornwallis, his position untenable, attempts an evacuation across the river that fails due to a storm. On October 17, he sends a flag of truce; formal surrender takes place on October 19. The British march out between the French and American lines, their band playing “The World Turned Upside Down.”

CHAPTER III — Aftermath of the Victory (pp. 26–36)

Irving describes the dissolution of the combined armies after Yorktown. Washington hurries to Eltham, where he finds his stepson John Parke Custis dying of camp fever. Custis expires on November 5, leaving four young children whom Washington will raise. Washington at Mount Vernon — his first visit in over six years — is brief. Lafayette sails for France to urge further aid. Washington stimulates Congress to prepare for the next campaign. The case of Captain Asgill, a British officer threatened with execution in retaliation for a murder, is described — Washington is relieved when Congress releases Asgill, sparing Washington a painful duty.

CHAPTER IV — The Close of the War (pp. 37–43)

Irving shows Washington maintaining precautions even as peace rumors circulate. Sir Guy Carleton, replacing Clinton, brings conciliatory news. Discontents arise in the army over back pay and pensions. Colonel Nicola writes an extraordinary letter suggesting Washington become king; Washington’s indignant reply crushes the notion: “No occurrence in the course of the war has given me more painful sensations.” The allied armies join on the Hudson. Congress contemplates reducing the army, heightening officers’ anxieties.

CHAPTER V — The Newburg Address (pp. 44–56)

Irving narrates the Newburg crisis of March 1783. Discontents among officers at Newburg reach a peak. Anonymous addresses circulate in camp urging the army to defy Congress if their grievances are not met. On March 15, Washington calls a meeting of officers. He makes an impassioned address, pausing to put on spectacles and saying, “Gentlemen, you will permit me to put on my spectacles, for I have grown not only gray but blind in the service of my country.” The gesture moves the officers to tears; they pass resolutions of loyalty. Washington’s moral authority saves the republic from a military coup.

CHAPTER VI — News of Peace (pp. 57–65)

Irving recounts the official news of peace in April 1783. Washington issues a proclamation of the cessation of hostilities. The Order of the Cincinnati is formed by officers desiring to perpetuate their association. Washington writes a circular letter to the state governors urging a “more perfect union.” A mutiny in the Pennsylvania Line over pay is suppressed. Washington tours the northern posts on Lake Champlain and the Mohawk Valley, assessing potential for westward expansion.

CHAPTER VII — Washington Resigns His Commission (pp. 66–77)

Irving describes the formal discharge of the army and Washington’s parting address. On November 25, 1783, the British evacuate New York; Washington enters the city in triumph. In Fraunces Tavern on December 4, he bids farewell to his officers — each man steps forward in silence to shake his hand, tears streaming down faces. He then proceeds to Annapolis, where Congress sits, and on December 23 formally resigns his commission. He tells Congress, “Having now finished the work assigned me, I retire from the great theatre of action.” The moment establishes the principle of civilian control of the military.

CHAPTER VIII — Mount Vernon and Domestic Life (pp. 78–81)

Irving paints a picture of Washington’s long-desired retirement at Mount Vernon. He enjoys a soldier’s repose, planning domestic improvements. The Council of Pennsylvania offers him a gift of land and stock, which he declines. He resumes agricultural life, visits his western lands with Dr. Craik, and entertains Lafayette for the last time before Lafayette returns to France. Irving includes recollections of the Fairfax family and Washington’s wistful glances at the ruins of Belvoir.

CHAPTER IX — Rural Life and Schemes of Improvement (pp. 82–95)

Irving describes Washington’s schemes for inland navigation. He refuses shares of stock offered by Virginia for his services but agrees to accept them for public uses such as education. The chapter covers his management of the estate, his tax of letter-writing and sitting for portraits, his ornamental gardening, and the reverential awe he inspired. An account of a visit from Mr. Watson reveals Washington’s festive gaiety, his passion for hunting, and his habit of reading the newspapers at breakfast. The death of General Greene is covered — Washington’s heartfelt regrets and encomiums.

CHAPTER X — The Federal Constitution (pp. 96–106)

Irving shows Washington’s growing doubt about the solidity of the Confederation. Correspondence with John Jay reveals his concern that the Articles of Confederation are inadequate. Shays’ Rebellion in Massachusetts (1786–87) alarms him. He heads the Virginia delegation to the Constitutional Convention in Philadelphia. Elected president of the convention, his presence lends gravitas to the proceedings. The Constitution is hammered out through the summer of 1787 and ratified by the states. Washington writes that the new government is “a little or a great thing according to the wisdom with which it is administered.”

CHAPTER XI — The First Presidency (pp. 107–118)

Irving describes how Washington is universally talked of for the presidency. His letters express genuine reluctance. He is unanimously elected. On April 30, 1789, he takes the oath of office on the balcony of Federal Hall in New York. The inauguration is a solemn ceremony witnessed by crowds. Chancellor Livingston administers the oath; Washington adds “So help me God” and kisses the Bible. Later, in his inaugural address to Congress, he speaks of “the preservation of the sacred fire of liberty and the destiny of the republican model of government.”

CHAPTER XII — Organizing the Government (pp. 119–126)

Irving details the anxious first days of the new government. Washington must create executive departments from scratch. He consults John Jay, Hamilton, James Madison, and Henry Knox. Hamilton is appointed Secretary of the Treasury, Knox Secretary of War. The cabinet begins to take shape. Washington’s position is lonely — he has no constitutional advisers initially. He relies heavily on Hamilton’s energetic mind.

CHAPTER XIII — The President’s Tour and the Death of His Mother (pp. 127–130)

Irving recounts Washington’s journey through the Eastern states in October 1789. John Hancock, Governor of Massachusetts, creates a contest of etiquette over who should pay the first call. Washington visits the scenes of his early military campaigns, the manufacturing establishments of Beverly, and Portsmouth, New Hampshire. He is received everywhere with joy. His mother, Mary Ball Washington, dies on August 25, 1789, at Fredericksburg. Irving sketches her character — a woman of strong mind who had raised Washington in piety and firmness.

CHAPTER XIV — Washington’s Illness and the Death of His Mother (pp. 131–135)

Washington suffers an alarming illness (a malignant carbuncle) that nearly kills him. The Senate rejects one of his nominations for a naval officer in Savannah, and he makes a sensitive vindication of his prerogative. The executive departments are instituted: Treasury (Hamilton), War (Knox), and State (temporarily John Jay). Hamilton is instructed to report a financial plan. The judiciary is organized under Chief Justice John Jay. Edmund Randolph serves as Attorney General. Congress adjourns.

CHAPTER XV — Jefferson and the French Revolution (pp. 136–147)

Irving introduces Thomas Jefferson, who is in Paris as minister, immersed in French politics. The contrast between Jefferson’s enthusiastic support for the French Revolution and Gouverneur Morris’s conservative views is drawn. News of the storming of the Bastille arrives in America, causing popular excitement. Washington maintains a cautious opinion. Hamilton is apprehensive. Jefferson is offered the State Department and, after hesitation, accepts, arriving in New York in March 1790.

CHAPTER XVI — Journey through the Eastern States (pp. 148–161)

Irving details Washington’s journey through the Eastern states, including a contest of etiquette with John Hancock in Boston. Washington records his entry into Boston, his reception, and an address from the Cincinnati Society. He returns to New York, completing the tour that strengthens national unity.

CHAPTER XVII — Northern Tour and Indian Affairs (pp. 162–167)

Washington’s Eastern tour continues. He visits Beverly’s manufacturing establishments, the new State of Rhode Island (which had belatedly ratified the Constitution), and Portsmouth. He boards the French squadron and embarks for New York, then tours Long Island before returning to Mount Vernon.

CHAPTER XVIII — Rhode Island and Mount Vernon (pp. 168–177)

Irving recounts Lafayette at the head of the French Revolution, writing to Washington in enthusiastic terms. Gouverneur Morris provides a different, more skeptical view. Lafayette presents Washington with the key of the Bastille — a symbol of the revolution. Washington visits Rhode Island and Mount Vernon, maintaining a careful silence on French affairs while privately expressing anxiety.

CHAPTER XIX — Frontier War with the Indians (pp. 178–185)

Irving describes frontier difficulties with Indians northwest of the Ohio. General Harmer’s expedition against the Miami towns in 1790 is a failure — Colonel Hardin’s detachment is ambushed, and a second detachment is compelled to retreat. Washington endures long anxiety awaiting the result, which ultimately forces a more serious expedition.

CHAPTER XX — Hamilton’s Financial System (pp. 186–194)

Irving describes Hamilton’s landmark financial arrangements in 1790–1791. Congress moves to Philadelphia — the new temporary capital. Hamilton submits his reports on public credit, proposing the assumption of state debts by the federal government and the creation of a national bank. Jefferson objects on constitutional grounds, beginning the great constitutional debate over implied powers. Two political parties form under Hamilton (Federalist) and Jefferson (Democratic-Republican). Kentucky and Vermont are admitted to the Union. A new expedition under General St. Clair is projected against hostile tribes — Washington issues solemn warnings to St. Clair before his departure.

CHAPTER XXI — Southern Tour and French Affairs (pp. 195–204)

Irving describes Washington’s Southern tour in 1791 — through Virginia, the Carolinas, and Georgia. He visits Savannah and Charleston, where he is greeted with enthusiasm. He writes to Lafayette, who depicts the troubles of a patriot leader. Jefferson’s optimistic view of the French Revolution is contrasted with John Adams’s distrust. The flight and recapture of Louis XVI reaches Washington; he expresses satisfaction when the King accepts the Constitution.

CHAPTER XXII — St. Clair’s Disaster (pp. 205–211)

Irving recounts St. Clair’s disastrous campaign against the Western Indians. On November 4, 1791, St. Clair is surprised by a confederation of Miami, Shawnee, and Delaware warriors near the Wabash River. Over 600 American soldiers are killed and the entire force routed — the worst defeat ever inflicted on the U.S. Army by Native Americans. Washington, dining with guests when the news arrives, controls his emotions until alone, then explodes with rage at St. Clair’s unpreparedness.

CHAPTER XXIII — Washington Contemplates Retirement (pp. 212–221)

Irving shows Washington’s growing weariness with public life. The apportionment bill leads to the first use of the veto power. He asks Madison to prepare a draft of a Farewell Address. Wayne is appointed to succeed St. Clair. Jefferson insists on retiring as Secretary of State at the end of Washington’s first term. Washington is deeply troubled — he cannot spare Jefferson from the divided cabinet.

CHAPTER XXIV — Discord in the Cabinet (pp. 222–228)

Irving details the open warfare between Hamilton and Jefferson. Jefferson suspects Hamilton of monarchical designs; Hamilton considers Jefferson a dangerous visionary. Freneau’s National Gazette attacks the administration. Washington’s healing admonitions to both — urging harmony and warning against party spirit — fail to reconcile them. The excise law meets violent opposition in Pennsylvania. Washington issues a proclamation against the Whiskey rebels.

CHAPTER XXV — Second Term Begins (pp. 229–232)

Irving describes Washington’s unanimous re-election in 1792. He is inaugurated for his second term on March 4, 1793 — his address is brief and solemn, only 133 words. The state of public affairs is gloomy with the French Revolution entering its most violent phase and war between France and England imminent.

CHAPTER XXVI — The French Revolution and Lafayette’s Imprisonment (pp. 233–238)

Irving recounts how Gouverneur Morris, now minister to France, reports the alarming progress of the Revolution. The overthrow of the French monarchy, the September massacres, and the imprisonment of Lafayette shake Washington. He writes a kind letter to the Marchioness Lafayette expressing his concern. Jefferson remains optimistic despite the massacres, but Washington is deeply shocked.

CHAPTER XXVII — Proclamation of Neutrality and the Genet Affair (pp. 239–248)

Irving describes Washington’s entrance upon his second term under gloomy auspices. Louis XVI is executed on January 21, 1793. France declares war on England. Belligerent excitement sweeps America. On April 22, 1793, Washington issues his Proclamation of Neutrality, declaring the United States “friendly and impartial toward the belligerent powers.” Citizen Genet, the new French minister, lands at Charleston and commissions privateers against British shipping before even presenting his credentials. He is greeted with wild enthusiasm in Philadelphia. Jefferson and Hamilton clash over the proper response; Washington calmly steers a middle course.

CHAPTER XXVIII — Genet’s Diplomacy (pp. 249–256)

Genet presents his letter of credence to Washington on May 18, 1793, despite his unwarranted proceedings at Charleston. Irving describes the diplomatic sparring. The British ship Grange is captured by French privateers in Delaware Bay. Washington demands restitution. Genet’s insolent tone — threatening to appeal from the President to the people — shocks the administration. Washington becomes increasingly sensitive to press attacks.

CHAPTER XXIX — The Little Sarah and Genet’s Recall (pp. 257–265)

Irving narrates the crisis of the Little Sarah, a British prize that Genet outfits as a French privateer in Philadelphia. Washington is called to Mount Vernon by the death of his estate manager. In his absence, the cabinet agonizes over what to do. Hamilton and Knox want to seize the vessel by force; Jefferson urges caution. Genet gives evasive assurances; the vessel sails in defiance on July 14. Washington’s fury on his return is described by Jefferson: “The President burst forth into one of those transports of passion beyond his control.” The Democratic Society forms in Philadelphia in imitation of Jacobin clubs. The recall of Genet is determined.

CHAPTER XXX — Genet’s Fall and the Cabinet’s Dissolution (pp. 266–274)

Irving describes the threatened dissolution of the cabinet. The French frigate Ambuscade defeats the British Boston in a single-ship action and returns to New York in triumph. A French fleet arrives. Genet enters New York amid cheering multitudes, tricolor cockades everywhere. He is informed of the measures for his recall and reacts with rage. His popularity quickly declines when it emerges that he threatened to appeal from the President to the people.

CHAPTER XXXI — Neutrality Threatened (pp. 275–280)

Irving details how British impressment of American seamen and seizures of American vessels bound for France endanger neutrality. Congress assembles in December 1793. Washington’s opening speech censures Genet. The Vice-President (Adams) alludes approvingly to the President’s firmness. The administration is in a minority in the House. Jefferson presents his report on commerce and then retires from the cabinet. His parting rebuke to Genet is a model of dignity. Jefferson’s private character of Washington — “his integrity was most pure, his judgment most inflexible” — is quoted.

CHAPTER XXXII — British Spoliations (pp. 281–285)

Irving describes the debate on Jefferson’s report on commercial intercourse. A naval force is proposed to protect against Algerian corsairs. Genet’s recall is followed by the arrival of his successor Fauchet. British captures of American vessels heighten public irritation. An embargo is laid. War with England seems imminent. The partisans of France are in the ascendant. Washington, determined to prevent war, resolves to send a special envoy to the British government.

CHAPTER XXXIII — Monroe Appointed to France and the Whiskey Rebellion (pp. 286–291)

Irving recounts James Monroe’s appointment as minister to France in place of Gouverneur Morris (whose recall the French demanded). Meanwhile, the Whiskey Rebellion erupts in western Pennsylvania — farmers resist the excise tax on whiskey. Washington issues a proclamation on August 7, 1794. When the insurgents persist, he issues a second proclamation and marches with 15,000 militia — the first and only time a sitting president commands troops in the field. The insurgents disperse without bloodshed. Washington denounces “self-created societies” for fomenting the rebellion.

CHAPTER XXXIV — Self-Created Societies and Wayne’s Campaign (pp. 292–296)

Washington’s denunciation of self-created societies is not relished by Congress. Irving describes General Wayne’s campaign against the Indians — the victory at Fallen Timbers on August 20, 1794. Hamilton reports a plan for the redemption of the public debt and retires as Secretary of the Treasury, succeeded by Oliver Wolcott. Knox also resigns, succeeded by Timothy Pickering. The session closes.

CHAPTER XXXV — Jay’s Treaty (pp. 297–307)

Irving details the anxious progress of the Jay Treaty negotiations. Jay’s Treaty arrives for ratification in March 1795. Its terms are unpopular — it secures British evacuation of Western posts and some commercial advantages but says nothing about impressment or neutral rights. The Senate ratifies with a reservation concerning West Indian trade. The treaty is published and met with violent opposition — copies are burned in Boston, New York, and Philadelphia. Washington, despite personal reservations, resolves to ratify, believing it better than no treaty at all.

CHAPTER XXXVI — Randolph’s Resignation (pp. 308–313)

Washington is recalled from Mount Vernon by a mysterious letter from Pickering. Irving describes how an intercepted dispatch from Fauchet, the French minister, implicates Secretary of State Edmund Randolph in questionable conduct — suggesting Randolph had solicited French money. Randolph resigns when confronted. His subsequent vindication is incomplete; he writes a pamphlet bitterly attacking Washington, which he later regrets. Pickering is transferred to State; McHenry becomes Secretary of War. George Washington Lafayette, the general’s son, arrives in America.

CHAPTER XXXVII — The Farewell Address (pp. 314–322)

Irving describes the opening of Congress in December 1795. Washington presents a cheerful summary of events. The French colors — sent in exchange for those Monroe presented — are formally received. The House demands papers related to Jay’s Treaty, and Washington refuses — a landmark assertion of executive privilege. The treaty is ultimately implemented. Monroe is recalled from France; C. C. Pinckney is appointed. France adopts a resentful policy, ordering seizures of American vessels. Washington’s Farewell Address is published in September 1796.

CHAPTER XXXVIII — Last Days of the Presidency (pp. 323–330)

Washington meets Congress for the last time on December 7, 1796. Irving describes his speech recommending a military academy, national university, and gradual naval increase. The Senate’s reply is warm; the House’s more measured. Andrew Jackson votes against the reply, daring to dissent from the universal praise. The French minister Adet publishes an offensive letter attempting to influence the election. John Adams is declared President. Washington writes to Knox on the eve of retirement. Spurious letters from 1776 are republished — Washington denounces them as forgeries. His farewell dinner is an emotional affair.

CHAPTER XXXIX — At Mount Vernon Again (pp. 331–339)

Irving describes Washington’s return to Mount Vernon in March 1797. The influx of visitors is overwhelming. Lawrence Lewis arrives as an aide in entertaining. Washington’s counsel to his granddaughter Nelly Custis on love matters is recounted with charm. Nelly’s romantic episode with the young Carroll of Carrollton is described — Washington’s gentle intercession when she is caught wandering alone in the woods. George Washington Lafayette, the general’s son, arrives from France.

CHAPTER XL — The Quasi-War with France (pp. 340–347)

Irving recounts the XYZ Affair — the degrading treatment of American envoys by Talleyrand’s agents demanding bribes before negotiations could begin. The French Directory refuses to receive C. C. Pinckney. Congress prepares for war. Washington is appointed Commander-in-Chief of the provisional army. He accepts on the condition he need not take the field unless absolutely necessary. He arranges the three major-generals: Hamilton, Pinckney, and Knox. Knox is aggrieved at being ranked below Hamilton, but Washington insists.

CHAPTER XLI — Last Year of Washington’s Life (pp. 348–357)

Washington is taxed anew with the cares of office, organizing the army, corresponding with Lafayette (now released from Olmutz), and managing his estate. A marriage at Mount Vernon — Nelly Custis marries Lawrence Lewis. The appointment of a minister to the French Republic is noted. Washington’s activity on his estate continues despite his sixty-seventh year.

CHAPTER XLII — Washington’s Death (pp. 358–368)

Irving concludes with Washington’s final days. He digests a comprehensive plan for managing his estate, writes to Hamilton approving Hamilton’s plan for a military academy, and rides out in cold rain on December 12, 1799. He develops acute laryngitis. Despite being bled four times, his condition deteriorates. His last words include “I die hard, but I am not afraid to go” and “‘Tis well.” He expires between ten and eleven on the evening of December 14, 1799, aged 67. His funeral is simple and modest at Mount Vernon. His will provides for the emancipation of his slaves after Martha’s death — a final act of moral courage. Congress resolves to shroud the Speaker’s chair in black. The nation mourns.
