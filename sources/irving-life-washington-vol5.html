Irving — The Life of George Washington, Vol. V

Washington Irving (1783–1859) — The Life of George Washington. — Volume V. Extended Annotated Edition. Jazzybee Verlag, Altenmünster, 2014. ISBN: 9783849642204.

This is Volume V (the concluding volume) of Washington Irving’s five-volume biography of George Washington, first published 1855–1859. It covers the period from Washington’s inauguration as first President (1789) through his death (1799) — the establishment of the federal government, the rise of partisan politics under Hamilton and Jefferson, foreign crises with France and Britain, and Washington’s final retirement at Mount Vernon. Modern “Extended Annotated” introductory material (a biographical primer on Irving) has been stripped; the text is Irving’s original work only.

Scope: Covers 1789–1799 — Washington’s unanimous election and inauguration; the organization of the executive departments under Hamilton, Jefferson, and Knox; the creation of the federal judiciary; Hamilton’s financial system and the assumption of state debts; the location of the permanent capital; the rise of the Federalist and Republican parties; the French Revolution and its American repercussions; the Genet affair; the Whiskey Rebellion; Jay’s Treaty; the Farewell Address; Washington’s retirement to Mount Vernon; the Quasi-War with France; his appointment as Commander-in-Chief; his death and the provisions of his will.

Includes appendix material: Portraits of Washington (by H. T. Tuckerman); the full text of Washington’s Farewell Address; and the full text of Washington’s last will and testament.

Chapter-by-Chapter Summary

CHAPTER I — Washington Begins His Administration

Irving describes how the eyes of the world are upon Washington at the commencement of his administration. He must create the government from the ground up. The inauguration is held on April 30, 1789, on the balcony of Federal Hall in New York — Chancellor Livingston administers the oath before an immense crowd. In his inaugural address, Washington speaks of “the preservation of the sacred fire of liberty” as his guiding motive. He is acutely aware of the precedent his actions will set for all future presidents.

CHAPTER II — The President’s Household

Irving details how, the moment the inauguration is over, Washington perceives he is no longer master of himself or his home. The pressure for patronage begins immediately. Washington deliberately establishes formal etiquette for the presidential household — his weekly levees, his carriage, his manner of receiving visitors. Mrs. Adams’s description of his reception at the presidential mansion is quoted, noting his dignity and reserve. He maintains the distance necessary to preserve the respect due to the office.

CHAPTER III — Mrs. Washington’s Journey to New York

On May 17, 1789, Mrs. Washington sets out from Mount Vernon accompanied by her grandchildren Eleanor Custis and George Washington Parke Custis. Irving recounts her journey — she is received with honors along the route, including military escorts and addresses from local dignitaries. Her arrival and reception at the seat of government mark the establishment of a proper style for the President’s family. The social life of the presidential household is described, with Washington’s niece acting as hostess.

CHAPTER IV — Organizing the Departments

Irving shows Washington calling upon the heads of departments to furnish papers and documents respecting their duties. The Treasury, War, and State departments are established. The President’s cabinet begins to take shape with Hamilton as Treasury Secretary, Knox as War Secretary, and John Jay temporarily handling foreign affairs. The question of titles for the President — “His Highness” is proposed — is ultimately settled by Congress with the simple “President of the United States.”

CHAPTER V — The Cabinet Takes Shape

Irving describes how the Department of State still needs a head. John Jay is appointed the first Chief Justice of the Supreme Court. Washington offers the State Department to Thomas Jefferson, then minister to France. Jefferson hesitates, consulting his friends. After long deliberation, he accepts, though Irving notes his reluctance to leave the exciting political scene in Paris where the French Revolution is unfolding.

CHAPTER VI — Washington’s Eastern Tour

In October 1789, Washington embarks on a tour through the Eastern states. Irving describes his journey through Connecticut, Massachusetts, and New Hampshire — his reception by the people, his visit to the scenes of his early military campaigns, the manufacturing establishments of Beverly. The contest of etiquette with Governor John Hancock in Boston provides a notable episode: Hancock, suffering from gout, refuses to call on the President first; Washington stands on protocol; eventually Hancock relents.

CHAPTER VII — Trumbull and the Historical Paintings

Irving recounts how Colonel John Trumbull, Washington’s former aide-de-camp and now an eminent historical painter, arrives from England. Trumbull proposes a series of historical paintings of the Revolution. Washington sits for his portrait. The chapter includes their correspondence about the paintings, which will eventually adorn the Capitol Rotunda.

CHAPTER VIII — The First Congress Under the Constitution

Congress reassembles on January 4, 1790. Irving describes Washington’s address opening the session — he recommends attention to public credit, a naval force, Indian affairs, and the militia system. Hamilton’s epochal Report on Public Credit is presented on January 14, proposing that the federal government assume state debts and fund the national debt at par. The report sparks the great debate that will define early American politics.

CHAPTER IX — The Assumption of State Debts

Irving describes the long and angry debates over the assumption of state debts. Southern states, many of which had already paid their debts, oppose assuming the debts of northern states. A compromise is effected through Thomas Jefferson’s agency: in exchange for southern votes for assumption, the permanent capital will be located on the Potomac. The bill passes, and the seat of government is moved to Philadelphia for ten years before moving to the new federal city.

CHAPTER X — Affairs in France

Irving shows Washington’s attention often called from domestic affairs to the unfolding French Revolution. Lafayette, now at the head of the Revolution, writes enthusiastic letters full of hope. Gouverneur Morris, in Paris, reports a different view — he foresees the violence to come. Washington maintains a cautious silence in public but expresses anxiety in private correspondence.

CHAPTER XI — Indian Depredations on the Frontier

Irving recounts the frequent Indian depredations on the frontier settlements northwest of the Ohio. Washington authorizes offensive operations. General Harmer’s expedition against the Miami towns in 1790 is a failure — Colonel Hardin’s detachment is ambushed, and a second detachment is forced to retreat with heavy losses. Washington endures long anxiety awaiting the result.

CHAPTER XII — The Seat of Government Moves to Philadelphia

Congress reassembles at Philadelphia in December 1790. Washington’s residence at the new seat is established. His opening speech recommends attention to the national debt, the militia, and post roads. The creation of a national bank is proposed by Hamilton. Jefferson raises constitutional objections — the first major debate over implied powers. Washington requires written opinions from his cabinet, and Hamilton’s masterful defense of the bank’s constitutionality convinces Washington to sign the bill.

CHAPTER XIII — Washington’s Southern Tour

In March 1791, Washington sets out on a 1,887-mile tour through the Southern states. Irving describes his route through Virginia, North Carolina, South Carolina, and Georgia. He visits Savannah, Augusta, and Charleston, noting the condition of the Southern country. The tour is a triumphal progress, strengthening the bonds of union. He returns to Philadelphia in July.

CHAPTER XIV — Autumn at Mount Vernon

Irving recounts how Washington snatches a few weeks of autumn at Mount Vernon. He instructs a new agent in the management of the estate, supervises agricultural improvements, and enjoys the joy of being home after the cares of office. His passion for farming and his careful attention to every detail of the plantation are evident.

CHAPTER XV — The Apportionment Bill

A bill for apportioning representatives among the states is introduced. Irving describes how the bill gives an additional fraction to certain states in a manner Washington considers unconstitutional. He consults his cabinet and, convinced the bill violates the Constitution, exercises the first presidential veto on April 5, 1792. The veto establishes an important precedent for executive power.

CHAPTER XVI — Washington Considers Retirement

Irving recounts Jefferson’s painful letter expressing his desire to retire from the cabinet. Washington is deeply troubled — he cannot spare Jefferson, and the cabinet is already dangerously divided between Hamilton and Jefferson. Washington urges Jefferson to remain. He himself begins to consider retirement at the end of his first term.

CHAPTER XVII — Second Term Begins

Washington consents to be a candidate for re-election after a painful conflict of feelings. Irving notes there is no opposition — the electoral vote is unanimous. He is inaugurated on March 4, 1793, in Philadelphia. His address is brief and solemn, only a few sentences. The state of public affairs is gloomy: the French Revolution has entered its most violent phase, and war between France and England is imminent.

CHAPTER XVIII — Gouverneur Morris in France

Irving describes Gouverneur Morris’s appointment as minister plenipotentiary to the French court. His diplomatic correspondence from Paris details the progress of the French Revolution — the imprisonment of the king, the September massacres, the execution of Louis XVI. Morris’s gloomy dispatches contrast sharply with the enthusiastic reports of other observers. Washington’s anxiety for Lafayette, now imprisoned in Olmutz, is acute.

CHAPTER XIX — The French Revolution and American Neutrality

Irving shows Washington beginning his second term under gloomy auspices — a divided cabinet, increasing party exasperation, and a threatened abatement of his popularity. The execution of Louis XVI shocks the world. France declares war on England. On April 22, 1793, Washington issues his Proclamation of Neutrality, declaring the United States “friendly and impartial toward the belligerent powers.” The proclamation is met with fierce opposition from pro-French partisans.

CHAPTER XX — The Genet Affair Begins

Irving recounts the arrival of Citizen Genet as French minister in April 1793. Genet lands at Charleston and, before presenting his credentials, commissions privateers against British shipping, sets up French consular courts to adjudicate prizes, and appeals to the American people over the head of their government. He arrives in Philadelphia on May 18 and presents his letter of credence to a cold and wary Washington. The cabinet is divided on how to respond — Jefferson is sympathetic, Hamilton hostile.

CHAPTER XXI — The Little Sarah Crisis

Irving details the crisis of the Little Sarah in June–July 1793. While Washington is briefly at Mount Vernon, the French minister outfits the British prize Little Sarah as a French privateer in Philadelphia. Governor Mifflin is determined to prevent her departure. Jefferson urges delay until the President returns. Genet gives an evasive assurance, and the vessel sails in defiance on July 14. Washington’s fury on his return is vividly recorded by Jefferson — he bursts out that he had rather be in his grave than in his present situation. The Democratic Society of Philadelphia is formed in imitation of Jacobin clubs.

CHAPTER XXII — The Cabinet Threatens to Dissolve

Irving describes how Washington, already annoyed by managing a divided cabinet, is now threatened with its dissolution. Hamilton announces his intention to retire. Jefferson does the same. Washington calls on Jefferson at his country residence, picturing his “deep distress” at being deserted. Their remarkable private conversation is recorded: Washington denies any monarchical inclinations and declares no man would oppose such a change more decidedly than himself. A compromise keeps Jefferson until January 1794. Genet visits New York and receives a hero’s welcome — the Ambuscade has just defeated the Boston in a single-ship action. But when Genet’s threat to appeal from the President to the people becomes known, his popularity collapses.

CHAPTER XXIII — British Spoliations and Neutrality Endangered

Irving details how British measures endanger neutrality in 1793. British cruisers are instructed to detain all vessels bound to France with provisions. American seamen are impressively pressed into British service. The Western posts are held in violation of the peace treaty. The hostilities of the Indians continued, attributed by many to British influence. Congress assembles in December 1793. Washington’s opening speech urges defense preparations and warns against foreign entanglements. Jefferson’s report on commercial intercourse is presented.

CHAPTER XXIV — Public Affairs Grow More Complicated

Irving shows public affairs becoming more complicated. Events in Europe are gloomy. The debate on Jefferson’s commercial report becomes heated — Fisher Ames and William Giles clash. Genet’s machinations continue — he sends emissaries to Kentucky to recruit for an expedition against Spanish Louisiana. British captures of American vessels heighten public irritation to fever pitch. Washington, determined to avoid war, resolves to send a special envoy to England. Hamilton recommends John Jay for the mission.

CHAPTER XXV — Genet Recalled and Monroe Appointed

Irving recounts the recall of Genet by the French government in February 1794 — his successor Fauchet arrives. James Monroe is appointed minister to France in place of Gouverneur Morris. Washington writes a warm letter of support to Morris despite the recall. Monroe is received in Paris with enthusiasm — the Convention decrees the French and American flags be entwined in the hall. The news of Genet’s successor pacifies the immediate crisis, but French-American relations remain strained.

CHAPTER XXVI — The Whiskey Rebellion

Irving describes the insurrection in Western Pennsylvania against the excise law in the summer of 1794. The insurgents fire upon the federal marshal, attack the inspector’s house, and compel a surrender. Washington issues a proclamation on August 7. When the insurgents persist, he calls out 15,000 militia — the largest army ever assembled in America to that date — and marches in person to Carlisle and Bedford. The insurgents disperse without a shot fired. Washington denounces “self-created societies” for fomenting the rebellion — a bold step that incurs lasting enmity. Hamilton reports a plan for the redemption of the public debt and retires as Treasury Secretary, succeeded by Oliver Wolcott. Knox also resigns, succeeded by Timothy Pickering.

CHAPTER XXVII — Jay’s Treaty

Irving describes Washington’s anxious watch over the mission of John Jay to England. Jay’s Treaty arrives for ratification in March 1795. Washington studies it closely — some provisions are satisfactory, others not. On the whole he considers it the best treaty attainable. The Senate ratifies on June 24 with a reservation concerning West Indian trade. When an abstract of the treaty is published, the country explodes in opposition — treaty copies are burned in Boston, New York, and Philadelphia. Washington resolves to ratify despite the clamor, writing that he will not substitute the opinions of others for his own conviction. The treaty is signed on August 18, 1795.

CHAPTER XXVIII — Washington at Mount Vernon

Irving shows Washington trying to enjoy Mount Vernon while constant political crises summon him back to Philadelphia. A mysterious letter from Pickering brings him hurrying back — an intercepted dispatch from French Minister Fauchet appears to implicate Secretary of State Edmund Randolph in soliciting French money. Washington confronts Randolph, who resigns in anger. Randolph publishes a vindication attacking Washington, which he later deeply regrets. Pickering is transferred to State; McHenry becomes War Secretary. George Washington Lafayette, the general’s son, arrives from France.

CHAPTER XXIX — The President’s Speech at the Opening of Congress

Irving describes Washington’s speech at the opening of Congress in December 1795 — a cheerful summary of events. Wayne’s victory at Fallen Timbers (August 1794) has led to the Treaty of Greenville, ending the Indian war. Treaties with Algiers and Morocco secure peace. Washington receives the colors of France. The House demands papers related to Jay’s Treaty; Washington refuses — a landmark assertion of executive privilege. The House ultimately votes the necessary appropriations for the treaty. Monroe is recalled from France and C. C. Pinckney appointed.

CHAPTER XXX — Washington Considers a Third Term

Irving describes Washington’s firm resolve to retire rather than stand for a third term. He turns to Hamilton to help recast his Farewell Address, working from Madison’s earlier draft. The address is published in September 1796 in the Philadelphia Daily Advertiser. It warns against the baneful effects of party spirit, against permanent foreign alliances, and in favor of union, religion, morality, and public credit. The address is read throughout the country with profound emotion.

CHAPTER XXXI — Retirement to Mount Vernon

Irving describes how Washington, his official career terminated, sets out for Mount Vernon on March 9, 1797, accompanied by Mrs. Washington, Nelly Custis, and George Washington Parke Custis. He reaches home a private citizen. His joy is unbounded — he writes to friends of his happiness at being “once more master of his own time and roof.” The daily round of a Virginia planter begins anew: rising with the sun, riding the farms, entertaining the constant stream of visitors.

CHAPTER XXXII — Washington Returns to Duty

Despite his retirement, Washington is soon called back. Irving describes how the French Directory refuses to receive the American minister and orders C. C. Pinckney to leave France. President Adams summons a special session of Congress in May 1797. The XYZ Affair — the degrading demand for bribes by Talleyrand’s agents before negotiations could begin — enrages the nation. War with France seems inevitable. On July 4, 1797, Washington writes to Adams that he would not “entrench himself under the cover of age and retirement” if his country required him. He is unanimously appointed Lieutenant-General and Commander-in-Chief of the American army.

CHAPTER XXXIII — Washington at Philadelphia Organizing the Army

Irving describes how Washington leaves his retirement in November 1798 and repairs to Philadelphia at the earnest request of the Secretary of War. He meets with public officers and arranges the plan for raising a provisional army. The selection of general officers proves difficult — Hamilton, Pinckney, and Knox. Knox is deeply aggrieved at being ranked below Hamilton. Washington writes a kind but firm letter to Knox, but the wound festers. Hamilton is appointed Inspector-General and second in command.

CHAPTER XXXIV — Last Days and Death

Irving’s closing chapter is a moving account of Washington’s final days. Winter has set in, yet Washington keeps up his active round — riding out daily to superintend his farms. On December 12, 1799, he rides in cold rain and snow. He develops acute laryngitis. Despite being bled repeatedly — a total of about 80 ounces of blood — his condition worsens. His last words are calm and dignified: “I die hard, but I am not afraid to go” and “‘Tis well.” He expires between ten and eleven on the evening of December 14, 1799, aged 67. His funeral on December 18 is simple and modest at Mount Vernon. His will provides for the emancipation of his slaves after Martha’s death. Congress shrouds the Speaker’s chair in black. A deep sorrow spreads across the nation and the world — Lord Bridport lowers his flag half-mast in Torbay, and Bonaparte orders black crape suspended from all French standards for ten days. Irving concludes with a reflection on Washington’s character: prudence, firmness, sagacity, moderation, an immovable justice, a courage that never faltered.

Appendix

  • Portraits of Washington — Notices of the various representations of Washington, prepared by H. T. Tuckerman for the illustrated edition of this work, describing the portraits by Peale, Trumbull, Stuart, Pine, Wertmuller, Ceracchi, Houdon, and Crawford, from 1772 to the last sittings.
  • Washington’s Farewell Address — The full text of the Farewell Address, September 17, 1796 — Washington’s reflections on the dangers of party spirit, the preservation of the Union, the importance of religion and morality, and the policy of avoiding permanent alliances with foreign nations.
  • Washington’s Will — The full text of Washington’s last will and testament, dated July 9, 1790 (with later codicils), including the provisions for the emancipation of his slaves upon the death of Mrs. Washington.
