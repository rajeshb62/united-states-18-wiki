Overview

Volume I opens Hildreth’s continuous narrative of British America with discovery, Spanish and French rivals, and the planting of the English colonies through the late Stuart age. The Advertisement rejects “centennial sermons and Fourth-of-July orations” and promises founders “unbedaubed with patriotic rouge,” drawn from laws, state papers, documents, and manuscript collections. The revised three volumes, Hildreth states, give “a pretty correct outline” of British America nearly three centuries down to the organization of the United States under the Federal Constitution.

Chapters I–XV cover voyages and territorial claims; the aspect and aboriginal inhabitants of North America; early Spanish, French, and Dutch lodgments; Virginia; New Netherland; Plymouth and Laconia; Massachusetts Bay, New Hampshire, Ligonia, and Pemaquid; Maryland and Virginia; the growth of Connecticut, Providence, Rhode Island, New Haven, and Maine; New England under the Long Parliament; Virginia and Maryland in the English civil wars and Commonwealth; New England under the Commonwealth; New Sweden, New Netherland, and the English conquest; New England under Charles II; and Virginia and Maryland under Charles II and James II. Full Chapter-by-Chapter Breakdown

Chapter I — Voyages of Discovery. Territorial Claims

Columbus sails while the Cape route to India is still unknown; Marco Polo’s East fires his design (1492). The chapter surveys Spanish, Portuguese, English, French, and later Dutch claims from first Atlantic crossings through Cabot, Verrazzano, Cartier, Raleigh’s patents, and competing papal and royal titles that frame later colonial charters.

Chapter II — Aspect and Aboriginal Inhabitants of North America

Within half a century of discovery the Spaniards occupy large tropical tracts; island colonies suffer native depopulation. Hildreth turns to the physical face of the temperate continent and to languages, war, hunting, fishing, and social customs of the aboriginal nations of the regions later colonized by the English—material he treats as background to settlement rather than romance.

Chapter III — Carolina. Colony of Saint Augustine. New Mexico. Virginia. Acadie. New France. New Netherland

Religious revolution divides Western Europe into Protestant and Catholic parties with “very important influences on the colonization of North America.” First lodgments: Spanish St. Augustine and New Mexico; French Acadie and New France; Dutch on the Hudson; abortive or early English attempts on the Carolina and Virginia coasts before the successful Jamestown enterprise.

Chapter IV — Settlement of Virginia

London Company charter names Gates, Somers, Hakluyt, and Wingfield (1606); Sir Thomas Smith and other merchants join. Voyage, arrival, Jamestown, Starving Time, Dale and Argall, tobacco, the House of Burgesses, and the shift from company rule toward royal province—the first durable English plantation on the Chesapeake.

Chapter V — Settlement of New Netherland

Two years after the first English colony in Virginia, and in the year of Hudson’s river discovery (1609), the Dutch obtain a Spanish truce amounting to independence. Dutch East India Company origins, West India Company, Fort Orange and Manhattan, trade with the Iroquois, and early friction with English neighbors.

Chapter VI — New England. Colony of New Plymouth. Laconia

Virginia’s first charter contemplated a second, northern colony (Hanham, Raleigh Gilbert, Parker, Popham, 1606). Failed Popham / Sagadahoc venture; Separatist flight to Holland; Mayflower compact and Plymouth; fishing and trading posts; Gorges and the Council for New England; Laconia and northern grants.

Chapter VII — Colony of Massachusetts Bay. New Hampshire. Ligonia. Pemaquid

Dorchester and other Puritan (non-separatist) projects seed Massachusetts Bay. Charter transfer, Winthrop fleet, freemen and General Court, New Hampshire settlements, Ligonia and Pemaquid on the eastern coast, and the uneasy border with French claims in Nova Scotia / Acadie.

Chapter VIII — Settlement of Maryland. Progress of Virginia

English Catholics face severer penalties than Puritan nonconformists. Calvert’s Avalon and Maryland charter; Ark and Dove; religious toleration experiments; boundary and trade friction with Virginia; Virginia’s tobacco economy and political consolidation in the same decades.

Chapter IX — Progress of Massachusetts. Connecticut. Providence. Rhode Island. New Haven. New Somerset. Maine

After freemen reclaim legislative rights (1634), deputies sit with magistrates. Connecticut River towns, Hooker’s migration, Pequot War, Williams at Providence, Portsmouth and Newport, Davenport’s New Haven, and Maine / New Somerset under Gorges and Massachusetts claims—proliferation of New England polities.

Chapter X — New England during the Long Parliament

Emigrant coin drains for imports; while migration continues, new arrivals restock money and demand. Currency, wampum, fishery, shipbuilding, manufactures; relations with the English Long Parliament; Antinomian aftermath; confederation impulses among the Puritan colonies amid civil war at home.

Chapter XI — Virginia and Maryland during the English Civil Wars and the Commonwealth

Long Parliament news rejoices Virginia as well as Massachusetts (1641). Burgesses seek liberties under the “happy Parliament”; Maryland’s proprietary crises; Claiborne and Ingle; submission and restoration of local authority under the Commonwealth—southern colonies navigating English revolution without New England’s theocratic frame.

Chapter XII — New England during the Commonwealth

Massachusetts freemen press for a complete code and fixed penalties; magistrates defend judicial discretion. Revised codes; persecution of Quakers; relations among the United Colonies; Rhode Island’s divergent charter path; and the posture of Puritan New England toward Cromwell’s England.

Chapter XIII — New Sweden. Progress of New Netherland. Its Conquest by the English

Usselinx, dissatisfied with the Dutch West India Company, finds a patron in Gustavus Adolphus; New Sweden on the Delaware. Stuyvesant’s rule; English pressure from Connecticut and Long Island; Nicolls and the 1664 conquest that renames New Netherland as New York under the Duke of York.

Chapter XIV — New England under Charles II

Puritan colonists watch the Restoration with anxiety (1660). The same ship that brings news of Charles II brings regicides Whalley and Goffe. Royal commissioners; Connecticut and Rhode Island charters; Massachusetts resistance and partial submission; King Philip’s War and its devastation; and the approach of charter crisis.

Chapter XV — Virginia and Maryland under Charles II and James II

Under the Commonwealth Virginia had enjoyed a popular government—taxpayers voting for burgesses, Assembly electing officers. Restoration reverses that course: royal governors, Navigation Acts enforcement, Bacon’s Rebellion, Culpeper and Howard, and Maryland’s proprietary and religious struggles under the later Stuarts, closing the volume on the eve of the Glorious Revolution (continued in Vol. II).

Relationship to the Wiki

Vol. I is the deep colonial background volume of Hildreth’s three-volume 1497–1789 half. This wiki’s center of gravity is the aftermath of the French and Indian War through the early republic, so Vol. I is retained as the continuous series base that makes Vol. II (intercolonial wars, Stamp Act, Townshend) and Vol. III (Revolution and Constitution) intelligible. It supplies settlement and institutional origin material for Virginia, Massachusetts, New York, Maryland, and the New England constellation that later supply revolutionary leadership. Continues in hildreth-history-united-states-vol2 (Ch. XVI–XXIX) and hildreth-history-united-states-vol3 (Ch. XXX–XLVIII); constitutional half is Vols. IV–VI in the United States 1800–65 wiki.
