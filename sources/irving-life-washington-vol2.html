Irving — The Life of George Washington, Vol. II

Washington Irving (1783–1859) — The Life of George Washington. — Volume II. Extended Annotated Edition. Jazzybee Verlag, Altenmünster, 2014. ISBN: 9783849642174.

This is Volume II of Washington Irving’s five-volume biography of George Washington, first published 1855–1859. This volume covers the early years of the American Revolutionary War, from Washington’s assumption of command at Cambridge (July 1775) through the campaigns of 1776 and the close of that year. Modern “Extended Annotated” introductory material (a biographical primer on Irving) has been stripped; the text is Irving’s original work only.

Scope: Covers July 1775 – December 1776 — Washington takes command of the Continental Army at Cambridge; the Siege of Boston; the invasion of Canada under Montgomery and Arnold; the Declaration of Independence; the Battle of Long Island; the retreat through New York and New Jersey; the victories at Trenton and Princeton; Washington earns the appellation “the American Fabius.”

Chapter-by-Chapter Summary

CHAPTER I — Washington Takes Command at Cambridge

On the 3rd of July, the morning after his arrival at Cambridge, Washington takes formal command of the army drawn up on the Common. Irving describes the review and the ancient elm under which Washington drew his sword as commander-in-chief. Washington’s first task is to assess the raw provincial forces — undisciplined, poorly equipped, and unfamiliar with European tactics — while the British hold Boston and Bunker’s Hill. He writes to Congress requesting supplies, powder, and a reorganization of the medical department.

CHAPTER II — Allaying Discontents Among the Generals

Washington’s justice and impartiality are tested as soon as he takes command, as Irving narrates his handling of precedence disputes among the generals. Major-General Ward’s claims and the rivalry between Connecticut and Massachusetts officers demand delicate diplomacy. Washington insists on merit over seniority, establishing an early precedent for his leadership style. The chapter reveals the sectional jealousies that would plague the army throughout the war.

CHAPTER III — The Siege of Boston Begins

Irving describes how Washington’s great object is to force the enemy out of Boston. His lines cut off the town from the country, but besieging a place defended by a superior naval force presents immense difficulties. The British hold Bunker’s Hill with strong works; their floating batteries and ships of war command the harbor. Washington’s lack of powder (scarcely nine rounds per man) forces him to feign strength while concealing critical shortages from both the enemy and his own officers.

CHAPTER IV — Affairs in Other Quarters

Events elsewhere demand Washington’s superintending care, as Irving recounts. The Northern Department under Schuyler plans the invasion of Canada. Arnold’s expedition through the wilderness of Maine is detailed — the grueling portage between the Kennebec and Dead Rivers, the loss of bateaux, the starvation of men forced to eat their cartridge boxes and moccasins. Meanwhile, Lord Dunmore’s depredations in Virginia and rising tensions in the Carolinas require Washington’s attention, stretching his command across the continent.

CHAPTER V — The Siege Continues

The siege of Boston continues without major action. Irving notes how the British strengthen their lines while Washington grapples with short enlistments, lack of powder, and the militia system. A council of war decides against a direct assault. Washington devises a plan for a night attack on Boston, but his officers advise against it as too risky. He acquiesces, though he later confides to Reed that he believes the enterprise would have succeeded with resolution.

CHAPTER VI — Naval Operations and the Canada Expedition

While the two expeditions threaten Canada from different quarters, war continues along the seaboard. Irving describes how the British in Boston, cut off from supplies, send out foraging parties. Washington commissions armed vessels — the beginning of the American navy. The schooner Hannah captures British transports. The burning of Falmouth (Portland, Maine) by Lieutenant Mowat on October 18, 1775 shocks the colonies: 139 dwellings and 228 stores are destroyed, and the act is viewed as proving British intent to wage a war of terror, pushing public sentiment toward independence.

CHAPTER VII — General Howe Takes Command in Boston

Irving details how General Howe, after taking command in Boston following Gage’s departure, proceeds to fortify the town. He clears houses and throws up redoubts. The Old South Church is desecrated — its pews removed, floor covered with earth, converted into a riding school for Burgoyne’s light dragoons. The North Church is demolished for fuel. Howe issues three proclamations restricting movement from the town. Washington, incensed by the Falmouth burning and Dunmore’s actions, retaliates by ordering the seizure of crown officers as hostages. Henry Knox steps forward, offering to bring artillery from Ticonderoga — the beginning of his legendary feat.

CHAPTER VIII — Dispatches from Canada

Irving recounts the dispatches from Schuyler giving news of the Canada expedition. Chamblee is captured by Montgomery. St. Johns is invested and surrenders after a stout defence by Major Preston. Arnold’s march through the Maine wilderness is described in vivid detail: the carrying-place, the loss of bateaux, the starvation when Colonel Enos turns back with 300 men. Arnold pushes on, reaching the Chaudière River, and emerges at Point Levi opposite Quebec on November 9. The inhabitants marvel that such a march was possible.

CHAPTER IX — Arnold Before Quebec

Arnold’s approach to Quebec. His army is reduced to a handful. Irving describes how he crosses the St. Lawrence and ascends the Plains of Abraham by moonlight, replicating Wolfe’s feat. He summons the town; the garrison fires upon his flag. Arnold awaits Montgomery’s arrival. When Montgomery descends from Montreal, the combined force of about 900 effective men prepares for an assault on the fortress in a driving snowstorm on the last night of 1775.

CHAPTER X — Reorganizing the Army

Irving details the immense difficulty of forming an army under new regulations. The militia system defeats efforts to raise regular regiments. Short enlistments leave Washington without troops as 1776 dawns — his army drops from over 20,000 to fewer than 10,000 men. He writes movingly to Congress and to Reed: “Search the volumes of history through, and I much question whether a case similar to ours is to be found.” The distress of the camp and the “unconquerable desire to return to their homes” among the soldiery are lyrically rendered.

CHAPTER XI — Washington’s Mind Turns to Mount Vernon

Amid the concerns of war, Washington’s thoughts revert to his home on the Potomac. Irving presents his correspondence with his manager Lund Washington: detailed directions for managing the estate, keeping up hospitality to the poor, and his famous instruction to give forty or fifty pounds a year in charity. Mrs. Washington arrives at Cambridge in her carriage with her son and daughter-in-law, bringing domestic grace to headquarters. Irving includes the anecdote of Washington breaking up a brawl between Virginia riflemen and Marblehead sailors by seizing two men by the throat.

CHAPTER XII — The Canadian Invasion Continued

Irving turns from the siege of Boston to the invasion of Canada. Montgomery and Arnold prepare for the assault on Quebec in a snowstorm on December 31, 1775. Montgomery is killed at the first barrier as he cries “Push on, my brave boys, Quebec is ours!” Arnold is wounded in the leg. Morgan and his riflemen fight heroically in the lower town but are forced to surrender. The attack fails; the shattered army retreats three miles from the city. Irving reflects that had Arnold fallen at Quebec, his name would have remained unsullied.

CHAPTER XIII — Lord Dunmore’s Proceedings in Virginia

A vessel captured bearing supplies from Lord Dunmore to Boston reveals his plan to proclaim liberty to indentured servants and slaves, threatening Virginia with a servile war. Norfolk is bombarded and burnt by Dunmore’s fleet. Washington writes that if Dunmore is not crushed before spring, he will become “the most formidable enemy America has.” Lee’s stern policy toward Tories is contrasted with Washington’s more moderate approach.

CHAPTER XIV — British Preparations to Evacuate Boston

Early in January 1776, great activity stirs in Boston harbor. Transports take in supplies for a removal of the army. Sir Henry Clinton sails with a secret expedition to the South. Washington, suspecting New York may be the next target, sends Lee to fortify it. Lee arrives in New York on February 4, puts the city under martial law, seizes Tories, and begins fortifications. His threat to chain Tory friends to houses if the British fire on the city keeps the inhabitants in a fever.

CHAPTER XV — Washington Still Hopeful of Canada

Irving shows Washington still hopeful about Canada despite gathering gloom. He expects tidings that Montgomery and Arnold possess Quebec. Instead, Schuyler’s dispatch announces the failure before Quebec, Montgomery’s death, and Arnold’s wounding. Washington is deeply moved but writes to Schuyler that “we must not despair.” He applies to Massachusetts, New Hampshire, and Connecticut for three regiments to reinforce Canada.

CHAPTER XVI — Schuyler’s Letter on the Canadian Reverse

Schuyler’s letter announces the disaster with manly feeling. He wishes he had no occasion to send such intelligence. Washington’s reply expresses deep sorrow at Montgomery’s fall and concern for the wounded Arnold. Measures are taken to reinforce the army. Schuyler, falsely accused by New Englanders of negligence in forwarding supplies, receives Washington’s unwavering support.

CHAPTER XVII — Winter Siege of Boston

The siege continues through the winter without striking incident. Irving details how the British remain within their works while Washington labors to form a new army. Knox arrives with the artillery from Ticonderoga — fifty cannon, mortars, and howitzers dragged on sledges across frozen lakes and snowy wastes. The arrival is celebrated by christening the huge brass mortar “Congress.” General Putnam’s men construct batteries on Lechmere Point under constant fire. Washington, still eager to attack, proposes an assault when the bay freezes, but his council of war deems it too dangerous.

CHAPTER XVIII — The Occupation of Dorchester Heights

The evening of March 4, 1776 is fixed for the occupation of Dorchester Heights. Irving dramatically narrates how a simultaneous cannonade distracts the enemy while General Thomas leads 1,200 men with 300 wagons of fascines and hay bundles. By dawn, two redoubts frown along the height. Howe exclaims, “The rebels have done more work in one night than my whole army would have done in a month.” A storm prevents Howe’s counterattack. Washington reminds his men it is the anniversary of the Boston Massacre and calls on them to revenge their brethren.

CHAPTER XIX — The British Evacuate Boston

The British fleet bearing the army from Boston disappears from the coast on March 17, 1776. Irving narrates Washington’s entry into the city in triumph. Howe’s retreat is precipitate — cannon spiked, stores destroyed, and the harbor filled with 78 ships carrying 11,000 troops and refugees. Washington writes that “no electric shock” could have struck the Tories with greater consternation. Congress votes a gold medal in Washington’s honor. The question of where the British will strike next looms — New York is the likely target.

CHAPTER XX — Arnold Before Quebec After the Assault

Irving recounts Arnold’s blockade of Quebec after the failed assault. Wounded and crippled, Arnold maintains the siege through the winter with a force that at one point numbers only 500 men. Smallpox ravages the American camp. General Thomas arrives to find the army in a forlorn condition. On May 6, British reinforcements arrive and Carleton sallies forth. The Americans retreat precipitately, abandoning baggage, artillery, and even their sick. The Canadian campaign ends in disaster.

CHAPTER XXI — The Canadian Reverse Reaches Congress

Irving describes how Washington sends General Gates to lay the dispatches before Congress. The news spreads alarm. Congress appoints a committee to confer with Washington, leading to the establishment of a Board of War and Ordnance. Washington’s conferences in Philadelphia result in resolutions for three-year enlistments, a flying camp of 10,000 militia, and the strengthening of the Hudson Highlands.

CHAPTER XXII — Disaster in Canada Continues

Dispatches from Canada remain disastrous. Arnold, commanding at Montreal, establishes a post at the Cedars which is captured by a British and Indian force under Captain Forster. The Americans are compelled to abandon Montreal. The retreat toward the Richelieu and Lake Champlain becomes a rout as Carleton advances. Washington urges reinforcements but has few men to spare.

CHAPTER XXIII — The Close of the Canadian Campaign

Irving narrates the disastrous close of the Canadian campaign. General Thomas dies of smallpox. The army, ravaged by disease and dispirited, evacuates Canada and falls back to Crown Point and Ticonderoga. Washington, though grieved, refuses to despair. He writes that “a manly and spirited opposition only can insure success.”

CHAPTER XXIV — The British Aim at New York and the Hudson

The British aim to seize New York and the Hudson River as the base of operations. Irving details how Washington moves the army from Boston to New York. He fortifies the city and surrounding heights. On June 29, the British fleet under Admiral Howe appears off Sandy Hook — 130 sail. The Declaration of Independence is proclaimed on July 9. Washington has it read to the army, telling his men that “the peace and safety of our country” now depend on their arms.

CHAPTER XXV — First Appearance of Alexander Hamilton

Irving introduces Alexander Hamilton, who first appears in the military ranks as a captain of artillery. His early life, character, and education are sketched. Greene recommends him to Washington’s notice. When Washington sees the skill and science of Hamilton’s fortifications, he invites him to his marquee — beginning the historic partnership.

CHAPTER XXVI — The Declaration of Independence and the British Arrive

The exultation over the Declaration is soon overclouded. Irving describes how British ships stand up the bay. The fleet under Admiral Howe arrives with reinforcements. British forces land on Staten Island, joined by Hessian troops — 4,300 Brunswick troops and nearly 13,000 Hessians hired by the British ministry. The total British force under Howe’s command numbers over 31,000 men. Washington has barely 10,000 fit for duty.

CHAPTER XXVII — Lord Howe’s Efforts at Reconciliation

Lord Howe arrives with powers to treat for peace. Irving recounts his proclamation offering pardon to all who return to their allegiance. When he sends a letter addressed to “George Washington, Esq.,” Washington refuses to receive it — he will not accept a private designation when his public station as commander-in-chief is at issue. Colonel Patterson’s interview with Washington on the subject is described: Washington insists on proper recognition of American rank. His conduct receives the applause of Congress. A conspiracy against Washington’s life — the Tryon plot — is uncovered. Mayor Matthews is arrested, and Hickey, one of Washington’s guards, is executed.

CHAPTER XXVIII — Security of the Hudson

Irving shows Washington anxious to prevent an enemy irruption into the Highlands. Fortifications are erected. A chain and chevaux-de-frise are stretched across the river. General George Clinton commands in the Highlands with prompt energy, calling out militia from their harvest fields. The Phoenix and Rose pass up the Hudson, throwing the river counties into alarm. Local militias muster along the shores.

CHAPTER XXIX — Intelligence from General Lee

Letters from Lee give Washington intelligence of Sir Henry Clinton’s failed expedition to the South. The attack on Sullivan’s Island is detailed: Colonel Moultrie’s heroic defense, the repulse of the British fleet under Sir Peter Parker, the burning of the frigate Actaeon, and the British withdrawal. Lee writes of the “cool courage” of the garrison. The victory raises American spirits at a dark hour.

CHAPTER XXX — General Putnam’s Project

Irving describes Putnam’s mechanical projects to obstruct the Hudson. He constructs chevaux-de-frise by sinking ships filled with stones. Fire-ships are prepared to attack the British fleet. Experiments with a submarine machine (Bushnell’s “American Turtle”) are attempted. Putnam’s vaunted mortar “the Congress” eventually bursts during bombardment.

CHAPTER XXXI — The Battle of Long Island

Irving narrates the Battle of Long Island (August 27, 1776). The British turn the American left flank through the unguarded Jamaica Pass. Stirling’s division makes a heroic stand against overwhelming forces, fighting until surrounded. Stirling surrenders but his sacrifice saves the army from total destruction. The routed Americans retreat to the fortifications at Brooklyn. Washington, who had galloped to the scene, witnesses the disaster from a hilltop and reportedly exclaims, “Good God! What brave fellows I must this day lose!”

CHAPTER XXXII — The Night After the Battle

The night after the battle is weary and sleepless. Irving describes Washington’s decision to retreat from Brooklyn across the East River. Colonel Glover’s Marblehead fishermen man the boats. A providential fog conceals the movement. Washington is among the last to embark. The retreat is one of the most signal achievements of the war, saving the army from destruction.

CHAPTER XXXIII — The British in Possession of Long Island

The enemy now holds Long Island. Irving shows Washington’s distress: “Our situation is truly distressing.” The militia desert in droves — whole regiments at a time. Washington writes that if he could have foreseen such difficulties, no consideration would have induced him to accept command. The British prepare to land on Manhattan while Washington debates whether to abandon New York.

CHAPTER XXXIV — The British Land on Manhattan

Irving details the British landing at Kip’s Bay on September 15. The American militia flee in confusion without firing a shot. Washington, galloping to the scene, is so enraged at the cowardice that he dashes his hat on the ground, exclaims “Are these the men with whom I am to defend America!” and snaps his pistols at the fugitives. An aide seizes his bridle and hurries him away. The army retreats to Harlem Heights.

CHAPTER XXXV — The Fortified Camp

The main body assembles on Harlem Heights. Irving recounts the skirmish of September 16, when the British advance is met by Knowlton’s rangers and Weedon’s Virginians. Knowlton is killed, but the Americans drive the enemy back — their first success in the campaign. Washington, who had been stung by the British bugles sounding “like a fox-chase,” is heartened. The army fortifies its position from Harlem to King’s Bridge.

CHAPTER XXXVI — Lee Delays His March

Irving describes the critical delay of General Lee in marching from the South. Washington sends urgent letters. The British threaten to surround the American army on Manhattan. Lee’s slowness is contrasted with his earlier celerity. Washington writes to the President of Congress that he cannot account for Lee’s tardiness.

CHAPTER XXXVII — Washington Decamps from Manhattan

Irving narrates Washington’s evacuation of Manhattan Island. The army marches to White Plains in four divisions. The British land at Throg’s Neck and Pell’s Point, attempting to cut off the retreat. Washington chooses a strong position at White Plains, fortifying Chatterton’s Hill.

CHAPTER XXXVIII — The Battle of White Plains

Howe advances from the shore. Irving describes the Battle of White Plains (October 28): the British carry Chatterton’s Hill after a fierce fight. Washington withdraws to a stronger position. Howe declines to attack and turns toward the Hudson. Washington crosses into New Jersey, leaving garrisons at Fort Washington and Fort Lee.

CHAPTER XXXIX — Intelligence from the Northern Army

Irving recounts Arnold’s naval engagement on Lake Champlain (October 11–13). Arnold fights heroically in the Congress galley, but the American fleet is destroyed. Nevertheless, the delay prevents Carleton from reaching Ticonderoga before winter. The campaign on the lakes saves the Northern department for the year.

CHAPTER XL — Washington Crosses the Hudson

On November 12, Washington crosses the Hudson with the residue of his troops. Irving narrates the dispute over Fort Washington: Greene, on the spot, believes it can be held; Washington’s order was discretionary. On November 16, the British storm the fort. The garrison of 2,000 men is captured. Magaw surrenders. Washington watches from the Jersey shore and is said to have wept at the sight of his men being bayoneted while begging quarter.

CHAPTER XLI — The Capture of Fort Washington

With Fort Washington lost, Fort Lee is untenable. Irving describes Cornwallis crossing the Hudson on November 20 with 6,000 men and climbing the Palisades. Greene is forced to abandon Fort Lee, leaving tents standing and kettles on the fire. The retreat across the Jerseys begins — a harrowing 100-mile march through a hostile or terrified countryside. Washington writes to Congress urging desperate preparations.

CHAPTER XLII — Lee’s Delay and the Retreat

Despite Washington’s repeated orders, Lee does not reach Peekskill until November 30. Irving recounts Lee’s laggard march and his meditations on attacking British posts independently. The army continues its retreat under Cornwallis’s close pursuit. Washington opens a letter from Lee to Reed and discovers Lee’s disparaging comments about “a certain great man” — a revelation that deeply wounds Washington’s trust in his closest aides.

CHAPTER XLIII — General Lee Captured

Irving narrates Lee’s capture at Baskingridge on December 13. Lingering at a tavern three miles from his camp, he is surprised by Colonel Harcourt’s dragoons, guided by a Tory. Lee is carried off in his slippers and blanket coat. Washington’s comment to his brother is the severest he permits himself: “by his own folly and imprudence.” The army is freed from Lee’s troublesome independence.

CHAPTER XLIV — The Hessian Prisoners at Trenton

The army is reduced to a few thousand men. Irving describes Washington’s daring plan to cross the Delaware on Christmas night and surprise the Hessian post at Trenton. The crossing is perilous — the river is choked with ice, the night is stormy. Colonel Rahl, the Hessian commander, is a brave but careless officer who scorns the idea of breastworks. On the morning of December 26, Washington’s forces converge on Trenton from two directions. Rahl is mortally wounded; nearly 1,000 Hessians surrender. Washington writes that “this is a glorious day for our country.”

CHAPTER XLV — Colonel Griffin’s Episode at Trenton

Irving recounts Colonel Griffin’s operations against British posts as a diversion for the Trenton attack. He succeeds in luring Count Donop toward Mount Holly, preventing him from reinforcing Rahl. After Trenton, the Americans drive the Hessians from Bordentown and Burlington. The fugitives spread panic, abandoning sick and stores.

CHAPTER XLVI — Washington’s Masterly Campaign

Irving concludes the volume with Washington’s masterful follow-up to Trenton. Cornwallis, recalled from embarking for England, hastens to Trenton with 8,000 men. On January 2, 1777, he attacks Washington at the Assunpink but is repulsed. Confident of bagging “the fox in the morning,” Cornwallis halts. Washington steals a march in the night, attacks the British rear at Princeton on January 3, and wins a sharp victory. General Mercer is mortally wounded. Washington establishes winter quarters at Morristown. The campaign closes with Washington hailed as “the American Fabius” — a leader who had snatched victory from the jaws of disaster through patience, daring, and strategic genius.
