Source

Adams, John Quincy. Diary of John Quincy Adams, Volume 2: March 1786 – December 1788. Edited by David Grayson Allen, Robert J. Taylor, Marc Friedlaender, and Celeste Walker. The Adams Papers, Series I: Diaries. Cambridge, Mass.: The Belknap Press of Harvard University Press, 1981.

Digital source: Harvard University Press / Adams Papers scholarly edition scan (Internet Archive / HathiTrust style page-image PDF). Filename on intake: The Adams Papers Digital Edition - Diary of John Quincy Adams, Volumes 1 and 2 (this extract is Volume 2 only).

This is the modern annotated scholarly text of JQA’s own manuscript diary for the Harvard and Newburyport years—not Charles Francis Adams’s 1870s Memoirs (selected, rephrased extracts) and not the Library of America selected Diaries (Waldstreicher, LOA #293–294). The Adams Papers volume prints the diary day by day with editorial footnotes, a Chronology of JQA’s life 1767–1788, a Descriptive List of Illustrations, and indexes.

Text

raw/adams-papers-diary-vol2.txt — ~27,000 lines, ~206,000 words. Page-image OCR of the 1981 Belknap Press volume. Continuous diary text is generally legible; some plate pages (Harvard Theses 1787, facsimile images) OCR as noise. Body of the diary runs from the March 1786 admission sequence through 31 December 1788; front matter (title, copyright, Introduction materials, Descriptive List of Illustrations) and back matter (Chronology, indexes) frame the diary proper.

Scope and character

Volume 2 opens with JQA completing the formalities of admission to Harvard College (16–17 March 1786) and closes with the last days of December 1788 at Newburyport, still reading law in Theophilus Parsons’s office. Between those poles the diary records:

  • Junior and senior college life at Cambridge (recitations, tutors, classmates, Phi Beta Kappa, forensic exercises)
  • Day-by-day observation of Shays’ Rebellion and the “perfect State of Anarchy” of late 1786–early 1787 as news reached Cambridge
  • Commencement Day, 18 July 1787
  • Removal to Newburyport and legal apprenticeship under Theophilus Parsons
  • Office reading (especially Blackstone), social life, balls, and local politics
  • Debate over the proposed Federal Constitution (Parsons federal; JQA’s own mixed, skeptical reflections on aristocracy and free government)
  • The Phi Beta Kappa oration of 5 September 1788 (full text entered in the diary)
  • Illness and a recuperative sojourn at Braintree in autumn 1788, then return to Newburyport

There are no numbered “chapters.” The volume is a continuous dated diary. The content summary below follows chronological blocks corresponding to the main phases of these thirty-four months.

Contents

Front matter

Title page and series identification for The Adams Papers / Diary of John Quincy Adams, Volume 2. Copyright and Belknap Press imprint (1981). Descriptive List of Illustrations (including JQA’s certificate of admission and related plates). Editorial apparatus introduces the diaries and the documentary standards of the edition.

March–April 1786 — Admission and first days at Harvard

17 March 1786. “This morning I finished the business of my admission. I carried the bond to Mr. Gannett the Steward. He then certified that I had complied with the Law. This certificate I went with to the President’s, who thereupon signed an Admittatur… The Steward told me then I was to all intents and Purposes, generally, and in all particulars a Member of the University.” JQA visits Boston, the House of Representatives and Supreme Court, then Braintree; packs furniture for college; begins the diary’s Harvard stretch with classmates, cousins, and Cambridge society. Footnotes identify the surviving admission certificate (Adams Papers) and related illustrations.

May–August 1786 — Junior year: tutors, recitations, college opinion

Daily recitations (Greek, Homer, mathematics/geography under young tutors), sharp private judgments on tutorial competence, and the social round of Cambridge and Boston. 1 May 1786 and following: critique of Tutor Jennison’s youth and mistakes (“I don’t know how it happened, but there have been eleven days knocked in the head”); absurd constructions of Homer; reflections that tutors should be chosen closer to graduation when college learning is still fresh. Class reputation, exhibition parts, and the contrast between college discipline and the wider Commonwealth run through the summer entries.

September–December 1786 — Shays’ Rebellion from Cambridge

7 September 1786. “The Commonwealth is in a State of considerable fermentation. Last week at Northampton, in the County of Hampshire, a body of armed men to the number of three or four hundred, prevented the Court of common Pleas from sitting, and bruised the high-sheriff dangerously, as it is reported. The same Court was likewise stopp’d the day before yesterday, at Worcester by 400 men… The Governor issued a Proclamation, calling upon the People at large to support the Constitution… Where this will end Time alone, can disclose. I fear, it will not before some blood is shed.”

JQA attributes the “grievances” (courts, Senate, salaries, taxes) less to real oppression than to idleness, dissipation, debt, and incapacity to pay. Disturbances “if properly managed may be productive of advantages to a Republican Government,” but if suffered to gain ground “must infallibly lead to a civil war.” 9 September: Concord courts threatened; “We are now in a perfect State of Anarchy. No laws observed, and no power to Punish delinquents.” College life continues in parallel—exhibition parts (Freeman English oration; Bridge Latin; Adams and Cranch a forensic on whether inequality among citizens is necessary to preserve the republic). Autumn and early winter entries track further news of insurgents, militia offers from Boston, and the mood of the colleges as the crisis deepens into December.

January–June 1787 — Senior year; residual unrest; study and society

Winter and spring of the senior year: recitations, friendships (Bridge, Cranch, Freeman, and others), visits to Boston and Braintree, and continued political comment as the Shays crisis is suppressed and public order is restored. JQA’s entries mix college routine with reading, sermons, and social calls. The approach of Commencement concentrates attention on debts of classmates, parts and performances, and the formal close of undergraduate life.

18 July 1787 — Commencement Day

Wednesday July 18th. Commencement Day. “At about 11 o’clock the procession began from the door of Harvard. The succeeding Classes went before us; and we preceded the President and fellows of the University…” The volume includes the printed Harvard Theses for 1787 (plate; OCR of the Latin broadside is noisy) listing Johannes Quincy Adams among the graduates. The diary surrounds the day with the practical anxieties of classmates who cannot pay their bills and with the ceremonial order of the university and Commonwealth officers.

August–early September 1787 — After college; toward Newburyport

Post-commencement emptiness at Cambridge: “All my classmates gone.” Visits, Phi Beta Kappa business (5 September meeting: officers elected; orations by Lowell and Freeman; dinner and admissions), court-watching in Boston (Shehane burglary trial), and preparation to enter the law. 1 September 1787: departure from Braintree with Mrs. Cranch toward Boston and Cambridge, then staging for Newburyport.

September–December 1787 — Parsons’s office; Federal Constitution

JQA settles at Newburyport as a student in the office of Theophilus Parsons. Office hours, Blackstone, local society, and balls fill the autumn. 12 October 1787: political chat with Parsons, who “favours very much the federal constitution, which has lately been proposed by the Convention of the States.” JQA’s own reflection: the Constitution will increase “the influence, power and wealth of those who have any already”; “there are to be no titles of nobility; but there will be great distinctions; and those distinctions will soon be hereditary… For my own part I am willing to take my chance under any government whatever, but it is hard to give up a System which I have always been taught to cherish, and to confess, that a free government is inconsistent with human nature.”

20 November 1787: Newburyport town meeting chooses King, Judge Greenleaf, Parsons, and Gen. Titcomb as delegates to the state ratifying convention—“all in favour of the constitution, and the town appears to be very unanimous for it.” 21 November: Parsons advises evening study of ethical writers, Quintilian, and Christian evidences so that a lawyer’s necessary defense of good and bad causes will not lead to “universal skepticism.” JQA resumes Gibbon; health is uneven; social life (Hoopers, Tracys, Townsend, Little, Putnam) continues beside the office.

January–August 1788 — Law study, Blackstone, Newburyport life

Winter and spring: continued office work; second full reading of Blackstone (fourth volume finished a second time by 3 May 1788); Foster’s Crown Law; walks, club evenings, sermons (Andrews, Carey’s illness, liberal vs. “bigotted” taste in preaching). 2 January 1788 and nearby entries: long social evenings of forced singing and “playing pawns” (redeeming pledges by kissing)—“‘Tis a profanation, of one of the most endearing demonstrations of Love”—set against happier college-style song at Little’s. Reflective set pieces (e.g. 4 January on “Nothing”) alternate with routine diary lines. Local courts, Haverhill and Salem visits, and the rhythms of a seaport legal apprenticeship dominate the summer.

3–5 September 1788 — Phi Beta Kappa anniversary; oration

3–4 September: shooting party; departure with Bridge for Cambridge. Friday 5 September 1788: anniversary assembly of ΦΒΚ “more numerous than I have known it… near forty members,” including two from Dartmouth; the governor and officers of the French squadron attend. JQA delivers the oration (full text entered in the diary): on youthful prospects, the deceptions of fancy and passion, industry and economy, Roman examples, and the dignity of useful stations (divine, merchant, and other callings) short of empire or discovery. Dinner at Warland’s “crowned the enjoyments of friendship.”

October–December 1788 — Braintree recuperation; return to Newburyport

Poor health forces a break from study. Early October: stage to Boston; home to Braintree with father and cousin; medicine, Voltaire, Gibbon, gunning, family company (Charles, Tom, Shaws, Cranches); General Knox dines (2 November). Line-a-day and fuller entries thin and thicken with recovery. Mid-November: return north (Wilmington, Haverhill) to Newburyport for Andrews’s ordination, dancing, office, Salem court, balls, and cold weather. 31 December 1788: evening with Foster at Mr. Jackson’s—“He was out.” The continuous diary of Volume 2 ends with the close of the year still in Parsons’s orbit.

Back matter

Chronology: John Quincy Adams, 1767–1788 — condensed life calendar from birth at Braintree (11 July 1767) through European years with JA, Dana, and the peace commission, return, Harvard, and the threshold of the legal career (overlaps and extends beyond the diary span of this volume). Indexes to the volume (persons, places, subjects) and residual editorial apparatus close the book.

Editorial notes (edition)

  • Day-by-day text follows JQA’s manuscripts (diary booklets and related line-a-day records), with original spelling and punctuation largely retained and elucidated in footnotes.
  • Footnotes identify classmates, ministers, lawyers, and public men; locate letters and printed sources (e.g. governor’s proclamation in the Massachusetts Centinel); and cross-reference Adams Papers documents (admission certificate, letters to TBA, etc.).
  • Plates include documentary facsimiles (admission; Harvard Theses 1787); OCR of plate pages is unreliable—rely on the set prose of the diary and Chronology for fact.

Related sources in this wiki

  • Continuations and parallel selections of JQA’s life-writing appear in other projects (Library of America Diaries; CFA Memoirs); this volume is the annotated full diary text for 1786–1788 only.
  • Contemporary constitutional debate in this wiki: The Federalist Papers; Irving’s Washington volumes for the Confederation and early federal years; Hamilton treasury reports for the financial settlement that followed ratification.
