On the Constitutionality of a National Bank

Submitted to President George Washington, February 23, 1791 Alexander Hamilton, Secretary of the Treasury

Context

In February 1791, Congress passed a bill chartering the First Bank of the United States for twenty years. President Washington, uncertain of the bill’s constitutionality, requested written opinions from his cabinet. Secretary of State Thomas Jefferson and Attorney General Edmund Randolph both submitted opinions arguing the bank was unconstitutional. Hamilton, as Secretary of the Treasury and the bill’s original proponent, submitted this opinion in defence of the bank’s constitutionality. Washington ultimately accepted Hamilton’s reasoning and signed the bill into law.

Hamilton opens by acknowledging his personal stake as the measure’s originator and his “firm persuasion, that principles of construction, like those espoused by the Secretary of State and Attorney-General, would be fatal to the just and indispensable authority of the United States.” The opinion proceeds as a single sustained legal argument, systematically dismantling Jefferson’s and Randolph’s objections.

Contents

I. The Nature of Sovereignty and the Doctrine of Implied Powers

Hamilton states his fundamental axiom: every power vested in a government is in its nature SOVEREIGN, and includes by force of the term a right to employ all means requisite and fairly applicable to the attainment of the ends of such power, provided those means are not precluded by constitutional restrictions, are not immoral, and are not contrary to the essential ends of political society. This principle, he argues, would be admitted as an axiom in its application to government in general; the burden falls on those who would deny it to prove a distinction showing that a rule essential to the preservation of social order is inapplicable to the United States.

The division of sovereignty does not alter the principle. The fact that powers of sovereignty are divided between national and state governments does not mean that each portion of power is not sovereign with regard to its proper objects. Each has sovereign power as to certain things, and not as to other things. To deny that the federal government has sovereign power as to its declared purposes because its power does not extend to all laws would be equally to deny that state governments have sovereign power in any case, because their power does not extend to every case. The Supremacy Clause (Article VI) is decisive: the Constitution, laws made in pursuance thereof, and treaties made under their authority are the supreme law of the land. “The power which can create the supreme law of the land, in any case, is doubtless sovereign as to such case.”

Incorporation is incident to sovereign power. This general and indisputable principle puts at once an end to the abstract question of whether the United States have power to erect a corporation: it is unquestionably incident to sovereign power to erect corporations, and consequently to that of the United States in relation to the objects entrusted to the management of the government. The difference is this: where the authority of the government is general, it can create corporations in all cases; where it is confined to certain branches of legislation, it can create corporations only in those cases. Hamilton notes with particular force that the fundamental principle he advances “has been untouched by either” Jefferson or Randolph.

The doctrine of implied and resulting powers. Hamilton concedes that the Constitution is founded on the principle that all powers not delegated are reserved. This is a mere consequence of the republican maxim that all government is a delegation of power. But how much is delegated in each case is a question of fact to be made out by fair reasoning and construction upon the particular provisions of the Constitution, taking as guides the general principles and general ends of government. He makes a crucial tripartite distinction: there are express powers, implied powers (as effectually delegated as the former), and resulting powers — a distinct class exemplified by the sovereign jurisdiction the United States would acquire over conquered territory, which would result from the whole mass of the government’s powers and from the nature of political society rather than from any enumerated power.

The only question in any case is whether the means to be employed has a natural relation to any of the acknowledged objects or lawful ends of the government. Thus Congress may not erect a corporation for superintending the police of Philadelphia, because they are not authorized to regulate that city’s police. But one may be erected in relation to the collection of taxes, trade with foreign countries, trade between the states, or trade with Indian tribes, because it is the province of the federal government to regulate those objects, and it is incident to a general sovereign or legislative power to regulate a thing to employ all the means which relate to its regulation to the best and greatest advantage.

Hamilton attacks what he calls “a strange fallacy” in the reasoning of his opponents: the treatment of incorporation as “some great independent substantive thing, as a political engine of peculiar magnitude and moment,” when in truth it is merely “a quality, capacity, or mean to an end.” He traces the power of incorporation to Roman law, under which a voluntary association of individuals at any time or for any purpose was capable of producing it, and notes that in English law it is part of the executive authority often delegated. The importance of the power has been exaggerated, leading to erroneous conclusions.

II. The General Denial of Corporate Power — Rebuttal of Jefferson and Randolph

Jefferson and Randolph argued that since the Constitution does not explicitly grant Congress the power to charter corporations, the bank is unconstitutional. Hamilton counters this with multiple independent arguments.

The express grant of exclusive legislation in certain places refutes the general denial. Congress has express power to exercise exclusive legislation in all cases whatsoever over the District (not exceeding ten miles square) and over all places purchased for forts, arsenals, dock-yards, and other needful buildings. Language does not afford a more complete designation of sovereign power than “exclusive legislation in all cases whatsoever.” This necessarily includes the power to erect corporations. “Surely, it can never be believed, that Congress, with exclusive power of legislation, in all cases whatsoever, cannot erect a corporation within the district which shall become the seat of government, for the better regulation of its police.” Yet Jefferson had declared the power to erect corporations “a right remaining exclusively with the states.”

The convention’s rejection of a corporate power clause is unreliable evidence. Jefferson argued that the Constitutional Convention rejected a proposition to empower Congress to make corporations. Hamilton responds: “What was the precise nature or extent of this proposition, or what the reasons for refusing it, is not ascertained by any authentic document, or even by accurate recollection.” As far as any document exists, it specifies only canals. Some affirm it was confined to canals; others that it embraced banks; others that it extended to incorporating generally. Some say it was rejected as improper, others as unnecessary and inexpedient to furnish an additional topic of objection. “In this state of the matter, no inference whatever can be drawn from it.” Moreover, even if the proposition had been exactly as Jefferson claimed, the intention of the framers is to be sought in the instrument itself according to the usual rules of construction — extrinsic circumstances regarding the convention’s intention must be rejected if a power is deducible by fair inference from the Constitution’s actual provisions.

The argument that incorporation is too important to be implied is circular. The claim that if the Constitution intended to confer so important a power it would have been expressly mentioned rests on “an exaggerated and erroneous conception of the nature of the power.” Hamilton reiterates that it is a mean, not an end, and ought to have been left to implication rather than expressly granted.

III. The Doctrine of Necessary and Proper Means — Liberal Construction

The meaning of “necessary.” Jefferson had argued that no means are necessary but those without which the grant of the power would be nugatory — and further, that the necessity which warrants constitutional exercise of the power depends on “casual and temporary circumstances.” Hamilton demolishes this: “an idea which alone refutes the construction.” The expediency of exercising a particular power at a particular time may depend on circumstances, but the constitutional right of exercising it must be uniform and invariable, the same today as tomorrow.

“According to both [the grammatical and popular sense], necessary often means no more than needful, requisite, incidental, useful, or conducive to.” It is common to say “it is necessary for a government or a person to do this or that thing” when nothing more is intended than that the interest of the government or person requires or will be promoted by it. The whole turn of the necessary and proper clause indicates “that it was the intent of the convention, by that clause, to give a liberal latitude to the exercise of the specified powers.” To understand the word as Jefferson does would be to depart from its obvious and popular sense and give it a restrictive operation “never before entertained” — as if “absolutely or indispensably” had been prefixed.

The consequences of Jefferson’s construction. Such a construction would beget endless uncertainty and embarrassment. There are few measures of any government which would stand so severe a test. To insist upon it would make the criterion of any implied power “a case of extreme necessity, which is rather a rule to justify the overleaping of the bounds of constitutional authority, than to govern the ordinary exercise of it.” By the same reasoning, one could prove that no state government has a right to incorporate a bank, since the state’s public business could be performed without one — and indeed that there was no power to incorporate a town for police purposes, since an incorporation may be dispensed with even if it is better to have one.

The correct test: relation to end, not degree of necessity. “The degree in which a measure is necessary, can never be a test of the legal right to adopt it. That must be a matter of opinion, and can only be a test of expediency. The relation between the measure and the end; between the nature of the mean employed towards the execution of a power, and the object of that power; must be the criterion of constitutionality; not the more or less of necessity or utility.”

The lighthouse precedent. The act concerning light-houses, beacons, buoys, and public piers — passed under the power to regulate trade — is a decisive example of the government’s own practice contradicting Jefferson’s restrictive rule. It cannot be affirmed that the exercise of that power was strictly necessary, or that the power to regulate trade would be nugatory without that of regulating such establishments.

The rule of liberal construction. Powers contained in a constitution of government, especially those concerning the general administration of the affairs of a country — its finances, trade, defence — ought to be construed liberally in advancement of the public good. This rule does not depend on the particular form of government or the demarcation of its boundaries, “but on the nature and objects of government itself.” The means by which national exigencies are to be provided for, national inconveniences obviated, and national prosperity promoted are of such infinite variety and complexity that there must be great latitude of discretion.

Randolph’s distinction refuted. The Attorney-General admitted the rule but argued it should be stricter for the federal constitution because there is more danger of error in defining partial than general powers. Hamilton responds: the reason for the rule — the variety and extent of public exigencies, a far greater proportion of which are objects of national administration — forbids such a distinction. Greater danger of error may be a prudential reason for caution in practice, but it cannot be a rule of restrictive interpretation. Randolph himself defined “necessary” as “incidental” and “the natural means of executing a power,” yet failed to apply this definition consistently.

The necessary and proper clause gives explicit sanction to implied powers. While the clause gives no new or independent power, it provides “an explicit sanction to the doctrine of implied powers” and is equivalent to an admission that the government as to its specified powers and objects has plenary and sovereign authority — in some cases paramount to the states, in others coordinate with it.

The doctrine is not unlimited. Hamilton clarifies that his doctrine does not affirm that the national government is sovereign in all respects, but only to the extent of the objects of its specified powers. The criterion of constitutionality is: (1) is the end clearly comprehended within any specified power? (2) does the measure have an obvious relation to that end? (3) is it forbidden by any particular provision? An additional useful criterion: does the proposed measure abridge a preexisting right of any state or individual? If not, there is a strong presumption in favour of constitutionality.

IV. Application to the Bank — Specific Powers

Hamilton applies his doctrine by tracing “a natural and obvious relation between the institution of a bank, and the objects of several of the enumerated powers of the government.”

A. The power to collect taxes. A bank relates to tax collection in two ways: indirectly, by increasing the quantity of circulating medium and quickening circulation, which facilitates the means of paying; directly, by creating a convenient species of medium in which taxes are to be paid. To designate or appoint the money or thing in which taxes are to be paid is a proper and necessary exercise of the power of collection. Congress could have required payment in kind, in state paper money, in bills of existing banks, or in bills issued under federal authority. The appointment of the medium is an incident to the power of collection.

Hamilton constructs an elegant hypothetical: the government could itself issue bills payable on demand, set apart a fund for answering them, and appoint officers to direct the fund — all of which would be constitutional and would amount to the institution of a bank. “The simplest and most precise idea of a bank, is, a deposit of coin or other property, as a fund for circulating a credit upon it, which is to answer the purpose of money.” If the fund were vested in the officers and their successors as private property incapable of being resumed without violating the sanction protecting property rights, this would amount to the erection of a corporation — for the true definition of a corporation is “a legal person, or a person created by act of law; consisting of one or more natural persons, authorized to hold property or a franchise in succession, in a legal, as contradistinguished from a natural, capacity.”

B. The power to borrow money. A bank has a direct relation to borrowing because it is “an usual, and in sudden emergencies, an essential instrument, in the obtaining of loans to government.” A nation threatened with war needs large sums quickly; taxes require time to collect; anticipation is indispensable. With a bank the supply can at once be had; without one, loans from individuals must be sought — often too slow, sometimes not practicable at all.

Hamilton uses a concrete example from the very moment of writing: “An Indian expedition is to be prosecuted. The only fund out of which the money can arise consistently with the public engagements, is a tax, which only begins to be collected in July next. The preparations, however, are instantly to be made. The money must therefore be borrowed; and of whom could it be borrowed, if there were no public banks?” He posits that if no banks existed, it would be necessary to create one, and if individuals offered to lend on condition that they be incorporated as a bank, “Can it be believed that a compliance with this proposition would be unconstitutional?”

C. The power to regulate trade between the states. A bank is conducive to creating a convenient medium of exchange between states and maintaining full circulation by preventing the frequent displacement of metals in reciprocal remittances. Money is “the very hinge on which commerce turns” — and this includes paper as well as gold and silver. Hamilton rejects Randolph’s contention that the medium of circulation is confined to coin: it is even supposable that the whole or greatest part of the coin might be carried out of the country.

D. The power of common defence and the property clause. The aid of such an institution is essential to measures for protecting the frontiers, as demonstrated by the Indian expedition. Moreover, the bank is within the clause authorizing Congress to make all needful rules and regulations concerning the property of the United States. The government becomes a joint proprietor of the bank’s stock. Hamilton argues by analogy: it is admitted that under this clause Congress can erect a government (a corporation of the highest nature) over the Western Territory. “By what rule of construction can it be maintained, that the same words, in a constitution of government, will not have the same effect when applied to one species of property as to another, as far as the subject is capable of it?”

V. Response to Jefferson’s and Randolph’s Specific Objections

Against the laws of alienage, descent, escheat, forfeiture, distribution, and monopoly. Jefferson argued that the proposed incorporation creates capacities and attributes against these foundation laws of the states and “prostrates” state jurisprudence. Hamilton responds: if these are truly the foundation laws of the several states, then most of them have subverted their own foundations, since scarcely one has not made material alterations in its law of descents. Moreover, the erection of a corporation does not violate these laws — it creates a kind of person to which they are inapplicable. An artificial person can have no country (alienage inapplicable), no heirs (descent inapplicable), cannot commit a crime (forfeiture inapplicable), and cannot die (distribution inapplicable). “To do a thing which is against the law, is to do something which it forbids, and which is a violation of it.”

Even if incorporation did alter state laws, Hamilton argues this proves nothing against constitutionality: almost every new law alters an old one in some way. Congress’s power to establish uniform bankruptcy laws or regulate foreign coin necessarily alters state laws. The power to prohibit exportation alters the common law right of every citizen to export property. “It can, therefore, never be good reasoning, to say, this or that act is unconstitutional, because it alters this or that law of a state.”

Monopoly rebuttal. Jefferson characterized the bank as a monopoly. Hamilton responds: the bill neither prohibits any state from erecting as many banks as it pleases, nor any number of individuals from associating to carry on the business. “Monopoly implies a legal impediment to the carrying on the trade by others than those to whom it is granted.” No such impediment exists here.

Paramount laws rebuttal. Jefferson charged that the bank’s by-laws would be paramount to state laws. Hamilton: the by-laws of a bank can operate only upon its own members and concern only the disposition of its own property, essentially resembling the rules of a private mercantile partnership. They are expressly not to be contrary to law — meaning state law as well as federal law. If a corporate by-law is contrary to state law, it must be overruled as void unless the state law is contrary to federal law, in which case the question is between state and federal law, not between state and corporate law.

Randolph’s enumeration critique. Randolph attempted to enumerate what powers are included under each head of federal authority and concluded that incorporation was not among them. Hamilton demolishes this approach by showing the enumerations are radically incomplete. Under the power to regulate commerce, Randolph omitted: the power to prohibit exportation; to prescribe rules for American shipping (how vessels shall be navigated, by citizens or foreigners); to regulate the manner of contracting with seamen and ship policies; to grant bounties to certain vessels and merchandise; to prescribe inspection rules for exports; to regulate pilots; to regulate bills of exchange between states. Under the borrowing power, Randolph omitted pledging or mortgaging a fund, collateral conditions, and lotteries as borrowing expedients. The enumerations are “so imperfect as to authorize no conclusion whatever.”

“The practice of mankind.” Hamilton invokes a powerful argument from usage: all principal commercial nations have made use of trading corporations or companies for external commerce, proving that their establishment is incident to the regulation of commerce. Banks are “an usual engine in the administration of national finances, and an ordinary, and the most effectual, instrument of loans.” It pleads strongly against the supposition that a government clothed with most of the important prerogatives of sovereignty — in relation to its revenues, debt, credit, defence, trade, and intercourse with foreign nations — is forbidden to make use of that instrument. “To all questions of this nature, the practice of mankind ought to have great weight against the theories of individuals.”

The aggregate view of the Constitution. The combined powers of laying and collecting taxes, borrowing money indefinitely, coining money and regulating foreign coins, and making needful rules concerning property — together with the reason and nature of the thing — speak strongly that “it is the manifest design and scope of the constitution, to vest in Congress all the powers requisite to the effectual administration of the finances of the United States.” To suppose the government precluded from so usual and important an instrument as a bank is to suppose what does not coincide with the general tenor and complexion of the Constitution. “Little less than a prohibiting clause can destroy the strong presumptions which result from the general aspect of the government. Nothing but demonstration should exclude the idea that the power exists.”

VI. Summary of Hamilton’s Position

Hamilton concludes by restating the propositions he has demonstrated:

  1. The power of the government as to the objects entrusted to its management is in its nature sovereign.
  2. The right of erecting corporations is one inherent in, and inseparable from, the idea of sovereign power.
  3. The position that the government can exercise no power but such as is delegated does not militate against this principle.
  4. The word “necessary” in the general clause can have no restrictive operation derogating from this principle — the degree to which a measure is necessary is a test of expediency only, not of constitutional right.
  5. The power to erect corporations is not an independent and substantive power, but an incidental and auxiliary one, more properly left to implication than expressly granted.
  6. The principle does not extend the power of government beyond prescribed limits, because it only affirms a power to incorporate for purposes within the sphere of the specified powers.
  7. The right to exercise such a power in certain cases is unequivocally granted in the most positive and comprehensive terms (as in the exclusive legislation clause).
  8. Such a power has actually been exercised in two eminent instances: the erection of governments northwest and southwest of the Ohio River — the last independent of any antecedent compact.

Hence results “a full and complete demonstration, that the Secretary of State and Attorney-General are mistaken, when they deny generally the power of the national government to erect corporations.”

VII. Conclusion

Having shown the bank’s natural relation to the power of collecting taxes, regulating trade, providing for the common defence, and the property clause, Hamilton rests his case. He notes that the proposed measure stands even the most severe test on the question of whether it abridges any preexisting right of any state or individual — each state may still erect as many banks as it pleases, and every individual may still carry on banking to any extent. He corrects Jefferson’s misstatement that the bill contemplates a repayment rather than a loan to the government, explaining that the government remains a proprietor of the stock and will share in profit or loss — hence the sum is manifestly and in the strictest sense a loan.
