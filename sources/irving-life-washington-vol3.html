[source] The Life of George Washington — Volume III (Irving, 1899 Desmond)

The Life of George Washington — Volume III

Author: Washington Irving
Publisher: Desmond Publishing Co., Boston, 1899
Source: Internet Archive (University of Florida library copy)
OCLC: 7523322

Overview

Volume III covers the pivotal middle years of the American Revolutionary War, from the autumn of 1777 through the summer of 1781. It opens with Sir Henry Clinton’s attempt to relieve Burgoyne, follows the war across multiple theatres — the Hudson Highlands, Pennsylvania, the southern states, and the Virginia coast — and closes with the combined French and American armies marching towards Yorktown. The volume covers the Saratoga campaign, the Battle of Germantown, the winter at Valley Forge, the Conway Cabal, the Battle of Monmouth, the treason of Benedict Arnold, Greene’s southern campaign, and the final concentration of forces that would defeat Cornwallis.

Contents

Chapter I — Sir Henry Clinton’s Hudson Expedition (October 1777)

The volume opens with Sir Henry Clinton’s expedition up the Hudson, launched in October 1777 to relieve Burgoyne. After awaiting reinforcements from Europe — ships that took three months to cross the Atlantic in Dutch bottoms — Clinton assembles between three and four thousand men on board ships-of-war, armed galleys, and flat-bottomed boats at New York. A southern destination is publicly given out, but shrewd observers surmise the real objective. The narrative surveys the weak state of the Hudson Highland defences: Putnam commands only 1,100 Continentals and 400 militia at Peekskill. The Highland forts — Clinton, Montgomery, and Constitution — are poorly garrisoned. Governor George Clinton, the champion of the Highlands, is absent attending the State Legislature at Kingston (Esopus). Putnam sends urgent intelligence to the governor on September 29, warning that the enemy has called in guides from Croton River, ordered hard bread, and drawn the ships up the river. The governor hastens to his post with whatever militia he can collect. The chapter describes the chain, boom, and chevaux-de-frise across the Hudson between Fort Montgomery and Anthony’s Nose — obstructions that had repeatedly given way under the pressure of the tide. Fort Clinton had been recently erected within rifle shot of Fort Montgomery, separated by a deep ravine and stream called Peploep’s Kill. The combined garrison of both forts numbers fewer than 600 men, mostly militia.

The narrative follows Clinton’s plan: a feint at Peekskill deceives Putnam while the main British force marches secretly through the mountains. Forts Montgomery and Clinton are overpowered after a gallant defence. The commanders narrowly escape. The American frigates in the river are set ablaze and explode in a spectacular conflagration. Putnam and Governor Clinton make rallying efforts. A spy is detected through a silver bullet containing a secret message. Esopus (Kingston) is burnt. The British continue their ravaging progress up the Hudson, spreading destruction through the countryside.

Chapter II — Battle of Saratoga — Surrender of Burgoyne (October 1777)

Scarcity grips the British camp. Gates bides his time, refusing to be drawn into a premature engagement. Burgoyne undertakes a foraging movement that leads to the second battle of Saratoga on October 7, 1777. The British and Hessian forces are routed in a fierce engagement. The narrative describes the harrowing experience of the Baroness Riedesel and Lady Harriet Ackland during the battle — the Baroness, with her three small children, taking refuge in the cellar of a house near the battlefield while cannonballs crash overhead. General Simon Fraser is mortally wounded by a marksman from Morgan’s rifle corps; his funeral is conducted under a hail of enemy fire. The British begin a desperate night retreat. In one of the most celebrated episodes of the campaign, Lady Harriet Ackland — wife of the wounded Major Ackland, a British prisoner — makes her way through enemy lines in a small boat on the Hudson to plead for permission to nurse her husband. Washington Irving dwells on her courage and the chivalrous treatment she receives from General Gates. Burgoyne’s situation becomes hopeless at Saratoga. The capitulation is signed on October 17, 1777, and the Convention Army marches out with the honours of war. The conduct of the American troops is marked by discipline and restraint. In a famous scene, General Schuyler — who had been superseded by Gates — shows magnanimous courtesy toward the Baroness Riedesel and generous conduct toward Burgoyne himself, refusing to gloat over his rival’s triumph. The defeated British army is marched down the Hudson. The victory at Saratoga transforms the strategic situation and seals the fate of the British campaign to split the colonies.

Chapter III — Battle of Germantown (October 4, 1777)

Washington advances his army to Skippack Creek, keeping a watchful eye on the British occupation of Philadelphia. The British fleet is active in the Delaware River, where forts and obstructions have been placed to deny the Royal Navy access to the city. Washington meditates a bold stroke against the main British encampment at Germantown. The chapter narrates the Battle of Germantown (October 4, 1777) in full detail: the complex American plan calling for four separate columns converging on the British camp in the early morning fog. The initial advance drives Howe’s pickets back, but confusion in the fog, the failure of General Stephen’s and General Greene’s columns to link up properly, and the stubborn defence of the Chew House (where Colonel Musgrave and a detachment of the 40th Foot barricade themselves and hold out against repeated assaults) cause the American attack to unravel. A friendly fire incident — soldiers mistaking each other for the enemy in the fog — triggers a panic and retreat. Despite the defeat, Washington’s audacity impresses European observers: it is the first time the Americans have taken the offensive against a major British force in a pitched battle.

Chapter IV — Defence of the Delaware Forts (October–November 1777)

Washington establishes his army at White Marsh, north of Philadelphia. He undertakes measures to cut off the enemy’s supplies from the countryside. The narrative focuses on the forts on the Delaware River — Fort Mifflin (on Mud Island) and Fort Mercer (at Red Bank, New Jersey) — which block British supply lines up the river to Philadelphia. Colonel Christopher Greene of Rhode Island commands Fort Mercer. Count Donop, a Hessian colonel of European reputation, leads an assault on Fort Mercer on October 22, 1777. The attack is repulsed with heavy losses. Donop himself is mortally wounded; he dies several days later after receiving compassionate care from the Americans. The chapter details the siege operations, the shelling of Fort Mifflin from British batteries on Province Island, the heroic but doomed defence by the American garrison, and the eventual abandonment of both forts as untenable. The fall of the Delaware forts clears the river for British shipping, solidifying Howe’s hold on Philadelphia for the winter.

Chapter V — The Conway Cabal Begins (Winter 1777–78)

The chapter introduces the intrigues that would become known as the Conway Cabal. General De Kalb is commissioned a major-general in the Continental Army. Thomas Conway, an Irish-French officer who has risen rapidly through lobbying and political connections, makes pretensions to high command that are thwarted by Washington — Conway is made Inspector-General rather than receiving a major-general’s command. This snub fuels resentment. The Conway Cabal takes shape: a faction in Congress and the army — including Conway, General Horatio Gates (the hero of Saratoga), and Thomas Mifflin (Quartermaster-General) — begins manoeuvring to replace Washington as commander-in-chief. Gates is remiss in his correspondence with Washington after Saratoga, failing to send the customary report to his commanding general. He is dilatory in forwarding troops from the victorious northern army to the main army in Pennsylvania. Washington dispatches his young aide-de-camp Alexander Hamilton on a mission to Gates’s headquarters to press for reinforcements. Hamilton finds Gates evasive. James Wilkinson, Gates’s adjutant, is the bearer of dispatches to Congress from the north; he is a tardy traveller whose leisurely journey allows news of the cabal’s machinations to spread. Conway’s correspondence disparaging Washington is detected — a letter falls into Washington’s hands containing Conway’s boast that “no heaven, no earth” can save Washington from being supplanted. Washington’s response is measured but firm; he writes an apology for his army’s condition, declining to dignify the attacks with public notice.

Chapter VI — Fort Mifflin and the Delaware Campaign (November 1777)

Further hostilities on the Delaware continue. Fort Mifflin is attacked repeatedly by British batteries on Province Island and by ships of the Royal Navy. The garrison defends bravely but is gradually reduced under relentless bombardment. The mission of Alexander Hamilton to Gates is described in greater detail; Hamilton also visits the camps of Governor Clinton and Putnam on the Hudson. Putnam is depicted as “on his hobby-horse” — energetic but unreliable in his commands. The narrative details the great difficulties Washington faces in procuring reinforcements from the northern army, as Gates proves uncooperative. The intrigues of the cabal deepen: letters from James Lovell (a Congressional delegate from Massachusetts and ally of the cabal) and Thomas Mifflin to Gates reveal the extent of the conspiracy. The American works at Red Bank (Fort Mercer) are destroyed to prevent their use by the enemy. The British finally gain complete possession of the Delaware River. Washington’s army, unable to challenge Howe in Philadelphia, must contemplate a winter encampment.

Chapter VII — March to Valley Forge (December 1777)

Washington considers the question of an attack on Philadelphia. General Joseph Reed visits headquarters for consultations. The enemy’s works around Philadelphia are carefully reconnoitred. A council of war produces divided opinions — some favour an attack, others caution against it. Lafayette distinguishes himself in a daring reconnaissance exploit and is rewarded with command of a division. Congress modifies the Board of War, placing Gates at its head — a victory for the cabal. Another letter from Lovell to Gates reveals the continuing machinations. Sir William Howe sallies forth from Philadelphia with his army for manoeuvres and skirmishes. Conway is formally appointed Inspector-General. A council of war debates the question of winter quarters. The army begins its dreary march to Valley Forge on December 19, 1777 — a twelve-mile march through mud and freezing cold, many soldiers barefoot and leaving bloody footprints in the snow. At Valley Forge, the troops begin hutting, constructing log cabins in the wooded hills. Washington writes vindicatory letters defending his conduct of the campaign to Congress and to his critics. The chapter closes with a retrospective of the year 1777 — a year of defeats (Brandywine, Germantown, the loss of Philadelphia) offset by the great victory at Saratoga.

Chapter VIII — The Gates-Washington Correspondence (Winter 1777–78)

Gates is in the ascendant, basking in the glory of Saratoga and presiding over the Board of War. The “Conway letter” — in which Conway wrote disparagingly of Washington — becomes the focus of suspicion and acrimony. A consequential correspondence develops between Gates and Washington. Gates attempts to explain away the correspondence, but his explanations are evasive and unsatisfactory. Dr. James Craik, Washington’s long-time friend and physician, sends a warning letter alerting Washington to the plot against him. Anonymous letters circulate, attacking Washington’s character and leadership. A projected expedition to Canada — backed by the Board of War — is launched, with Lafayette given command but with Gates and the Board attempting to control it from behind the scenes. Lafayette finds himself caught between conflicting instructions from Washington and the Board of War; he seeks Washington’s counsel, who advises him wisely.

Chapter IX — Collapse of the Cabal (Spring 1778)

Gates undertakes to explain his role in the Conway correspondence. Washington subjects the explanation to a searching analysis and finds it wanting. The correspondence between the two generals is brought to a close, but the affair has damaged Gates’s standing. Spurious letters — forged or distorted — are published in an attempt to smear Washington. The Canada expedition — the pet project of the Board of War — proves a fiasco. Lafayette, who had been sent north to lead the expedition, encounters nothing but perplexities: inadequate supplies, unreliable troops, and hostile conditions. He is forced to abandon the enterprise. Throughout the crisis, Washington offers Lafayette sage counsel and support. The cabal’s influence wanes as its machinations are exposed and its members turn on each other.

Chapter X — Wilkinson and the Aftermath (Spring 1778)

More trouble erupts over the Conway letter. A correspondence between Lord Stirling and James Wilkinson reveals Wilkinson’s role in spreading gossip and betraying confidences. Wilkinson’s honour is wounded by accusations of duplicity. He has a passage at arms with General Gates, who accuses him of leaking the Conway letter. His seat on the Board of War becomes uncomfortable. Wilkinson determines that Lord Stirling “shall bleed” — a reference to a contemplated duel. His wounded honour is eventually healed through negotiations. Wilkinson seeks an interview with Washington and, in a dramatic turn, shows Washington the full correspondence between Gates and the conspirators. He then denounces Gates and resigns the secretaryship of the Board of War. Cast out of employment, Wilkinson fades from the scene for a time. The chapter closes with Irving’s concluding remarks on the Conway Cabal — a conspiracy that failed because Washington’s integrity, patience, and the trust of his officers and men proved unshakeable.

Chapter XI — Reforms at Valley Forge — Steuben’s Arrival (Winter–Spring 1778)

A committee of arrangement arrives at Valley Forge to work with Washington on army reforms. The camp suffers from severe scarcity of provisions — the army is nearly starved. The enemy, by contrast, revels in Philadelphia, enjoying balls, theatricals, and the famous Mischianza. An attempt is made to surprise Light-Horse Harry Lee; he makes a gallant defence, is praised by Washington, and is promoted. A letter from General Charles Lee (recently exchanged) is received. Burgoyne returns to England in disgrace. Martha Washington arrives at Valley Forge to join her husband for the winter, boosting morale. Bryan Fairfax — a friend of Washington’s from pre-war Virginia — visits the camp. Then comes the pivotal arrival of Baron Friedrich Wilhelm von Steuben, a Prussian officer who had served under Frederick the Great. His character and methods are described: his bluff manner, his theatricality, his skill as a drillmaster. Steuben takes charge of training the army, drilling the ragged Continentals in the manual of arms, bayonet drill, and evolutions. The discipline of the army is transformed. Greene is made quartermaster-general, reluctantly accepting the thankless but crucial administrative post. The army that emerges from Valley Forge in the spring is a professionally trained force — a direct result of Steuben’s labours.

Chapter XII — Winter at Valley Forge — North’s Conciliatory Bills (Winter–Spring 1778)

The fortifications of the Hudson are reviewed. A project to surprise Sir Henry Clinton is contemplated but not executed. General Howe forages in the Jerseys; ships and stores are burnt at Bordentown in a British raid. Plans for the next campaign are debated at headquarters. Gates and Mifflin are placed under Washington’s direct command — a sign that Washington has decisively overcome the cabal. Conway, the catalyst of the conspiracy, falls from power and eventually leaves the service. Lord North’s Conciliatory Bills — a last-ditch British offer to negotiate, proposing to renounce the right to tax the colonies and appoint commissioners to treat — arrive in America, sent to Washington by Governor Tryon. Washington forwards them to Congress, which resolves to reject any terms that do not acknowledge American independence. Washington sends a firm letter to Tryon. There is rejoicing at Valley Forge when word arrives of the French Alliance. The British in Philadelphia celebrate their own grand fête — the Mischianza, an elaborate tournament and pageant organized by Major John André for the departing General Howe.

Chapter XIII — Lafayette at Barren Hill — British Evacuation of Philadelphia (May–June 1778)

Lafayette is detached from the main army with a small force to keep watch on Philadelphia. He takes a position at Barren Hill, about twelve miles from the city. Sir Henry Clinton — now in command after Howe’s recall — devises a plan to entrap the young Marquis, sending a superior force to cut off his retreat. Washington becomes alarmed for Lafayette’s safety. Lafayette, displaying coolness and tactical skill, extricates his force by a clever stratagem — marching his troops as if to offer battle while secretly withdrawing by a different route, using the cover of woods and ravines. The exchange of General Charles Lee (captured earlier in the war) and Colonel Ethan Allen (captured in 1775 after the failed attack on Montreal) is negotiated. Ethan Allen visits Valley Forge and has an audience with Washington, who forms a measured opinion of the fiery Vermont leader. Preparations begin in Philadelphia for the British evacuation. Washington takes corresponding measures. The Carlisle Peace Commission — the British commissioners sent to treat under Lord North’s Conciliatory Bills — arrives in Philadelphia. They are disappointed to find Congress intransigent on independence. Their proceedings fail utterly, and their manifesto threatening dire consequences is contemptuously received.

Chapter XIV — Battle of Monmouth (June 28, 1778)

Preparations accelerate for the British evacuation of Philadelphia. Washington calls a council of war. General Charles Lee argues vehemently against attacking the British during their march across New Jersey — he believes the American army is not ready for a general engagement. Philadelphia is evacuated on June 18, 1778. Sir Henry Clinton marches his army — a column twelve miles long — across New Jersey toward New York. Washington pursues, calling another council of war. There is a sharp conflict of opinions: some officers favour a partial attack on the British rear; others, following Lee, oppose any attack. Lee’s conduct regarding the command of the advance force is contradictory and evasive — first he refuses the command, then demands it, then seems reluctant to exercise it. The Battle of Monmouth Court House begins on June 28, 1778, in intense heat. Lee’s handling of the attack is hesitant and confused; he orders a retreat at a critical moment, throwing the advance corps into disorder. Washington arrives on the field to find the American troops falling back. In the most famous scene of the battle, Washington rides up to Lee and rebukes him in terms that leave Lee shaken. Washington then personally rallies the troops, reforms the line, and directs the defence, stabilising the situation. The battle ends with the two armies facing each other on the field; Clinton slips away during the night. Both sides claim victory. The subsequent march of the armies continues.

Chapter XV — Lee’s Court-Martial (July–August 1778)

A bitter correspondence between Lee and Washington follows the Battle of Monmouth. Lee writes insulting letters to Washington, demanding satisfaction. Washington responds with icy formality. Lee asks for a trial by court-martial, confident he can vindicate himself. The court-martial is convened, and the evidence is heard. The verdict: Lee is found guilty of disobedience of orders, misbehaviour before the enemy, and disrespect to the commander-in-chief. He is suspended from command for one year. The chapter concludes with Lee’s subsequent history — his vituperative attacks on Washington after his suspension, his writing of pamphlets that damage his own reputation further, and his eventual death in obscurity. The end of Lee’s military career marks the end of a major source of friction within the American high command.

Chapter XVI — Arrival of D’Estaing — Rhode Island Expedition (July–August 1778)

The arrival of the French fleet under the Count d’Estaing — a dozen ships of the line and several frigates — opens a new phase of the war. Washington and D’Estaing exchange cordial correspondence. D’Estaing’s plans are ambitious: a combined Franco-American expedition against Newport, Rhode Island, where a British garrison under General Pigot holds the town. Perturbation seizes New York at the news. The French fleet sails for Narragansett Bay. Excitement grips the French crews as they anticipate action. The expedition against Rhode Island begins with General Sullivan commanding the American land forces. Operations proceed by sea and land — the French fleet drives the British ships ashore, and American troops land on the island. But then a violent storm disperses the French fleet, damaging several ships. D’Estaing withdraws to Boston for repairs, infuriating the American troops. The expedition fails. Irritation grows between the allied forces, with accusations of bad faith on both sides. Washington writes a considerate letter to the Count d’Estaing, smoothing over the tensions and preserving the alliance. The French fleet refits at Boston before departing for the West Indies.

Chapter XVII — Indian Warfare — Wyoming — Savannah Captured (Summer–Autumn 1778)

The narrative turns to savage Indian warfare on the frontier. The Valley of Wyoming (in present-day Pennsylvania) is devastated in a brutal massacre — settlers are slaughtered, homes burned, and the valley left desolate. The British and their Indian allies ravage the frontier settlements. In New York, the British undertake counter-movements; Washington responds with his own manoeuvres. British foraging parties range through the countryside. Colonel Baylor’s dragoons are massacred in a surprise night attack at Old Tappan, New Jersey, on September 28, 1778 — the British refusing quarter and bayoneting sleeping men. A British expedition against Little Egg Harbor on the New Jersey coast results in the massacre of Pulaski’s infantry. The Americans retaliate by executing Donop’s Rangers. Admiral Byron arrives from England to command the British fleet and endeavours to trap D’Estaing, but is disappointed as the French admiral eludes him. A British expedition against St. Lucia in the West Indies is launched. A major expedition against Georgia is mounted: Savannah is captured on December 29, 1778, and Georgia is subdued. General Lincoln is sent to command in the Southern Department, tasked with the defence of the South.

Chapter XVIII — Middlebrook Winter Quarters — Canada Expedition Abandoned (Winter 1778–79)

The American army settles into winter cantonments. Washington establishes his headquarters at Middlebrook, New Jersey, a strong defensive position in the Watchung Mountains. A plan of alarm signals for the Jerseys is devised to warn of British incursions. Lafayette’s project for an invasion of Canada — a scheme that has strong backing in Congress — is debated. Washington opposes the expedition vigorously, arguing that the army lacks the resources, the troops are too few, the season is wrong, and the Canadians have shown little enthusiasm for the American cause. Congress eventually abandons the plan. Washington visits Philadelphia, where he finds the war spirit declining, dissensions growing in Congress, and sectional feelings threatening national unity. He makes patriotic appeals to Congress and the country. Plans for the next campaign are developed. An avenging expedition against the Indian tribes is set on foot to repress frontier atrocities. Discontents among the Jersey troops over pay and conditions are appeased by Washington’s personal intervention. The campaign against the Iroquois — the Sullivan-Clinton expedition — is launched and proves successful, destroying Indian settlements and breaking the power of the Six Nations.

Chapter XIX — Stony Point Stormed (May–July 1779)

The enemy turns to predatory warfare. British forces ravage the Chesapeake Bay. Hostilities on the Hudson intensify: Sir Henry Clinton captures Verplanck’s Point and Stony Point, two key positions on opposite sides of the Hudson, fortifying them as bases for further operations. New Haven, Connecticut, is captured and plundered. Fairfield and Norwalk, Connecticut, are burned to the ground by British forces under General Tryon. Washington devises a counter-stroke. On the night of July 15-16, 1779, Brigadier General Anthony Wayne leads a picked force of light infantry in a daring midnight assault on Stony Point. The attack is made with unloaded muskets and fixed bayonets — orders are to rely on cold steel alone. The storming party crosses the flooded causeway under fire, scales the rocky heights, and takes the fort by surprise in a twenty-five-minute action. It is one of the most brilliant American feats of arms of the war. The fort’s guns are spiked and the works demolished before the Americans withdraw. A generous letter from Lee (Henry “Light-Horse Harry” Lee, not Charles Lee) commends Wayne’s exploit.

Chapter XX — Paulus Hook — West Point Fortified (Summer 1779)

An expedition against Penobscot, Maine, by a combined American naval and land force ends in disaster — the Massachusetts ships are trapped and destroyed by a British relief squadron. Major Henry (“Light-Horse Harry”) Lee conducts a night surprise of Paulus Hook (present-day Jersey City) on August 19, 1779, capturing the British garrison in a daring raid. Washington turns his attention to fortifying West Point as the key defensive position on the Hudson — a natural fortress commanding a great bend in the river. His style of living there is described: the daily routine at headquarters, the table at which he entertains officers and distinguished visitors with simple dignity. Sir Henry Clinton receives reinforcements from Europe. The Count d’Estaing arrives on the coast of Georgia with the French fleet. Plans are made in consequence — the French cooperate with General Lincoln in an attempt to recapture Savannah. The French minister visits Washington at his Highland camp. Washington writes to Lafayette. The siege of Savannah fails; d’Estaing is repulsed with heavy losses, including the death of the Polish nobleman Count Casimir Pulaski. Washington sends reinforcements to Lincoln. The army goes into winter quarters. Sir Henry Clinton dispatches an expedition to the South — the opening move of the British southern strategy.

Chapter XXI — The Morristown Winter (Winter 1779–80)

The army suffers terribly at Morristown, New Jersey, during the winter of 1779-80 — the worst winter of the war. The weather is intensely cold, with snowstorms and freezing temperatures exceeding even those of Valley Forge. The currency is in a state of complete derangement — Continental dollars have become nearly worthless. The commissariat is in chaos; supplies cannot be purchased with depreciated paper money. Washington authorizes the impressment of supplies from the surrounding countryside. The people of New Jersey display patriotic conduct, supplying the army despite the hardship to themselves. New York Bay freezes over, allowing the British to move troops and supplies across the ice. Lord Stirling leads an expedition against Staten Island. General Knyphausen launches an incursion into the Jerseys. The church of the Reverend James Caldwell at Elizabethtown is burned by the British — Caldwell’s character as a fighting parson is described. A British foray into Westchester County results in the burning of Young’s House in the valley of the Neperan. The army clings to existence through the brutal winter, its endurance a testament to Washington’s leadership.

Chapter XXII — Arnold in Philadelphia (1778–79)

Benedict Arnold, now military commander of Philadelphia after the British evacuation, is depicted in an unflattering light. His command is characterized by unpopular measures: he fraternizes with the loyalist elite, adopts an extravagant lifestyle far beyond his means, and engages in dubious commercial schemes and speculations. He collides repeatedly with the Pennsylvania Executive Council, which regards him with suspicion. His land project — a scheme to profit from Western lands — embroils him in controversy. Formal charges are sent against him to Congress. He publishes a defiant address to the public defending his conduct. The charges are referred to a court-martial. Amid these troubles, Arnold marries Margaret (“Peggy”) Shippen, the beautiful young daughter of a prominent Philadelphia loyalist-leaning family — a marriage that draws him deeper into the British orbit. The court-martial verdict convicts him on minor charges; Washington issues a public reprimand. Arnold obtains leave of absence from the army, brooding over what he regards as unjust treatment. The seeds of his treason are sown in these months of resentment, debt, and bitterness.

Chapter XXIII — The Southern Campaign Begins — Siege of Charleston (Early 1780)

South Carolina is threatened. The chapter describes the province’s condition and mixed population — wealthy lowcountry planters, upcountry Scots-Irish settlers, and a large enslaved population. Sir Henry Clinton endures a stormy voyage from New York to the South, losing many horses overboard. Lieutenant-Colonel Banastre Tarleton — young, ruthless, and brilliant — is introduced; his character as a cavalry commander is sketched. The British fleet arrives at Tybee Island. Clinton advances upon Charleston, South Carolina’s principal city and port. General Lincoln prepares for defence, aided by Commodore Whipple of the Continental Navy and Governor John Rutledge. Washington writes of his forebodings about the southern theatre. British troops embark from New York to reinforce Clinton. Washington sends De Kalb southward with Continental reinforcements. Washington writes a hopeful letter to Steuben about the prospects of the southern campaign, despite the growing danger.

Chapter XXIV — Currency Crisis — Arnold’s Debt and Difficulties (Winter-Spring 1780)

The evils of the depreciating Continental currency are examined in depth — its collapse is undermining the war effort at every level. Washington proposes military reforms to Congress, but Congress is jealous of military power and suspicious of standing armies. A committee of three is sent to confer with Washington at headquarters. The army’s losses from currency depreciation are to be made good to the troops — a promise that proves difficult to fulfil. Lafayette returns from France with joyful news: a French expeditionary force under Rochambeau is on its way. A scheme for a combined attack on New York is contemplated. Arnold, meanwhile, is beset by debts and difficulties in Philadelphia. He makes overtures to the French minister — seeking money or position — but is rebuffed. Anxious to return to active service and escape his creditors and critics, he seeks a command. A mutiny of the Connecticut troops over their enlistment terms erupts. Washington writes urgently to Joseph Reed, president of Pennsylvania, for aid from the Pennsylvania militia. The good effects of his letter are seen as the mutiny is quelled.

Chapter XXV — Fall of Charleston — Gates’s Southern Command (May 1780)

The siege of Charleston continues. British ships force their way past Fort Moultrie into Charleston Harbor. British troops under General Prevost march up from Savannah to complete the encirclement. Tarleton and his dragoons prove their ferocity in a brush with Colonel Washington (William Washington, a distant cousin of the commander-in-chief) at Monk’s Corner and in an action at Lanier’s Ferry. Charleston is reinforced by General Woodford with Virginia Continentals, but the situation is hopeless. On May 12, 1780, General Lincoln surrenders Charleston and his entire army of over 5,000 men — the worst American defeat of the war. Tarleton’s reputation for brutality is sealed when he overtakes Colonel Buford’s retreating regiment at Waxhaw Creek and, after a brief resistance, refuses quarter, slaughtering over a hundred men — the infamous “Waxhaw Massacre.” Sir Henry Clinton, leaving Cornwallis in command of the South, embarks for New York, believing the southern colonies are effectively subdued.

Chapter XXVI — Knyphausen’s Incursion into the Jerseys (June 1780)

General Knyphausen, the Hessian commander left in charge of New York, launches a marauding expedition into the Jerseys. The British sack Connecticut Farms (present-day Union, New Jersey). The narrative describes the murder of Mrs. Hannah Caldwell, wife of the Reverend James Caldwell — she is shot through a window by a British soldier while holding her infant child. The incident becomes a celebrated atrocity story that galvanizes American resistance. Sir Henry Clinton, arriving back from Charleston, takes personal command. The British burn Springfield, New Jersey, but are checked by American militia and Continentals under General Greene. The Jerseys are evacuated by the British as they fall back to New York.

Chapter XXVII — Gates Appointed to the Southern Command (Summer 1780)

Washington applies to the state legislatures for aid in recruiting the depleted army. The ladies of Philadelphia organize subscriptions to support the soldiers. Amid the crisis in the South, Congress appoints Gates — the hero of Saratoga — to command the Southern Department, passing over Washington’s preferred candidate. The French fleet arrives at Newport, Rhode Island, with the Count de Rochambeau. Preparations begin for a combined movement against New York. Arnold obtains command at West Point — a crucial posting that will prove disastrous. Greene resigns the office of quartermaster-general, exhausted by the thankless administrative burden.

Chapter XXVIII — Battle of Camden (August 16, 1780)

The narrative describes the difficulties of invading North Carolina: the character of its people — hardy, independent, and largely hostile to the British — and its rugged, wooded terrain. Thomas Sumter is introduced — his character, his story, and his role as a partisan leader. His attacks on Rocky Mount and Hanging Rock are described. The slow advance of De Kalb’s Continentals southward is traced. Gates arrives to take command and immediately decides on a bold advance into South Carolina. The army marches through a desolate, sandy region, subsisting on green corn and unripe peaches — many men fall ill. The Battle of Camden (August 16, 1780) is narrated: Gates’s foolish deployment of militia on the left wing against Cornwallis’s best troops; the militia’s immediate flight; the desperate stand of the Continentals under De Kalb, who falls mortally wounded with eleven wounds; Gates’s flight from the field — he rode 60 miles to Charlotte in a single day, his reputation destroyed. Sumter is surprised by Tarleton at the Waxhaws. Washington’s opinion of militia is expressed in his letter to Gates after the disaster. The American cause in the South lies in ruins.

Chapter XXIX — Arnold’s Treason — Negotiations with André (Summer 1780)

The narrative turns to the dramatic central episode of the volume: the treason of Benedict Arnold. His secret correspondence with the enemy through Major John André, Sir Henry Clinton’s adjutant-general, is traced. Arnold’s negotiations with André proceed through intermediaries — Joseph Stansbury, a Philadelphia merchant, and Joshua Hett Smith. The details of the plot emerge: Arnold agrees to surrender West Point — the key fortress of the Hudson — in exchange for £20,000 and a brigadier-general’s commission in the British army. The parting scene with Washington at Arnold’s headquarters is laden with dramatic irony — Washington unsuspectingly confides in a man who is plotting to betray him. The midnight conference on the banks of the Hudson aboard the British sloop Vulture takes place on the night of September 21-22, 1780. André is rowed ashore and meets with Arnold in the darkness among the trees. The return of André by land — against his wishes, as he had wanted to return by water — and the circumstances of his capture near Tarrytown by three militiamen (John Paulding, Isaac Van Wart, and David Williams) on September 23, 1780, are described. The incriminating papers hidden in André’s boot reveal the plot.

Chapter XXX — Arnold’s Flight — Washington’s Response (September 1780)

Washington had travelled to Hartford for an interview with the French officers (Rochambeau and Ternay) to plan combined operations. The return journey is described — Washington had planned to stop at Arnold’s headquarters on his way back. A change of plan delays him. Meanwhile, the captured André is brought to Arnold’s headquarters. Tidings of the capture reach Arnold on the morning of September 24. Arnold hastily excuses himself from the breakfast table, flees to the shore, boards his barge, and escapes down the Hudson to the Vulture. He leaves his wife, Peggy Shippen Arnold, behind. Washington’s arrival at Arnold’s headquarters shortly after the flight — his calm composure in the crisis — is one of the great scenes of the biography. He takes immediate precautions to secure West Point and the other Highland posts. Letters from the traitor are received, attempting to justify his conduct. Mrs. Arnold’s situation is described — her apparent hysterical collapse (which some historians believe was a calculated performance to protect herself). Washington, ever chivalrous, sees that she is safely conducted to her family in Philadelphia.

Chapter XXXI — André’s Trial and Execution (October 1780)

Major John André’s conduct as a prisoner of war is described: his dignity, his charm, his fatalistic acceptance of his fate. He converses with Colonel Benjamin Tallmadge, the head of Washington’s intelligence service, who tells him the story of Nathan Hale — the American spy executed by the British in 1776 — a story that has a profound effect on André. André is held at Tappan, New York. A flurry of correspondence on his behalf ensues: Sir Henry Clinton pleads for his life; General Robertson comes to Tappan under a flag of truce to argue that André was not a spy but a prisoner of war under a flag. Washington refuses the appeal. A board of general officers convenes to try André; they find him guilty of spying and sentence him to death. André is executed by hanging on October 2, 1780. His composure on the scaffold, his remark “I am reconciled to my death, but I detest the mode,” and his placing of the noose around his own neck are described. The three captors are rewarded by Congress. Arnold receives his blood money and a British commission, but his name becomes a synonym for treachery. He issues a proclamation attempting to justify his actions. The after fortunes of Mrs. Arnold are summarized — she eventually reunites with her husband in British-occupied New York and later in England.

Chapter XXXII — Greene Takes Command in the South (Autumn 1780)

Greene assumes command at West Point in the immediate aftermath of the Arnold affair. Insidious attempts are made to shake Washington’s confidence in his other officers — suspicion and paranoia threaten the army’s morale. A plan is devised to entrap Arnold — to capture him from New York and bring him to justice. The character of Sergeant John Champe, who volunteers for the dangerous mission, is described. The plot fails when the boat meant to extract him is delayed. A court of inquiry investigates Gates’s conduct at Camden; his reputation is tarnished but he avoids a formal court-martial. Greene is appointed to the Southern Department — Washington’s best battlefield commander is sent to salvage the situation in the South. Washington’s detailed instructions to Greene are given. Incursions from Canada threaten the northern frontier; the Mohawk Valley is ravaged by British and Indian forces. The state of the army is surveyed — reforms are adopted, including enlistment for the duration of the war and half-pay for officers. The army’s institutional foundation is strengthened.

Chapter XXXIII — Lafayette’s Light Infantry — New York Island Plans (Autumn 1780)

The Marquis Lafayette takes command of a picked corps of light infantry — the elite of the army. He proposes a brilliant stroke against British posts on New York Island, dreaming of decisive action. Preparations for an attack are made. The Marquis of Chastellux, a French general and man of letters, visits the American camp and records his impressions of Washington and the army. The attack on the British posts is ultimately given up as impractical. General Stark forages in Westchester County. Major Benjamin Tallmadge conducts a daring exploit on Long Island, capturing a British officer. The inactivity of the campaigning season frustrates the eager young Frenchman.

Chapter XXXIV — Cornwallis in South Carolina — Ferguson’s Foray — Battle of King’s Mountain (Autumn 1780)

The narrative returns to the South. Cornwallis adopts rigorous measures to subdue South Carolina — confiscations, harsh reprisals, and the mobilization of loyalist militia. Major Patrick Ferguson is sent with a force of loyalists to scour the mountain country between the Catawba and the Yadkin rivers, tasked with protecting Cornwallis’s left flank and recruiting loyalists. Ferguson, a brilliant and inventive officer, finds himself in a hornet’s nest as the backcountry people rise against him. The mountain men — the fierce Scotch-Irish and German settlers of the Appalachian frontier — and the “men from Kentucky” rally under Colonel William Campbell, John Sevier, and Isaac Shelby. They pursue Ferguson to King’s Mountain, a rocky ridge on the border of North and South Carolina. The Battle of King’s Mountain (October 7, 1780) is narrated: the frontiersmen surround the hill, advance from tree to tree, and pick off the loyalists with their long rifles. Ferguson is killed, shot from his horse with his silver whistle still at his lips. The entire loyalist force is killed or captured. Cornwallis orders a retrograde march back into South Carolina, his invasion of North Carolina temporarily halted. The victory sparks new hope in the southern backcountry.

Chapter XXXV — Marion and Sumter — Greene on the Pedee (Late 1780)

The legendary figure of Francis Marion — “the Swamp Fox” — is introduced. His character, his strange by-names, his haunting of the swamps of the Pee Dee and Santee — eluding British pursuit and striking at isolated outposts. Tarleton goes in quest of him but cannot catch him. Sumter operates on the west side of the Santee and has a sharp affair with Tarleton at Blackstock Hill, where the British cavalry is repulsed. Gates is at Hillsborough, but his domestic misfortunes have compounded his military disgrace — he has lost a son. Greene arrives to take command of the shattered southern army. His considerate conduct toward the defeated Gates is a mark of his character; Gates retires to his estate. The condition of Greene’s army is described — a handful of ragged Continentals and unreliable militia. Colonel William Washington (the cavalry commander) employs a clever stratagem at Clermont. Daniel Morgan is detached to the district of Ninety-Six to raise the militia there. Greene posts himself on the Pedee River, establishing a secure base from which to rebuild his army.

Chapter XXXVI — Arnold in Virginia — Pennsylvania Line Mutiny (Winter 1781)

Hostile embarkations from New York signal a new British offensive in the South. Arnold, now a British brigadier-general, is given command of an expedition to Virginia — his commission from Clinton. The country is in a necessitous state, its resources nearly exhausted by four years of war. Washington urgently presses for a foreign loan; Colonel John Laurens is sent as special envoy to France to seek aid in men and money. Meanwhile, grievances of the Pennsylvania Line — over enlistment terms, bounties, and the scandalous injustice that many men who signed up “for the war” had been kept beyond their original three-year terms — come to a head. The Pennsylvania Line mutinies on January 1, 1781, with over 1,300 men marching toward Philadelphia to demand redress. The mutineers negotiate with emissaries from Congress and from Sir Henry Clinton (who attempts to lure them to the British side). An accommodation is reached — the men are discharged or granted furloughs. Washington doubts the wisdom of the conciliatory policy, fearing it will encourage other mutinies. He adopts a rigorous course with other malcontents — executing the leaders of a mutiny in the New Jersey Line — and the policy proves successful. The Articles of Confederation, finally ratified by all thirteen states, come into effect, giving the national government a more solid legal foundation.

Chapter XXXVII — Arnold in Virginia — Steuben Checks the Ravages (January–March 1781)

Arnold’s expedition into Virginia is described as buccaneering in character — he sails up the James River, destroys public stores, burns Richmond (the new state capital), and ravages the countryside. Baron Steuben, commanding in Virginia, attempts to check Arnold’s depredations with a scratch force of militia and new levies. Arnold establishes a strong base at Portsmouth. Congress resolves to form executive departments — a crucial administrative reform. Alexander Hamilton is suggested by Sullivan for the post of Superintendent of Finance. Washington expresses his high opinion of Hamilton’s abilities. But a misunderstanding between Hamilton and the commander-in-chief — a quarrel at headquarters over a trifling matter — leads to Hamilton’s angry resignation as Washington’s aide-de-camp. Washington’s relationship with his brilliant but prickly protégé is strained, though their mutual respect endures.

Chapter XXXVIII — Cowpens (January 17, 1781)

Cornwallis prepares to invade North Carolina. He dispatches Tarleton with a picked force of infantry, cavalry, and light artillery to destroy the American detachment under Daniel Morgan. Morgan, with his mixed force of Continentals and militia, takes a defensive position at Hannah’s Cowpens — a grassy grazing ground in the South Carolina backcountry. The Battle of Cowpens (January 17, 1781) is narrated with full drama: Morgan’s masterful tactical deployment — the skirmishers in front, the militia in the centre instructed to fire two volleys and fall back, the Continentals on the ridge, and the cavalry concealed behind. Tarleton, contemptuous of the Americans, orders a headlong assault. The plan works perfectly: the militia falls back, drawing the British into a trap; the Continentals deliver a devastating volley and countercharge; the cavalry sweeps around the flank. The British force is destroyed — over 800 casualties against only 72 Americans. Tarleton escapes but his reputation is tarnished. Morgan pushes for the Catawba with his prisoners and spoils. Cornwallis, furious, endeavours to intercept him. The rising of the Catawba River — providential for the Americans — slows Cornwallis. Cornwallis halts at Ramsour’s Mills to consider his next move.

Chapter XXXIX — Greene’s Fabian Retreat to the Dan (January–February 1781)

Greene joins Morgan on the Catawba. Confronting Cornwallis’s superior army, Greene adopts the Fabian policy — avoiding battle while wearing down the enemy through manoeuvre and attrition. Cornwallis moves to cross the Catawba. An affair at McGowan’s Ford gives Greene time to retreat. Tarleton surprises the militia at Tarrant’s Tavern. Cornwallis’s advance is checked by the rising of the Yadkin River — another providential flood. A dramatic contest of skill and speed ensues as the two armies race for the Dan River. Greene masterfully manages his retreat, sending his infantry ahead while covering their withdrawal with cavalry and light troops. Cornwallis, encumbered by his baggage train, cannot catch him. Greene crosses the Dan into Virginia, his army intact and Cornwallis’s supply lines stretched to the breaking point. The race to the Dan is one of the finest examples of Greene’s generalship.

Chapter XL — Battle of Guilford Court House (March 15, 1781)

Cornwallis takes post at Hillsborough and issues a grandiloquent proclamation calling on loyalists to rally to the King’s standard. Greene recrosses the Dan into North Carolina. The country is scoured by the partisan bands of Henry Lee and Andrew Pickens. An affair with Colonel Pyle — in which Lee’s Legion, posing as British dragoons, captures or kills Pyle’s loyalist force — demonstrates the ruthless nature of the civil war in the South. Cornwallis manoeuvres desperately to bring Greene to battle. On March 15, 1781, the two armies meet at Guilford Court House. Greene adopts a three-line defensive formation, placing militia in front of the Continentals. The battle is fierce and protracted: the first line of militia fires and retreats; the second line of militia stands briefly but gives way; the third line — the Continental regulars — fights a desperate bayonet and musket duel with the British. Cornwallis, in a controversial act, orders his artillery to fire grapeshot into a melee where British and American troops are locked together. The Americans are forced from the field, but Cornwallis’s army is shattered — it has lost over a quarter of its strength. Greene retreats to Troublesome Creek. Cornwallis, unable to pursue, marches toward Cape Fear to rest and refit. Greene pursues him, brings him to a stand at Deep River, then determines to face about and carry the war back into South Carolina — a stroke of strategic genius. Cornwallis, abandoning the southern interior, marches for Virginia, hoping to link up with other British forces there.

Chapter XLI — Lafayette in Virginia — Operations Against Arnold (Spring 1781)

Arnold remains at Portsmouth, Virginia. Expeditions are sent against him. Washington issues detailed instructions to Lafayette, who is dispatched with a light corps to Virginia to oppose Arnold. Washington goes to Newport for consultations with Rochambeau. The French fleet sails from Newport but is pursued by the British. Lafayette’s expedition to Virginia gets underway. An engagement between the English and French fleets takes place off the Virginia Capes — the French fail to gain a decisive advantage. The expedition against Arnold at Portsmouth fails when the French ships cannot enter the harbour. Washington writes a frank letter to Colonel Laurens about the situation. Measures are taken to reinforce Greene’s southern army. General Phillips takes command at Portsmouth and begins marauding through the Virginia countryside. His depredations are checked by Lafayette, who marches and manoeuvres with energy. Mount Vernon itself is menaced — a British raiding party comes near the estate, and Lund Washington, the General’s cousin and estate manager, supplies them with provisions to prevent destruction. Washington is displeased when he learns of this. Phillips dies of fever, removing a capable British commander.

Chapter XLII — Washington and Rochambeau at Wethersfield (May 1781)

The army’s state remains inefficient, with recruitment lagging far behind requirements. Delancey’s loyalist corps conducts a marauding expedition. Colonel Greene (not Nathanael — this is another officer) is killed in action. The Count de Barras arrives to replace Destouches in command of the French squadron at Newport. More French naval forces are expected from the West Indies. Washington and Rochambeau meet at Wethersfield, Connecticut, in May 1781 for a crucial conference. The plan of combined operations is formulated: the immediate objective is to attack the British posts on New York Island. A financial arrangement by Robert Morris, the new Superintendent of Finance, provides hope of stable funding. A scheme is devised to attack the British works on New York Island and capture Delancey’s Corps. The American and French armies encamp in Westchester County. Reconnoitring expeditions are undertaken to probe the British defences. Washington begins to doubt the feasibility of the New York plan as French naval movements offer alternative possibilities.

Chapter XLIII — Cornwallis and Lafayette in Virginia (Summer 1781)

Movements and counter-movements between Cornwallis and Lafayette in Virginia are described in detail. Cornwallis marches through eastern Virginia, trying to trap the young Marquis. Tarleton and his troopers scour the countryside. Tarleton makes a dramatic dash at the Virginia Legislature, meeting in Charlottesville — lawmakers scatter to escape capture. An attempt is made to surprise Governor Thomas Jefferson at Monticello; Jefferson escapes narrowly, fleeing to Carter’s Mountain. Steuben is outwitted by Simcoe, the bold commander of the Queen’s Rangers. Lafayette is joined by Anthony Wayne with a brigade of Pennsylvania Continentals and by Steuben with Virginia levies. Lafayette now acts on the aggressive. A desperate mêlée between Macpherson and Simcoe’s forces is narrated. Cornwallis is pursued to Jamestown Island. “Mad Anthony” Wayne finds himself trapped in a morass during a sharp engagement; his impetuous valour extricates his men. Lafayette’s alertness prevents disaster. Washington writes his considered opinion of the Virginia campaign, praising Lafayette’s prudence and energy. Cornwallis, following Clinton’s orders and his own inclinations, moves to Yorktown on the Virginia Peninsula, where he fortifies and awaits developments.

Chapter XLIV — Greene’s Campaign in South Carolina (Spring–Summer 1781)

Greene’s retrograde operation in South Carolina — his southward march after the battle of Guilford — unfolds. He appears before Camden. The Battle of Hobkirk’s Hill (April 25, 1781) is fought: Greene’s attack misfires when an American regiment breaks under a British bayonet charge; he is forced to retreat, but Rawdon cannot pursue. Rawdon abandons Camden and falls back toward Charleston. Rapid successes follow for the Americans as the British outposts in the interior collapse. Greene lays siege to the fortress of Ninety-Six, a formidable star fort in the South Carolina backcountry, but is forced to raise the siege when Rawdon marches to its relief. Operations against Lord Rawdon continue. Greene retires to the High Hills of Santee to rest and refit his army in a healthy position. Sumter scours the lower country. Colonel Wade Hampton makes a bold dash to the gates of Charleston. Exploits of Lee and Hampton, and of Captain Armstrong at Quimby Bridge, are described. Actions in the neighbourhood of Charleston continue. Greene’s campaign — a masterpiece of strategic manoeuvre — ends with most of South Carolina’s interior in American hands; the British are bottled up in Charleston, Georgetown, and the coastal enclaves.

Chapter XLV — March to Yorktown — Visit to Mount Vernon (August–September 1781)

Washington is disappointed by the failure of the states to provide promised reinforcements for the campaign against New York. Word arrives that the French fleet under the Count de Grasse is destined for the Chesapeake, not New York. Washington resolves to abandon the proposed attack on New York and instead march the combined Franco-American army to Virginia to trap Cornwallis. Attempts on New York are postponed. The march of the armies to the Chesapeake begins — a masterwork of deception. Washington and Rochambeau march the troops through New Jersey and Pennsylvania, keeping the British in New York guessing as to their destination until it is too late. Stratagems to deceive the enemy are employed: false preparations for an attack on Staten Island, a conspicuous camp left in New Jersey. Arnold, now a British officer, ravages New London, Connecticut — his home state — in a final act of betrayal. Washington arrives in Philadelphia. The combined armies march through the city in a grand spectacle on September 2, 1781. Then Washington, accompanied by Rochambeau, travels by separate route toward the Chesapeake. On September 9, 1781, Washington visits Mount Vernon for the first time in six years — the first time he has seen his beloved home since the start of the war. The chapter describes the emotional homecoming, the crowding of guests at the estate (including the French officers Chastellux and Rochambeau), and the generous Virginian hospitality. On September 12, Washington tears himself away and rides onward to join Lafayette at Williamsburg. Cornwallis is entrenched at Yorktown. The stage is set for the decisive siege. The volume closes with Washington’s army converging on the British position and the combined forces preparing for the climax of the war.
