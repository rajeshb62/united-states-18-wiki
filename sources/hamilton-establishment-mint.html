Report on the Establishment of a Mint

Hamilton’s comprehensive plan for a national coinage system, responding to six specific questions posed by the House of Representatives and detailing the organization of the mint itself.

Submitted to the House of Representatives, January 28, 1791 Alexander Hamilton, Secretary of the Treasury

Source

Hamilton, Alexander. “Report on the Establishment of a Mint.” In Report on the Establishment of a National Bank and a Mint, by Alexander Hamilton, 272–325. Philadelphia: William M’Lean, 1791. Reprinted in The Works of Alexander Hamilton (Vol. I).

Hamilton’s report on the mint addresses six specific questions posed by the House of Representatives, plus the organization of the mint itself. The report opens with a framing of the immense stakes involved: “The general state of debtor and creditor; all the relations and consequences of price; the essential interests of trade and industry; the value of all property; the whole income, both of the state and of individuals, are liable to be sensibly influenced, beneficially, or otherwise, by the judicious, or injudicious regulation of this interesting object.” He acknowledges the difficulty of the subject — one that “has frequently occupied the reflections and researches of politicians, without having harmonized their opinions on some of the most important of the principles.”

Hamilton then answers why reform is necessary at all: “The immense disorder which actually reigns in so delicate and important a concern, and the still greater disorder which is every moment possible, call loudly for a reform.” He notes that the dollar, originally contemplated in the money transactions of the country, had “by successive diminutions of its weight and fineness, sustained a depreciation of five per cent.” Furthermore, “a nation ought not to suffer the value of the property of its citizens to fluctuate with the fluctuations of a foreign mint, and to change with the changes in the regulations of a foreign sovereign.” The unequal values allowed in different parts of the union for coins of the same intrinsic worth, the defective species embarrassing circulation, and the dissimilarity in the several state monies of account are all cited as inconveniences that a national coinage would remedy.

I. What Ought to Be the Money Unit?

Hamilton examines the existing monetary situation in depth. The pound is the unit of account in all states but with varying values — the Pennsylvania pound equals 2.6666 Spanish dollars, while the Virginia pound equals 2.5 dollars. However, there being no formal regulation on the point (the resolutions of Congress of July 6, 1785, and August 8, 1786, never having been carried into operation), the actual unit can only be inferred from usage. The manner of adjusting foreign exchanges indicates the dollar as best entitled to that character: “the old piastre of Spain, or old Seville piece of eight rials, of the value of four shillings and six pence sterling, is evidently contemplated.” For instance, one hundred pounds sterling equals one hundred and sixty-six and two-thirds pounds Pennsylvania currency, corresponding with the proportion between four shillings six pence sterling and seven shillings six pence, the current value of the dollar in that state.

But Hamilton notes that this circumstance “loses much of its weight” from two considerations: the Spanish dollar “has never had any settled or standard value, according to weight or fineness; but has been permitted to circulate by tale without regard to either; very much as a mere money of convenience,” while gold has had a fixed price by weight with an eye to its fineness. This “greater stability of value of the gold coins is an argument of force for regarding the money unit as having been hitherto virtually attached to gold, rather than to silver.”

The old Seville piece weighed 17 dwt. 12 grains and contained 386 grains 15 mites of fine silver, but this piece “has been long since out of circulation.” The dollars now in common circulation are of recent date and much inferior both in weight and fineness — average weight 17 dwt. 8 grains. The most reliable experiment makes the new dollar contain 370 grains and 933 thousandth parts of a grain of pure silver. The Spanish standard for silver coin in 1761 was 261 parts fine and 27 parts alloy; but this standard has since been altered considerably for the worse. From market computations in Amsterdam and London, the new dollar appears to contain about 368 grains of fine silver, and its immediate predecessor about 374 grains.

Hamilton ultimately argues for preserving the identity of the dollar for public confidence, and recommends the dollar as the money unit of the United States. He defines the dollar as containing 371.25 grains of fine silver (or the equivalent value in gold, 24.75 grains of fine gold). This figure is “within a small fraction of the mean of the two last emissions of dollars; the only ones which are now found in common circulation, and of which the newest is in the greatest abundance.” The alloy in each case to be one-twelfth of the total weight, making the unit 27 grains of standard gold and 405 grains of standard silver.

II. The Proportion Between Gold and Silver

Hamilton addresses the bimetallic ratio at great length, presenting one of the most sophisticated early American analyses of bimetallism. He surveys ratios adopted by European nations — England at 15.2:1, France at 15.5:1, Holland varying — and examines the dynamics of bimetallic flow in detail. His reasoning on the bimetallic question proceeds through several stages.

The case against exclusive attachment to either metal. Hamilton notes that a preference “ought to be given to neither of the metals for the money unit.” He directly challenges the prevailing Congressional resolutions (July 6, 1785 and August 8, 1786) which had attached the unit to silver, arguing that “if either were to be preferred, it ought to be gold rather than silver.” His reasons: gold “may, perhaps, in certain senses, be said to have greater stability than silver. As being of superior value, less liberties have been taken with it in the regulations of different countries. Its standard has remained more uniform. As being not so much an article of merchandize, owing to the use made of silver in the trade with the East-Indies and China, it is less liable to be influenced by circumstances of commercial demand.”

However, Hamilton ultimately concludes that attaching the unit exclusively to either metal “cannot be done effectually, without destroying the office and character of one of them as money; and reducing it to the situation of a mere merchandize.” He argues that “to annul the use of either of the metals as money, is to abridge the quantity of circulating medium, and is liable to all the objections which arise from a comparison of the benefits of a full, with the evils of a scanty circulation.” He dismisses the counterargument that only the favored metal would find its way into the country: “The practicability of this would in some measure depend on the abundance or scarcity of it in the country paying.” Furthermore, “it is often in the course of trade as desirable to possess the kind of money, as the kind of commodities, best adapted to a foreign market.”

The dynamics of bimetallic flow. Hamilton provides a rigorous explanation: “If two countries are supposed, in one of which the proportion of gold to silver is as 1 to 16, in the other as 1 to 15, gold being worth more, silver less, in one than in the other; it is manifest, that in their reciprocal payments, each will select that species which it values least, to pay to the other where it is valued most.” He then cites empirical evidence: “In Spain and England, where gold is rated higher than in other parts of Europe, there is a scarcity of silver; while it is found to abound in France and Holland, where it is rated higher in proportion to gold, than in the neighbouring nations.” He also notes the global flow “from Europe to China and the East-Indies, owing to the comparative cheapness of it in the former, and dearness of it in the latter.”

The danger of specie reduction. Hamilton warns that a more serious consequence than the mere banishment of one metal is “the diminution of the total quantity of specie which a country would naturally possess.” As often as a country over-rating either metal receives payment in that metal, it gets a less actual quantity than it ought. “There will be a continual effort to make payment to it in that species to which it has annexed an exaggerated estimation, wherever it is current at a less proportional value.” He qualifies this with caution, noting that “in such matters, there are always some local, and, many other particular circumstances, which qualify and vary the operation of general principles.”

A third ill consequence is “a greater and more frequent disturbance of the state of the money unit, by a greater and more frequent diversity between the legal and market proportions of the metals.” Though not yet experienced in the United States, Hamilton warns this would follow when commerce attained maturity.

The recommended ratio. Hamilton surveys the historical and contemporary evidence. He cites Sir Isaac Newton’s 1717 representation to the British Treasury: “By the course of trade and exchange between nation and nation in all Europe, fine gold is to fine silver as 14⅘ or 15 to 1.” Hamilton acknowledges that more than seventy years have passed since Newton’s calculation, but believes “the state of the matter, as represented by Sir Isaac Newton, is not very remote from its actual state.” In Holland, in December 1789, gold was to silver as 1 to 14.88; in London it approached 1 to 15. The existing proportion in the United States is about 1 to 15. Hamilton notes that “the innovations of the Spanish mint have imperceptibly introduced a proportion so analogous as this is, to that which prevails among the principal commercial nations.” The proportion of 1 to 15 is further recommended “by the particular situation of our trade, as being very nearly that which obtains in the market of Great-Britain, to which nation our specie is principally exported.” He recommends approximately 15:1, noting that “there can hardly be a better rule, in any country, for the legal, than the market proportion; if this can be supposed to have been produced by the free and steady course of commercial principles.”

III. The Proportion and Composition of Alloy

Pure gold and silver are too soft for practical coinage. Hamilton reviews English standards (11 parts fine gold to 1 part alloy — i.e., 22 carats fine), noting this coincides with the proportion in the coins of Portugal, England, France, and Spain. In the two former it is real; in the two latter there is a deduction for “remedy of weight and alloy.” The principal gold coins in Germany, Holland, Sweden, Denmark, Poland, and Italy are finer, ranging from 1¼ to 1⅞ carats above the English standard. Silver standards vary widely: Great Britain’s is 222 parts fine to 18 alloy, while other European nations range from 17 parts better to 75 worse.

The principal reasons for alloy are “the saving of expense in the refining of the metals” and “the rendering of them harder, as a security against too great waste by friction or wearing.” Hamilton notes that the reality of the hardening effect has been denied — “experience has been appealed to, as proving that the more alloyed coins wear faster than the purer” — but he considers the saving of trouble and expense sufficient inducement to follow existing examples. He recommends following the nations with whom the US has most intercourse: Spain, Portugal, England, and France.

For silver, copper is “the only proper one” for alloy. For gold, a mixture of silver and copper is used — in English coins equal parts, in other countries varying from one-third to two-thirds silver. The silver counteracts copper’s tendency to injure the coin’s color, while copper prevents the too great whiteness that silver alone would confer. Hamilton recommends establishing by law that the proportion of silver to copper in gold coins shall not be more than one-half nor less than one-third, vesting discretion in some proper place to regulate within those limits as experience recommends.

IV. Whether the Expense of Coinage Shall Be Defrayed by the Government

Hamilton presents an extended argument on gratuitous vs. duty-based coinage, calling it “perhaps, one of the nicest questions in the doctrine of money.” The practice of different nations is dissimilar: in England coinage is entirely free (the mint price of bullion equals the value in coin); in France there is a duty of eight percent; in Holland a difference of .96 percent on gold and 1.48 percent on silver. The resolution of August 8, 1786, had proceeded on the idea of a deduction of half percent from gold and two percent from silver.

Hamilton identifies two methods of defraying coinage expense out of the metals: (1) reducing the quantity of fine gold and silver in the coins, or (2) establishing a difference between the value of metals in coin and the mint price in bullion. He declares the first method “inadmissible,” unable to distinguish it from “raising the denomination of the coin; a measure which has been disapproved by the wisest men of the nations in which it has been practised, and condemned by the rest of the world.” To declare that less weight of gold or silver shall pass for the same sum “obliging creditors to receive less than their just dues, and depreciating property of every kind.” He warns that “there is scarcely any point, in the economy of national affairs, of greater moment than the uniform preservation of the intrinsic value of the money unit. On this the security and steady value of property essentially depend.”

The second method — letting the same sum of money represent the same quantity of metal, but allowing a mint price below coin value — is “greatly to be preferred.” But Hamilton exposes the complexity of the issue at length, analyzing the French experience and the English practice of mint delays. He ultimately argues that if the government charges significant seigniorage, bullion owners will send their metal abroad to be coined cheaper. Drawing on English precedent of free coinage and the experience of France (where “the government has found it necessary, on some occasions, to employ very violent methods to compel the bringing of bullion to the mint”), Hamilton argues for government bearing the cost. He recommends an experiment of one-half percent difference on each of the metals — “the least exceptionable expedient for restraining the melting down, or exportation of the former” — with immediate payment at the mint.

He also considers and rejects the alternative of increasing alloy content as a means of preventing exportation, citing objections of augmented expense, actual and perceived depreciation of the coin, and greater danger of counterfeiting: “the perfection of the coins is a great safe-guard against counterfeits.”

V. The Number, Denominations, Sizes, and Devices of the Coins

Hamilton proposes a decimal system following the resolution of August 8, 1786, which he says “has been approved abroad, as well as at home,” and “nothing can be more simple or convenient, than the decimal subdivisions.” The unit in the money of account continues as a dollar; its multiples are dimes, cents, and milles (tenths, hundredths, and thousandths).

The proposed coins are:

  • Gold coins: An “eagle” equal in weight and value to ten units or dollars (the largest gold piece). A second gold piece equal to a tenth part of the former, which shall also be a unit or dollar — this smaller piece is intended not to be numerous but to “have a sensible object in that metal, as well as in silver, to express the unit.” Fifty thousand at a time in circulation may suffice for this purpose.
  • Silver coins: The dollar (a unit). The disme or dime (a tenth part of the dollar) — “less than the least of the silver coins now in general currency in England.”
  • Copper coins: The cent (a hundredth part of a dollar). The half-cent (half the value of the former).

Hamilton discusses appropriate sizes and weights. He recommends the cent weigh 11 pennyweight, which “will about correspond with the value of the copper and the expense of coinage.” He notes that pieces of very small value are “a great accommodation, and the means of a beneficial economy to the poor; by enabling them to purchase in small portions, and at a more reasonable rate, the necessaries of which they stand in need.” The half-cent enables the poor to procure necessaries cheap, allowing them “with more comfort to themselves, to labour for less.”

On coin size, Hamilton is attentive to wear: “the more superficies or surface, the more the piece will be liable to be injured by friction; or, in other words, the faster it will wear.” Hence the form of the double guinea or double louis d’or is preferable to that of the half johannes for the large gold piece. To fortify the idea of the dollar’s identity, the new silver dollar’s form and size should agree with the present Spanish dollar as far as the quantity of matter permits.

For devices, Hamilton recommends simple emblematic designs: “The devices of the coins are far from being matters of indifference, as they may be made the vehicles of useful impressions. They ought, therefore, to be emblematical, but without losing sight of simplicity. The fewer sharp points and angles there are, the less will be the loss by wearing.” The eagle is acknowledged as “not a very expressive or apt appellation for the largest gold piece, but nothing better occurs.”

VI. Whether Foreign Coins Shall Be Permitted to Remain Current

A gradual phase-out, carefully designed to prevent a sudden stagnation of circulation:

  • Year 1 (after mint commences): Foreign coins continue at their present rates. The moneys paid into the treasury during this year, being recoined before re-issue, will afford a partial substitute before any interruption to pre-existing circulation.
  • Year 2: Privilege continued only to gold coins of Portugal, England, and France, plus Spanish silver coins. Treasury revenues and newly minted coins will materially extend the substitute.
  • Year 3: The remaining foreign coins at mint rates only. The extension of national coinage will be “so far increased during the third year, by the facility of procuring the remaining species to be recoined, which will arise from the diminution of their current values, as probably to enable the dispensing wholly with the circulation of the foreign coins, after that period.”
  • After: All foreign coin ceases to be legal tender. The progress of bank bill currency during the same time will also afford a substitute.

Hamilton recommends the President be given discretionary authority over the Spanish dollar — “It is possible that an exception in favour of this particular species of coin may be found expedient: and it may tend to obviate inconveniences, if there be a power to make the exception in a capacity to be exerted when the period shall arrive.”

Regarding the proposal of Secretary of State Jefferson to align the dollar’s weight with the unit of weight, Hamilton notes that the five-grain addition this would require is “so considerable a change in the money unit” that it would “abandon the advantage of preserving the identity of the dollar.” He suggests the end might be obtained by increasing the proportion of alloy in silver coins, but “this would destroy the uniformity, in that respect, between the gold and silver coins.”

VII. Organization of the Mint

Hamilton proposes a lean administrative structure:

  • Director of the Mint — general superintendence of the business
  • Assay-Master or Assayer — to receive metals brought to the mint, ascertain their fineness, and deliver them to be coined
  • Master Coiner — to conduct the making of the coins
  • Cashier — to receive and pay out coins
  • Auditor — to keep and adjust the accounts of the mint
  • Clerks — as many as the Director deems necessary, to assist the different officers
  • Workmen — as many as found requisite; the number is left indefinite because at certain times more are needed than at others, though they “will never be numerous”
  • Porter

Hamilton notes that European mints have various other officers, but those listed “appear to be indispensable.” Persons in the capacity of clerks will suffice instead of additional specialized officers, “with the advantage of greater economy.” The estimated ordinary annual expense of the establishment is 15,000–20,000.

VIII. Remedy for Errors in Weight and Alloy

Hamilton describes the English “pix” practice in detail:

“A certain number of pieces are taken promiscuously out of every fifteen pounds of gold coined at the mint, which are deposited for safe keeping in a strong box called the pix. This box, from time to time, is opened in the presence of the lord chancellor, the officers of the treasury, and others; and portions are selected from the pieces of each coinage, which are melted together, and the mass assayed by a jury of the company of goldsmiths. If the imperfection and deficiency, both in fineness and weight, fall short of a sixth of a carat or 40 grains of pure gold upon a pound of standard, the master of the mint is held excusable; because it is supposed that no workman can reasonably be answerable for greater exactness.”

Hamilton recommends similar regulation for the United States, noting that “the expediency of some similar regulation seems to be manifest.”
