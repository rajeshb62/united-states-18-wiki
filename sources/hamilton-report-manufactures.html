This is Hamilton’s most comprehensive state paper, often considered the founding document of American economic nationalism. It is a single continuous treatise without chapter divisions, but contains the following major arguments and subjects:

I. The Question Stated: Should Manufactures Be Encouraged?

Hamilton opens by noting that the House resolution of January 15, 1790, directs him to report on means of promoting manufactures — particularly those that would render the United States independent of foreign nations for military and other essential supplies. He observes that the expediency of encouraging manufactures, once deemed very questionable, is now “pretty generally admitted.” The embarrassments obstructing external trade have led to reflections on enlarging domestic commerce; foreign restrictions on agricultural exports create a desire for more extensive domestic demand; and the success of existing manufacturing enterprises justifies hope that obstacles are less formidable than previously apprehended.

Nevertheless, Hamilton acknowledges “respectable patrons of opinions unfriendly to the encouragement of manufactures” and summarizes their full case:

  • Agriculture is the most beneficial object of human industry, especially for the United States with its immense tracts of fertile, uninhabited territory. Nothing can afford so advantageous an employment of capital and labor as converting wilderness into cultivated farms.
  • Industry left to itself infallibly finds the most profitable employment. To accelerate manufactures by government patronage is to transfer industry from a more to a less beneficial channel by “force and art.”
  • Scarcity of hands — the small population relative to territory, the allurements of emigration to unsettled lands, and the ease with which artisans become independent farmers produce “a scarcity of hands for manufacturing occupation and dearness of labor generally.”
  • Deficiency of pecuniary capital makes successful competition with European manufactures appear “little less than desperate.” Extensive manufactures, the argument runs, can only be the offspring of a full or redundant population.
  • Monopoly and enhanced prices — if government does force manufactures prematurely through duties, prohibitions, or bounties, it only sacrifices the community to particular classes, creating virtual monopolies with inflated prices defrayed at public expense.

Hamilton concedes this mode of reasoning “has certainly respectable pretensions” and that if it had governed nations more generally they might have attained greater prosperity. But he immediately cautions that “most general theories admit of numerous exceptions, and there are few, if any, of the political kind which do not blend a considerable portion of error with the truths they inculcate.”

II. The Superior Productiveness of Agriculture — Examined and Refuted

Hamilton devotes his most sophisticated economic reasoning to dismantling the physiocratic claim that agriculture is not only the most productive but the only productive species of industry. He notes that this thesis “has not been verified by any accurate detail of facts and calculations” and that the general arguments for it “are rather subtle and paradoxical than solid or convincing.”

The physiocratic argument stated. The claim runs as follows: labor on land produces enough not only to replace expenses and maintain workers, but to yield a net surplus — rent — for the landlord. Manufacturing labor, by contrast, does nothing more than replace the stock employed (materials, tools, wages) and yield the ordinary profit on that stock. It produces nothing equivalent to rent; the additional value it gives to raw materials is counterbalanced by the value of agricultural produce consumed by manufacturers. Manufacturing, therefore, can augment society’s revenue only through parsimony, not through positive productiveness.

Hamilton’s three-part rebuttal. First, he argues that manufacturing labor does reproduce a value equal to what it consumes and continues the original capital in existence — this alone should exempt it from being considered “wholly unproductive.” Even if the consumption of agricultural produce by manufacturers exactly equals the value added to materials, the total amount of consumption and production in a given period would be double the agricultural produce consumed. There would “at every moment, in consequence of their labor, a greater value of goods in the market.”

Second, the claim that artificers can augment revenue only by parsimony is “true in no other sense than in one which is equally applicable to husbandmen or cultivators.” All classes can increase wealth only through savings; manufacturing enjoys no special disability here.

Third, the annual produce of a country can only be increased in two ways — by improvements in the productive powers of existing labor, or by increases in the quantity of labor. Manufacturing labor, being more susceptible to subdivision and simplification of operations, is capable of greater improvement in productive powers than agricultural labor.

The nature-cooperates-with-man fallacy. One physiocratic argument holds that in agriculture nature cooperates with man, so their joint labor must be greater than man’s alone. Hamilton calls this “both quaint and superficial.” It is quite conceivable that skilled human labor alone, applied to complex manufacturing, may be more productive in value than the combined labor of nature and man directed at simpler agricultural operations. Moreover, the mechanical powers — fire and water — are made “auxiliary to the prosecution of manufactures” to a far greater extent than in agriculture. Hamilton also notes that agricultural labor is “periodical and occasional, depending on seasons, and liable to various and long intermissions,” while manufacturing labor is “constant and regular, extending through the year, embracing in some instances night as well as day.”

The rent fallacy exposed. The central physiocratic argument — that manufacturing yields nothing equivalent to the rent of land — Hamilton dismisses as “rather verbal than substantial.” What appears as rent in agriculture is simply the ordinary profit of a separate capital: the land itself, advanced by its owner to a tenant. The farmer’s profit and the landlord’s rent are nothing more than “the ordinary profits of two capitals belonging to two different persons and united in the cultivation of a farm.” In manufacturing, the same division occurs: one person may lend capital (receiving interest, which corresponds to rent) while another carries on the enterprise (receiving profit, corresponding to the farmer’s return). Both together constitute the ordinary profit on the total capital employed. Hamilton concludes that “the rent, therefore, accruing to the proprietor of the land, far from being a criterion of exclusive productiveness, is no criterion even of superior productiveness.”

The hypothetical examples. Hamilton constructs a series of economic models demonstrating that manufacturing augments total societal revenue. If there is only a farmer, he must divide his labor between cultivation and making clothing and tools — producing less of both. If there is a farmer and an artificer, the farmer specializes in cultivation, producing provisions and raw materials; the artificer produces manufactured goods. The result: two quantities of value exist instead of one, and total revenue and consumption are double. If there are two farmers and no artificer, each dividing his time, the total product equals only what one farmer and one artificer would produce. The artificer’s labor, therefore, is “as positively productive as that of the farmer, and as positively augments the revenue of the society.”

Hamilton decomposes the artificer’s production into three parts: (1) the portion purchasing subsistence and materials from the farmer; (2) the portion supplying his own manufactured necessaries; and (3) the profit on stock employed — which “is, in every sense, as effective an addition to the income of the society as the rent of land.”

III. The Seven Circumstances by Which Manufactures Augment National Wealth

Rather than claim manufacturing is more productive than agriculture, Hamilton argues that the establishment of manufactures renders “the total mass of useful and productive labor, in a community, greater than it would otherwise be.” He enumerates seven circumstances:

1. Division of Labor. Hamilton enumerates three advantages: greater skill and dexterity from constant application to a single object; economy of time by avoiding transitions between operations; and extension of the use of machinery, since “a man occupied on a single object will have it more in his power and will be more naturally led to exert his imagination in devising methods to facilitate and abridge labor.” The mere separation of the cultivator from the artificer has the effect of augmenting the productive powers of labor.

2. Extension of Machinery. Machinery is “an artificial force brought in aid of the natural force of man; and, to all purposes of labor, is an increase of hands, an accession of strength, unencumbered, too, by the expense of maintaining the laborer.” Manufacturing pursuits are susceptible to the application of machinery in far greater degree than agriculture. When a nation procures its fabrics from abroad instead of making them at home, it “transfers to foreign nations the advantages accruing from the employment of machinery.” Hamilton cites the cotton mill invented in England within the last 20 years — where all spinning processes are performed by machines put in motion by water and attended chiefly by women and children — as a signal illustration.

3. Employment of Classes Otherwise Non-Productive. Manufacturing establishments provide “occasional and extra employment to industrious individuals and families” during the intermissions of ordinary pursuits. The husbandman experiences a new source of profit from the industry of his wife and daughters, “invited and stimulated by the demands of the neighboring manufactories.” Women and children are rendered more useful, and children more early useful. Of persons employed in the cotton manufactories of Great Britain, nearly four-sevenths are women and children, many of tender age.

4. Promotion of Immigration. Artisans will emigrate from Europe for better prices, cheaper provisions, exemption from taxes, greater personal independence, and “a perfect equality of religious privileges.” But they will not readily become farmers. Manufacturing draws skilled European workmen to America: “we shall, in a great measure, trade upon a foreign stock, reserving our own for the cultivation of our lands and the manning of our ships.”

5. Diversity of Talents and Dispositions. Minds of the strongest powers “fall below mediocrity and labor without effect if confined to uncongenial pursuits.” When all kinds of industry obtain in a community, each individual can find his proper element and “call into activity the whole vigor of his nature.” Hamilton adds that if there is truth to the observation of “a peculiar aptitude for mechanic improvements” in the American genius, it is a forcible reason for propagating manufactures.

6. A More Ample Field for Enterprise. The spirit of enterprise “must necessarily be contracted or expanded in proportion to the simplicity or variety of the occupations” in a society. It is less in a nation of mere cultivators than in one of cultivators and merchants, and less in a nation of cultivators and merchants than in one of cultivators, artificers, and merchants.

7. A Steady Domestic Market for Agricultural Surplus. This is “among the most important of the circumstances” and has “an immediate and direct relation to the prosperity of agriculture.” A domestic market is greatly preferable to a foreign one because it is far more reliable. Foreign demand is precarious — subject to artificial policy restrictions (European nations striving to supply themselves), seasonal fluctuations, and glut from coincident harvests. “Manufacturers, who constitute the most numerous class after the cultivators of the land, are for that reason the principal consumers of the surplus of their labor.” Hamilton emphasizes that the multiplication of manufactures “not only furnishes a market for those articles which have been accustomed to be produced in abundance in a country; but it likewise creates a demand for such as were either unknown, or produced in inconsiderable quantities. The bowels, as well as the surface of the earth, are ransacked for articles which were before neglected.”

IV. Response to Practical Objections

Scarcity of hands. Hamilton qualifies this objection significantly. Large districts are “pretty fully peopled” with flourishing towns. Moreover, the employment of women and children, the use of machinery (substituting “the agency of fire and water”), the engagement of persons ordinarily in other occupations during their leisure hours, and the attraction of foreign emigrants all “materially diminish everywhere the effect of a scarcity of hands.”

Dearness of labor. Hamilton provides a precise numerical example: suppose the labor cost difference between two countries for fabricating a given article is 10, and a machine performs half the necessary labor. The labor cost difference is reduced from 10 to 5. The “extra charges” on foreign manufactured goods — shipping, insurance, commissions, duties, fees — cannot be estimated at less than 15 to 30 per cent on the cost at the manufactory. This sum “may confidently be regarded as more than a counterpoise for the real difference in the price of labor.”

Want of capital. Hamilton calls this “the most indefinite of the objections.” He identifies three compensating resources: (1) banks, which “have a powerful tendency to extend the active capital of a country”; (2) foreign capital, which has already been experienced in external commerce and internal improvements; and (3) most originally and extensively, the funded debt.

Hamilton’s funded debt argument is a landmark of fiscal theory. Public funds answer the purpose of capital because of the ease and dispatch with which they can be turned into money. Against the objection that they destroy other capital to an equal amount, Hamilton constructs a numerical demonstration: if the annual revenue applied to service a 6% stock is in the ratio of 8 per 100 (6 interest, 2 principal), then the capital destroyed bears a proportion of only 8 to 100 compared to the capital created of 100. Hamilton further argues that the payment of interest involves “at most, a temporary transfer of some other capital… which he again restores to the circulation to resume the offices of a capital.” However, he carefully distinguishes between an absolute increase of real wealth and “an artificial increase of capital as an engine of business,” conceding that a funded debt has no pretensions in the first sense — but in the latter, its pretensions “are not easy to be controverted.” He warns against the conclusion that more debt is always better, noting “there may be a plethora in the political as in the natural body.”

V. Specific American Manufacturing Industries Already Established

Hamilton provides the most comprehensive inventory of American manufacturing ever compiled to that date, organized by material and trade:

1. Skins. Tanned and tawed leather, dressed skins, shoes, boots, slippers, harness and saddlery, portmanteaux and trunks, leather breeches, gloves, muffs and tippets, parchment, and glue.

2. Iron. Bar and sheet iron, steel, nail rods and nails, implements of husbandry, stoves, pots and other household utensils, steel and iron work for carriages and shipbuilding, anchors, scale beams and weights, tools of artificers, and arms of different kinds (though the last had diminished for want of demand).

3. Wood. Ships (built “nowhere in greater perfection”), cabinet wares and turnery, wool and cotton cards, machinery for manufactures and husbandry, mathematical instruments, coopers’ wares.

4. Flax and Hemp. Cables, sailcloth, cordage, twine, and pack thread.

5. Bricks and coarse tiles, and potters’ wares.

6. Ardent spirits and malt liquors.

7. Paper. Writing, printing, sheathing and wrapping paper, pasteboards, fullers or press papers, paper hangings.

8. Hats of fur, wool, and mixtures; women’s stuff and silk shoes.

9. Refined sugars.

10. Oils of animals and seeds, soap, spermaceti and tallow candles.

11. Copper and brass wires, particularly utensils for distillers, sugar refiners, and brewers; andirons and other household articles; philosophical apparatus.

12. Tin wares for most purposes of ordinary use.

13. Carriages of all kinds.

14. Snuff, chewing and smoking tobacco.

15. Starch and hair powder.

16. Lampblack and other painters’ colors.

17. Gunpowder.

Household manufacturing. Beyond regular trades, Hamilton describes a “vast scene of household manufacturing” which contributes more largely to supply than could be imagined. Great quantities of coarse cloths, coatings, serges, flannels, linsey-woolseys, hosiery, fustians, jeans, muslins, checked and striped cotton and linen goods, bedticks, coverlets, tow linens, and table linens are made in families — often for sale and even for exportation. Hamilton reports that in many districts two-thirds, three-fourths, and even four-fifths of all clothing is made by the inhabitants themselves.

VI. The Superiority of Manufacturing-and-Agricultural Nations in Trade

Hamilton argues there is “a moral certainty that the trade of a country which is both manufacturing and agricultural will be more lucrative and prosperous than that of a country which is merely agricultural.” He provides three reasons:

First, demand for agricultural products is irregular (affected by weather, seasons, and foreign harvests), while demand for manufactured goods is constant and regular. This uniformity on one side and unsteadiness on the other necessarily causes the exchange to turn to the disadvantage of purely agricultural states.

Second, a more diversified market attracts more foreign customers. “The greatest resort will ever be to those marts where commodities, while equally abundant, are most various.” The field of enterprise is enlarged for merchants in proportion to the variety as well as the abundance of commodities they find at home for export.

Third, diversification insulates against trade stagnations. A nation with few articles is “more quickly and sensibly affected” by stagnations than one with great variety. The latter “commonly finds itself indemnified by the high prices of some articles for the low prices of others.”

From these circumstances, Hamilton draws two inferences: (1) there is always a higher probability of a favorable balance of trade in manufacturing-agricultural countries; (2) such countries are likely to possess more pecuniary wealth. He cites the West India Islands, whose fertile soils “exchange to a loss with almost every other country,” as corroboration. Pre-Revolutionary America’s debt to Great Britain was progressive; post-Revolution, states with more manufactures “recovered fastest from the injuries of the late war and abound most in pecuniary resources.”

VII. Independence, Security, and the Burden of Distance

Hamilton elevates the argument to national security: “Every nation, with a view to those great objects, ought to endeavor to possess within itself all the essentials of national supply. These comprise the means of subsistence, habitation, clothing, and defense.” The extreme embarrassments of the Revolutionary War from an incapacity to supply themselves are “still matter of keen recollection.” The want of a navy renders external commerce a peculiarly precarious reliance.

Distance from Europe adds extra charges of 15 to 30 per cent on imports — a deadweight loss deducted from the value of American exports. These charges “principally fall upon us” and “are an additional cause of extraordinary deduction from the primitive value of our own products.” In time of war, these burdens would become catastrophic. Hamilton also notes that the fisheries of the United States suffer from the want of an adequate market, and that manufacturing would create demand for fish oils, bones, and skins.

VIII. The Proposed Means of Encouraging Manufactures

Hamilton enumerates eleven principal means, drawing on the practices of European nations:

1. Protective duties on rival foreign articles — amounting to a virtual bounty on domestic fabrics by enhancing charges on competitors. Hamilton notes this is “sanctioned by the laws of the United States in a variety of instances” and has “the additional recommendation of being a resource of revenue.”

2. Prohibitions or equivalent duties — fit only when a manufacture has progressed enough to insure due competition and adequate supply. Hamilton argues that considering “a monopoly of the domestic market to its own manufacturers as the reigning policy of manufacturing nations,” a similar policy is dictated “by the principles of distributive justice.”

3. Prohibitions of exportation of materials — to be adopted “with great circumspection, and only in very plain cases.”

4. Pecuniary bounties — “one of the most efficacious means” and “in some views the best.” Bounties have four advantages: they are more positive and direct than other modes; they avoid the temporary augmentation of price incident to duties; they do not tend to produce scarcity; and they are the only proper expedient for uniting the encouragement of agriculture with that of manufactures. Hamilton acknowledges “a degree of prejudice against bounties” but argues that “there is no purpose to which public money can be more beneficially applied than to the acquisition of a new and useful branch of industry.”

5. Premiums — distinguished from bounties in that they reward “some particular excellence or superiority” rather than the whole quantity produced. “Contrived so as to be both honorary and lucrative, they address themselves to different passions — touching the chords as well of emulation as of interest.”

6. Exemption of raw materials from duty — “It can hardly ever be advisable to add the obstructions of fiscal burdens to the difficulties which naturally embarrass a new manufacture.”

7. Drawbacks — refunds of duties on materials re-exported as finished goods, citing the examples of salt and molasses.

8. Encouragement of inventions and introduction of foreign improvements — “among the most useful and unexceptionable of the aids which can be given to manufacturers.” Hamilton recommends extending benefits to introducers as well as inventors.

9. Inspection systems — to prevent frauds, improve quality, and preserve the character of national manufactures. He notes the reputation of flour, lumber, and potash established by inspection.

10. Facilitating pecuniary remittances — a general circulation of bank paper and improvements in inland bills of exchange would promote commerce between states.

11. Internal improvements — roads and canals, which “put the remote parts of a country more nearly upon a level with those in the neighborhood of the town.” Hamilton cites the example of Great Britain’s improvements, noting that good roads and canals “encourage the cultivation of the remote, which must always be the most extensive circle of the country.”

IX. Constitutional Authority

Hamilton addresses potential constitutional objections to the manufacturing program. The National Legislature has express authority “to lay and collect taxes, duties, imposts, and excises, to pay the debts, and provide for the common defense and general welfare” — with only three qualifications (uniformity, apportionment of direct taxes, and no export duties). The phrase “general welfare,” he argues, “was doubtless intended to signify more than was expressed or imported in those which preceded.” It is “as comprehensive as any that could have been used.” The only admissible qualification is that the object must be “general and not local; its operation extending, in fact or by possibility, throughout the Union.”

On bounties specifically, Hamilton dismisses constitutional doubts as having “certainly no good foundation.” The power to raise money is “plenary and indefinite,” and “whatever concerns the general interests of learning, of agriculture, of manufactures, and of commerce is within the sphere of the national councils, as far as regards an application of money.”

X. Conclusion

Hamilton submits his report with the conviction that the encouragement of manufactures is essential to national independence and prosperity. He proposes establishing a Board for Promoting Arts, Agriculture, Manufactures, and Commerce — a commission of at least three members, funded by the surplus of additional duties, empowered to defray emigration expenses of skilled workmen, reward useful discoveries and inventions, and dispense premiums. He warns that “the progress of particular manufactures has been much retarded by the want of skillful workmen” and that “the capitals employed are not equal to the purposes of bringing from abroad workmen of a superior kind.” The board would administer rewards under “proper discretionary direction,” since it is “impracticable to apportion by general rules specific compensations for discoveries of unknown and disproportionate utility.”

The report closes with a call for Congress to act: “In what can it be so useful as in prompting and improving the efforts of industry? All which is humbly submitted.”
